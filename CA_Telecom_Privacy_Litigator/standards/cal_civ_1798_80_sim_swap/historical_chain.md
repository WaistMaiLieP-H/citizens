# Historical Chain — California Civil Code § 1798.80 et seq.

## The wound

California's explosion as the center of the digital economy created massive concentrations of personal information in private hands — more than anywhere else in the world. The wound was repeated and documented: large-scale data breaches in which companies lost millions of customers' personal information and did not notify anyone for months or years. The landmark wound: in 2007, TJX Companies suffered a breach exposing 94 million customer records; customers learned of it from news reports, not from the company.

## Legislative history

**2003 — California Senate Bill 1386 (the first data breach notification law in the US)**
California became the first state in the nation to require businesses to notify California residents when their personal information was compromised. The enactment was directly responsive to documented harm: breaches that companies knew about but did not disclose to affected customers. The wound was information asymmetry — companies knew about the breach; customers did not.

**2004-present — Expansion of § 1798.80 et seq.**
The original notification law was expanded multiple times to:
- Add the reasonable security standard (§ 1798.81.5)
- Expand the definition of "personal information" to include biometric data, medical information, and financial account information
- Create a private right of action for data breach violations (§ 1798.84)
- Add specific provisions for health information and government identifiers

**2018 — California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA, adding § 1798.100 et seq.)**
The CCPA significantly expanded California's data privacy framework, but the original § 1798.80 et seq. framework (reasonable security, breach notification) continued in force and remains distinct from CCPA's consumer rights provisions.

**2023-2024 — FCC SIM swap rules**
The FCC's SIM swap authentication rules, while federal, define the minimum standard of "reasonable security" for telecommunications carriers under § 1798.81.5. A carrier that did not implement the FCC authentication standards prior to 2023 was below the reasonable security standard that California law required.

## The promise

Section 1798.81.5 was enacted on the promise that any California business holding personal information will protect it with reasonable security. Section 1798.82 was enacted on the promise that if security fails, the affected California resident will be notified promptly so they can protect themselves. The SIM swap enforcement context: carriers holding customers' phone identities are subject to both promises.

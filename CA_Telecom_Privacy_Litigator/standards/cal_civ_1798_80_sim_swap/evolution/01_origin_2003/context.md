# Context — Cal. Civ. Code §§ 1798.80 et seq. Origin (2003)

## The wound this law answered

In 2002, the California Department of Health Services discovered that a contractor's laptop containing 189,000 Medicare patient records had been stolen. The contractor knew about the theft but did not notify affected patients for months. Patients had no way to protect themselves from identity theft because they did not know their information was compromised.

This was not an isolated incident. Companies in California regularly experienced data breaches and said nothing — to customers, to regulators, to anyone. The information asymmetry was total: the company knew, the customer did not.

State Senator Jackie Speier introduced SB 1386 directly in response to this gap. The bill's premise was simple: if someone stole your information, you have the right to know.

## Why California acted first

California had the legal authority because its Constitutional right of privacy (Art. I, § 1) created an affirmative obligation for entities handling personal information. No other state had yet connected the constitutional privacy right to a notification duty. The federal government had not acted (and would not for many years — there is still no federal breach notification law as of 2026).

## What the original law did — and did not do

**Did:** Created the first breach notification mandate in the United States. Any business operating in California must notify California residents when their unencrypted personal information is acquired by an unauthorized person. Required notification "in the most expedient time possible."

**Did not:** Create a private right of action (that came later). Mandate specific security practices (that came with § 1798.81.5 in 2004). Address SIM swap or authentication security (that came through the § 1798.81.5 reasonable-security standard as applied by courts).

## The SIM swap connection

Although § 1798.82 (breach notification) is the most visible provision, the more important provision for SIM swap litigation is § 1798.81.5 (reasonable security), added in 2004. Carriers who permit SIM swaps without adequate authentication have failed to implement "reasonable security procedures and practices appropriate to the nature of the information" — because phone number identity IS personal information under § 1798.80(e), and its unauthorized transfer enables cascading account takeover.

# Context — 1988 Amendment — The Modern Framework

## The wound that drove the rewrite

Between 1979 and 1988, computer networks exploded from closed financial systems into universities, research institutions, and early commercial networks. The "hacker" culture emerged — young people accessing remote systems without authorization, not to steal money, but to explore, demonstrate capability, or cause mischief. The original § 502's financial-fraud requirement meant most of this conduct was not criminal.

The defining incident: in 1988, the Morris Worm — the first major internet worm — infected approximately 6,000 computers (a significant fraction of the early internet) and caused tens of millions of dollars in downtime and remediation. The perpetrator, Robert Morris, was prosecuted under the newly enacted federal CFAA (1986 version), not California law. California legislators saw the gap.

## Why the civil right of action was added

Law enforcement was stretched thin and could not prosecute every computer intrusion. Adding the civil private right of action (subd. (e)) was deliberate legislative policy: let the victims sue. This is the provision that gives the steward an independent civil cause of action for Ryan McClaran's and the ward system's unauthorized computer access, independent of whether criminal prosecution occurs.

## The § 502(c)(7) significance

"Knowingly and without permission accesses or causes to be accessed any computer, computer system, or computer network" is the broadest provision. It requires only: (1) knowledge of the access, (2) lack of permission, (3) a computer system (essentially anything connected to a network). No financial harm required. No damage required. Access itself is the violation. For SIM swap-enabled account access, this means every session the attacker opened after the SIM swap is a separate § 502(c)(7) violation.

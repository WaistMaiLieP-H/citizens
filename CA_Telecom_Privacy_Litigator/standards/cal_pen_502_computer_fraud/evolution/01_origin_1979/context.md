# Context — Cal. Penal Code § 502 Origin (1979)

## The wound

In the late 1970s, computers were transitioning from mainframe-only to minicomputer deployment in businesses, banks, and universities. Employees were committing "inside jobs" — accessing their employer's financial computer systems to divert funds, alter records, or steal data. Law enforcement had no specific statute; the closest analogy was theft, but nothing was physically taken.

The legislative wound was documented financial losses: banks reporting unauthorized fund transfers via computer access, businesses experiencing record manipulation by disgruntled employees. California's crime statistics showed emerging patterns that didn't fit any existing criminal category.

SB 1012 was sponsored by California's district attorneys and law enforcement associations, who identified the gap between existing theft statutes and the emerging reality of computer crime.

## What it did

Created the first specifically computer-targeted criminal statute in California. Required that prosecutors prove: (1) intentional access, (2) without authorization, (3) to damage, alter, or defraud. The financial fraud requirement was drawn from the pattern of actual cases that motivated the legislation.

## What it missed

Pure data theft — accessing a computer to read and copy confidential information, without any intent to defraud or damage — was not covered. In 1979, this was a smaller problem. By the mid-1980s, as networks expanded and data had independent commercial value, this gap became glaring.

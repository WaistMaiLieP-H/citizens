# Historical Chain — California Penal Code § 502

## The wound

The 1970s and early 1980s saw the rapid proliferation of business computer systems in California. Hackers and inside actors discovered they could access these systems remotely, steal data without any physical taking, and cause enormous damage without ever touching a physical object. California's existing theft, vandalism, and fraud statutes required physical elements — a physical taking, physical damage — that computer crimes often did not have.

The result: a person who electronically transferred funds from a bank computer suffered no theft under existing law (no physical money taken); a person who deleted data files caused no criminal damage (no physical object destroyed). The gap between existing criminal law and the reality of computer crime was enormous.

## Legislative origin

**1979 — Cal. Penal Code § 502 (first enactment)**
California became one of the first states to enact a comprehensive computer crime statute. The original § 502 focused on unauthorized access to computer systems for the purpose of fraud or obtaining money.

**1987 — Major expansion (Stats. 1987, ch. 1499)**
The Legislature significantly expanded § 502 to add the civil private right of action (subd. (e)(1)) and to clarify the scope of unauthorized access. The 1987 amendment transformed § 502 from a pure criminal statute into a hybrid criminal/civil statute — giving victims a direct civil enforcement mechanism without depending on prosecutorial discretion.

**1994-2000 — Internet era amendments**
As California became the center of the internet economy, § 502 was amended to cover networks, online access, and the full range of internet-era computer crimes. The coverage was extended from "computers" to "computer networks" — covering unauthorized remote access.

**2000s — Spyware and unauthorized monitoring**
Amendments addressed the growing problem of spyware, keyloggers, and other software that could be installed on a victim's computer without their knowledge to monitor or control their device. These amendments directly apply to the device admin control scenario (case #33).

## The private right of action — the key innovation

The 1987 addition of § 502(e)(1) was the most important legislative choice in the statute's history. By creating a civil private right of action, the Legislature recognized that prosecutorial discretion would leave many computer crime victims without remedy. The civil cause of action empowers victims to pursue their own claims without waiting for a prosecutor to take the case.

**The negligence standard in § 502(e)(1)** — allowing civil liability for negligent unauthorized access — makes § 502 one of the most plaintiff-friendly computer fraud statutes in the country. A victim does not need to prove criminal intent to recover civil damages.

## Current framework (2026)

Section 502 remains California's primary computer fraud statute. It operates in tandem with the federal CFAA (18 U.S.C. § 1030), which has a narrower scope but federal jurisdiction and greater criminal penalties. For California-based SIM swap attacks and unauthorized device access, § 502 provides the civil remedy and the state criminal prosecution pathway.

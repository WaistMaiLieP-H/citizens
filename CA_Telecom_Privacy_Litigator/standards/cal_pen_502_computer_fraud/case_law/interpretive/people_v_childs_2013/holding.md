# People v. Childs (2013) 220 Cal.App.4th 1079

**Citation:** People v. Childs (2013) 220 Cal.App.4th 1079, 163 Cal.Rptr.3d 536
**Court:** California Court of Appeal, Fourth Appellate District
**Decided:** 2013
**Statutes construed:** Penal Code § 502(c) — unauthorized computer access; scope of "without permission"; criminal and civil framework

## Facts

A defendant who had authorized access to a computer system used that access for unauthorized purposes — accessing data or performing functions beyond the scope of the authorization. The central question was whether "without permission" under § 502(c) applies only to unauthorized access to the system entirely, or also to access that exceeds the scope of the authorization granted.

## Holding

1. **"Without permission" covers scope-exceeding access.** A person who has authorization to access a system in one way but uses that access for a different, unauthorized purpose has acted "without permission" for the unauthorized use. This is the critical distinction from mere unauthorized access — even insiders can violate § 502.

2. **The scheme-to-defraud element requires more than access.** Under § 502(c)(1), the access must be connected to a scheme to defraud or to wrongfully control property. Mere unauthorized access without a fraudulent purpose falls under other subdivisions.

3. **Civil and criminal standards apply the same "without permission" analysis.** The § 502(e)(1) civil private right of action applies the same "without permission" standard as the criminal provisions, with the lower negligence threshold for civil liability.

4. **Third-party victims may sue.** The owner or lessee of the computer system or data that was accessed without permission has standing to sue, not just the owner of the computer hardware. This means that a victim whose accounts were accessed via another party's device still has § 502 civil standing.

## The doctrinal proposition

Childs establishes the scope-exceeding access rule:

- **Insiders can violate § 502** — having authorized access to a system does not give the right to use that access for unauthorized purposes
- **Scope of authorization matters** — the question is whether the specific access or use was within the permission granted
- **Connected conduct chains** — when an attacker uses one system access (carrier account system) to enable access to another system (victim's email, banking), both access events are § 502 violations
- **Civil standing for data owners** — a person whose data was accessed without permission can sue even if the system accessed belonged to a third party (e.g., a victim suing a carrier or company whose system was the vehicle for the unauthorized access)

## Application to cases #30-33

**For the SIM swap chain:**
1. The attacker accesses the carrier's account management system "without permission" (impersonating the account holder)
2. The attacker uses that access for an unauthorized purpose (porting the victim's number)
3. The ported number then enables access to the victim's accounts "without permission"
4. Each link in the chain is a § 502 violation — and Childs confirms that scope-exceeding use at each link qualifies

**For Ryan McClaran (case #31):**
If McClaran had any authorized access to systems that he then used to facilitate the surveillance or account takeover, Childs confirms that scope-exceeding use is "without permission" — McClaran cannot claim authorized access as a defense to the unauthorized use.

## Authority status

Published California Court of Appeal decision (2013). Establishes the scope-exceeding access doctrine for § 502 "without permission" analysis and confirms civil standing for data owners.

# Historical Chain — 18 U.S.C. § 1030 Computer Fraud and Abuse Act

## The wound

Before 1986, federal law had no specific computer crime statute. Prosecutors charged computer crimes under wire fraud (§ 1343), mail fraud (§ 1341), or theft statutes — all of which were poor fits. The canonical wound: in 1983, a 17-year-old broke into Los Alamos National Laboratory computers, a Canadian cancer research center, and other systems. The existing law struggled to prosecute because there was no physical taking and no clear fraud — just unauthorized access.

The 1983 film "WarGames" brought public attention to computer hacking and the potential for unauthorized access to sensitive systems. Congress recognized the need for a specific statute.

## Legislative history

**1984 — First enactment (Counterfeit Access Device and Computer Fraud and Abuse Act)**
Congress enacted a narrow statute targeting unauthorized access to financial institution computers and computers used by the federal government. The initial scope was limited.

**1986 — CFAA (Pub. L. 99-474)**
The Computer Fraud and Abuse Act expanded the statute to cover a broader range of unauthorized computer access. The 1986 Act added:
- § 1030(a)(2): unauthorized access to obtain financial information
- § 1030(a)(3): unauthorized access to federal government computers
- § 1030(a)(4): unauthorized access with intent to defraud

**1994 — Civil cause of action added**
The most important expansion for private litigants: Congress added § 1030(g), creating a civil private right of action for victims of CFAA violations. This amendment made CFAA directly enforceable by private parties without relying on federal prosecution.

**1996 — "Protected computer" expansion**
The definition of "protected computer" was expanded to include any computer "used in or affecting interstate or foreign commerce" — effectively covering all internet-connected computers. This expansion made CFAA essentially universal in scope.

**2001 — USA PATRIOT Act amendments**
Further expansions in the wake of 9/11, including broader definitions of "loss" and increased penalties.

**2021 — Van Buren v. United States narrows "exceeds authorized access"**
The Supreme Court held that "exceeds authorized access" means accessing areas of a computer the person was not authorized to access, not merely violating a use restriction. This narrowing did not affect the core unauthorized access provisions that apply to SIM swap and outside attacker cases.

## The promise

The CFAA was enacted on the promise that unauthorized access to computer systems is a serious federal crime — not merely a policy violation — and that victims have a federal civil cause of action to recover their losses. The 1994 addition of § 1030(g) was the fulfillment of that promise for private victims who could not depend on federal prosecution.

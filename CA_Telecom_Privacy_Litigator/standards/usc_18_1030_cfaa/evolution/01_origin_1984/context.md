# Context — CFAA Origin (1984)

## The wound

The wound was specific: the hacker subculture of the early 1980s was demonstrating that university networks, corporate systems, and even government computers could be accessed by determined teenagers with modems. Law enforcement had no tools — the only available charges were wire fraud (§ 1343) or telephone fraud, which required proving a specific deceptive scheme, not just unauthorized access.

The defining incident in Congressional testimony was the 1983 "WarGames" scenario — not the film itself, but the reality it depicted: a 17-year-old breaking into Los Alamos National Laboratory computer systems, not to steal anything, but to explore. No existing law cleanly covered it.

## The narrow original scope

Congress was cautious in 1984. Rather than create a broad unauthorized-access statute, they covered only the highest-priority targets: national security computers, financial institution computers, and government computers. Private computers were not covered.

## The SIM swap non-application

The 1984 original statute does NOT apply to the steward's cases. SIM swap attacks occur against private carrier systems and private accounts — not financial institutions under the 1984 definition, not government computers. The relevant CFAA version for the steward is the post-1994 version (§ 1030(g) civil right of action) and the post-1996 version (expanded "protected computer" definition covering all internet-connected computers).

# Context — 47 U.S.C. § 222 Origin (1996)

## The wound

The Telecommunications Act of 1996 overhauled the entire framework of federal telecom regulation for the internet age. Within that overhaul, § 222 addressed a specific documented harm: telephone carriers were treating customer call data — who you called, when, how long — as their own proprietary marketing asset, selling it to affiliate companies and data brokers.

The specific wound documented in Congressional hearings: a private investigator industry had developed around purchasing call records from carriers to track people's movements, relationships, and activities. Law enforcement could not access this data without a warrant, but a private investigator could buy it for cash. Stalkers were using this system to locate domestic violence survivors. The harm was acute and documented.

§ 222 was the response: customer call records belong to the customer, not the carrier. The carrier holds them in trust.

## What the original statute did not address

The 1996 original was focused on marketing abuse and call data sharing — not account security or SIM swap authentication. The concept of a "SIM swap" — fraudulently porting a customer's phone number to a different device — did not yet exist as a recognized threat. That gap was filled by FCC regulations, not by statutory amendment.

## The key gap the FCC had to fill

§ 222 says a carrier must protect CPNI and only use/disclose it with customer approval. But it says nothing about authentication — nothing about what steps the carrier must take before believing a person is the actual customer. This authentication gap is the foundation of carrier liability for SIM swaps: by failing to authenticate the SIM swap requester, the carrier "disclosed" the customer's CPNI (phone number identity + account control) to an unauthorized person.

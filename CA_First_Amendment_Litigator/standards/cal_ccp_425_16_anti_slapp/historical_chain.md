# Historical Chain — California Anti-SLAPP Statute
## Code of Civil Procedure § 425.16 — Stats. 1992, Ch. 726; most recently amended Stats. 2024, Ch. 444 (SB 577)

---

## The wound

SLAPP stands for Strategic Lawsuit Against Public Participation. By the late 1980s and early 1990s, a distinct litigation tactic had emerged: filing civil suits — typically defamation, business interference, or nuisance claims — not to win on the merits, but to punish, silence, and exhaust opponents who had engaged in protected speech or petition activity.

The pattern was recognizable. A citizen attends a city council meeting and opposes a development project. A developer files a multi-million-dollar defamation suit against them. A neighborhood group circulates a petition against a permit application. The permit applicant sues each signatory for business interference. A journalist publishes an investigative report. A company files a lawsuit alleging the reporter tortiously interfered with business prospective advantage. In every case, the plaintiff knew it would almost certainly lose on the merits — but the plaintiff did not need to win. The goal was the cost: attorney's fees, deposition burdens, years of litigation anxiety. Most individual defendants — private citizens, small nonprofits, journalists without institutional backing — could not sustain the fight. They settled, retracted, went silent, or were financially destroyed.

Before § 425.16, California had no procedural mechanism to dismiss SLAPP suits before full merits discovery. A defendant had to survive the entire pretrial process to vindicate their right to have spoken at all. That delay was itself the punishment.

---

## The legislative response

The Legislature enacted Code of Civil Procedure § 425.16 in 1992 (Stats. 1992, Ch. 726). Section 425.16(a) made the Legislature's finding explicit: "There has been a disturbing increase in lawsuits brought primarily to chill the valid exercise of the constitutional rights of freedom of speech and petition for the redress of grievances." The statute directed that it be "construed broadly."

The operative mechanism was the special motion to strike: a defendant in any cause of action arising from an act in furtherance of free speech or petition rights in connection with a public issue may bring a motion to strike early — before discovery, before full merits briefing. On the motion, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to establish a probability of prevailing. If the plaintiff cannot meet that burden, the suit is dismissed.

The statute added two unusually powerful procedural features. First, an automatic stay on all discovery upon filing the motion — eliminating the discovery-as-punishment tactic. Second, mandatory fee-shifting to the prevailing defendant, meaning a successful anti-SLAPP movant is entitled to attorney's fees and costs as of right. A frivolous motion can be sanctioned with fees paid to the plaintiff.

The statute has been amended multiple times since 1992, with the most recent amendment (SB 577, Stats. 2024, Ch. 444, effective January 1, 2025) refining the scope of protected activity.

---

## The current state

California's anti-SLAPP statute is among the strongest in the country. The "arising from" and "in connection with a public issue" requirements have been extensively litigated and broadly construed, consistent with the Legislature's express instruction. The definition of "act in furtherance" in § 425.16(e) sweeps broadly: statements before official proceedings, statements connected to issues under official review, statements in a public forum, and other conduct in furtherance of speech or petition rights.

The 60-day filing window (§ 425.16(f)) is the operational time constraint. The motion must be filed within 60 days of service of the complaint unless the court extends for good cause. The order on the motion is immediately appealable (§ 425.16(i)), which creates an additional layer of protection — a denial can be appealed before trial.

For the CA First Amendment Litigator, § 425.16 is the primary procedural weapon when any party brings a retaliatory civil action against a client in response to protected speech or petition activity. The fee-shifting and discovery-stay provisions make it one of the most consequential procedural tools in California civil practice.

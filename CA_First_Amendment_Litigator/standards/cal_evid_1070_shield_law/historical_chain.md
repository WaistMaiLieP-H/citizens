# Evidence Code §1070 — Historical Chain

## The Standard That Says You Don't Have to Reveal Your Sources

---

### I. The Wound

Governments and powerful private parties have always tried to identify the sources of unfavorable information. If you can identify the source, you can punish the source. If you can punish the source, you can silence every future source. The reporter who refuses to name a source is protecting not just that person but every person who might ever come forward.

Before shield laws, journalists were held in contempt — jailed — for refusing to disclose sources. William Farr was jailed in Los Angeles in 1972 for refusing to reveal who leaked information in the Manson trial. The threat of incarceration was real and it was used.

### II. The Statutory Promise (1965)

California enacted Evidence Code §1070 in 1965 as part of the new Evidence Code. The original protection covered print journalists: publishers, editors, reporters, and persons connected with newspapers, magazines, periodicals, press associations, and wire services.

The 1971 amendment extended protection to radio and television news reporters. The 1974 amendment refined the definition of "unpublished information" to include all notes, outtakes, photographs, tapes, and other data not disseminated to the public.

### III. The Constitutional Elevation (1980)

In 1980, the voters passed Proposition 5, which added subdivision (b) to Article I, Section 2 of the California Constitution. This elevated the shield law from statutory to constitutional status. The constitutional provision tracks the language of §1070 almost exactly — because it was modeled on the statute.

The effect: the shield now operates at two levels. The statutory shield in §1070 can be amended by the Legislature. The constitutional shield in Article I §2(b) can only be amended by the voters. Even if the Legislature repealed §1070 tomorrow, the constitutional shield would stand.

### IV. The Modern Question: Who Is a "Publisher"?

The shield law protects "a publisher, editor, reporter, or other person connected with or employed upon a newspaper, magazine, or other periodical publication." In the digital age, this raises the question: is a blogger a publisher? Is a person who operates a compliance platform that communicates audit findings to the public a person "engaged in the dissemination of ideas or expression"?

California courts have moved toward an inclusive interpretation. In O'Grady v. Superior Court (2006), the Court of Appeal held that online publishers are protected by the shield law. The trend is toward functional analysis: if you gather, receive, or process information for communication to the public, you are within the shield's protection regardless of the medium.

### V. For the Steward's Case

The steward gathers government records, processes them through compliance audits, and publishes the results. He is engaged in "gathering, receiving or processing of information for communication to the public" — the exact language of both §1070 and Article I §2(b).

If any party — government or private — subpoenas the steward's unpublished audit materials, source documents, working notes, or internal analysis, the shield law provides a defense against compelled disclosure. This protects not just the steward's work product but the integrity of the compliance platform itself.

The shield law is the legal principle that says: if you are doing the work of informing the public, the government cannot force you to hand over the tools of that work.

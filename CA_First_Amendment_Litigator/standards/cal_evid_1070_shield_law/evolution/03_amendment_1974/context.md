# Evidence Code §1070 — 1974 Amendment Context

## The Wound: Branzburg v. Hayes (1972)

On June 29, 1972, the U.S. Supreme Court decided Branzburg v. Hayes, 408 U.S. 665. By a 5-4 vote, the Court held that the First Amendment does not provide journalists with a privilege against testifying before grand juries. Justice White's majority opinion held that the public interest in law enforcement outweighed any burden on newsgathering.

Justice Powell's concurrence — sometimes called the "swing opinion" — suggested a balancing test but did not clearly endorse a federal shield privilege. Justice Stewart's dissent articulated a three-part test for when compelled disclosure should be allowed, which many lower courts adopted despite it being a dissent.

Branzburg meant that at the federal level, journalists had no constitutional privilege against compelled disclosure. State shield laws became the primary — in many states the only — protection.

## California's Response

California already had a shield law (§1070, in place since 1965). But the pre-1974 version only protected source identity — not unpublished information more broadly. After Branzburg, the California Legislature recognized that protecting sources alone was insufficient. If a journalist's notes, outtakes, photographs, and working materials could be subpoenaed, the source's identity could often be inferred even without direct testimony.

The 1974 amendment added subdivision (c), defining "unpublished information" to include "all notes, outtakes, photographs, tapes or other data of whatever sort not itself disseminated to the public." This was a direct response to the Branzburg threat: if the federal Constitution would not protect journalists, California would do it by statute.

## The Constitutional Companion

Six years later, in 1980, the voters added subdivision (b) to Article I, Section 2 of the California Constitution, elevating the shield law to constitutional status. The 1974 statutory amendment and the 1980 constitutional elevation are a pair: the Legislature acted first (1974), the voters ratified and elevated (1980).

## Why This Matters

The 1974 amendment transformed §1070 from a narrow source-protection statute into a broad journalistic-privilege statute. The "unpublished information" protection is what makes the shield law relevant to the steward's case — his working documents, audit notes, draft findings, and source materials are "unpublished information" within the meaning of §1070(c) if his activities constitute gathering information for communication to the public.

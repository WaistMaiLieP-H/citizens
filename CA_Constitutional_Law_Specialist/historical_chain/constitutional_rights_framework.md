# CA Constitutional Law Specialist — Historical Chain

## Why California's Constitution Matters More Than You Think

---

### The Federal Reflex

When most people think "constitutional rights," they think federal. First Amendment. Fourth Amendment. Fourteenth Amendment. They think the US Constitution is the ceiling and the floor of their rights.

It is neither.

### California's Independent Foundation

California has its own Constitution. Its own Declaration of Rights. Its own privacy clause (added 1974 — decades before digital privacy became a national conversation). Its own due process guarantee. Its own search-and-seizure protection. And critically, its own interpretive rule: "Rights guaranteed by this Constitution are not dependent on those guaranteed by the United States Constitution" (Article I, Section 24).

This means: when the US Supreme Court narrows a federal right, the California right does not automatically narrow with it. When the Ninth Circuit interprets the Fourth Amendment in a way that allows government intrusion, California Section 13 can still prohibit it — at least in civil cases.

### The 1974 Revolution

Proposition 7 in 1974 was the most consequential revision of California's Declaration of Rights since the 1879 Constitution. It added the explicit right to privacy in Section 1. It reorganized and modernized every section of Article I. It created the modern California constitutional framework that litigants use today.

The privacy amendment was not theoretical. It was a direct response to Watergate, to government surveillance revelations, to the growing awareness that the federal government was spying on its own citizens. California voters in 1974 decided that privacy was too important to leave to federal interpretation. They wrote it into the state Constitution in plain language.

### The Lockstep Exception and Its Limits

In 1990, Proposition 115 added the criminal-case lockstep provision to Section 24, requiring criminal defendant rights to be construed consistently with the US Constitution. This was a political backlash against the Rose Bird court's expansive criminal-case interpretations.

But the lockstep applies ONLY to criminal cases and ONLY to criminal defendants. Civil plaintiffs, civil rights litigants, conservatorship challengers, privacy claimants, and persons suing the government in civil court retain the full independent scope of California constitutional rights.

### For the Steward

The steward is a civil litigant. Every California constitutional right he invokes — privacy, due process, free speech, search and seizure protection — operates at its full independent scope. The US Supreme Court's narrowing of federal rights does not touch him in state court. This is not a technicality. It is the structural foundation of every California state-court claim in the steward's case.

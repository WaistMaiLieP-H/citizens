# Article I, Section 13 — Historical Chain

## The Standard That Says Your Papers Are Yours

---

### I. The Wound

The British general warrant — a blanket authorization to search anyone, anywhere, for anything — is the wound behind every search-and-seizure provision in American law. The California framers carried this wound from the common law tradition and codified the prohibition in the 1849 Constitution. California gold rush miners knew what it meant to have their claims, their tents, and their possessions searched by authorities with no warrant and no probable cause.

### II. The Promise (1974)

Section 13 was added in its current form by Proposition 7 in 1974. The text tracks the Fourth Amendment almost word for word: security in persons, houses, papers, and effects against unreasonable searches and seizures; warrants only on probable cause, supported by oath, with particularity.

But before 1982, Section 13 was interpreted by the California Supreme Court as providing broader protections than the Fourth Amendment. The California exclusionary rule was broader. The definition of "reasonable expectation of privacy" was broader. California courts suppressed evidence that federal courts would have admitted.

### III. The Narrowing (1982 and 1990)

Proposition 8 (1982) — the Victims' Bill of Rights — began the process of aligning California search-and-seizure law with federal standards for criminal cases. Proposition 115 (1990) completed it by amending Section 24 to require that criminal defendants' rights be construed consistently with the US Constitution.

The effect: in criminal cases, Section 13 now provides the same protection as the Fourth Amendment — no more, no less.

### IV. The Civil Case Exception

But the §24 lockstep provision applies only to criminal cases. In civil proceedings — including civil rights litigation, conservatorship challenges, privacy torts, and government accountability actions — Section 13 retains its full pre-1982 independent force.

This means: when the steward challenges surveillance, communication interception, or unauthorized access to his papers and effects in a civil action, California Section 13 may provide broader protection than the Fourth Amendment. The pre-1982 California case law interpreting Section 13 independently is available in civil cases even though it is foreclosed in criminal cases.

### V. For the Steward's Case

The steward documents SIM swap/clone interference, communication interception, medical records access without authorization, and surveillance. In a civil action:

- Section 13's independent protection of "papers and effects" extends to digital communications, electronic medical records, and financial documents
- The broader pre-1982 California standard for what constitutes an "unreasonable" search may apply
- State actors who accessed the steward's information without proper authorization violated not only the Fourth Amendment but also Section 13 as an independent California constitutional provision

This is the constitutional foundation for every privacy and surveillance claim brought in California state court.

# Article I, Section 3 — Historical Chain

## The Standard That Opens the Government's Books

---

### I. The Wound

Government operates in secret when it can. When it operates in secret, it serves itself instead of the people. Every corruption scandal, every abuse of power, every misuse of public funds happened behind closed doors. The people of California decided — three separate times — that the doors stay open.

### II. The Original Promise (1974)

Section 3(a), added by Proposition 7 in 1974, codified three fundamental rights: the right to instruct representatives, the right to petition for redress of grievances, and the right to assemble freely. These are the participation rights — the mechanism by which self-government actually functions.

### III. The Right of Access (2004 — Prop. 59)

In 2004, the voters added subdivision (b) — the constitutional right of access to government information. This was the most consequential amendment to Section 3 since its creation.

Proposition 59 did four things:

1. **Established a constitutional right of access** to information about the conduct of the people's business. Not a statutory right. A constitutional right. This means the Legislature cannot repeal it.

2. **Created the broad/narrow construction rule.** Statutes furthering access must be broadly construed. Statutes limiting access must be narrowly construed. This is an interpretive command to every court in California: when in doubt, the public gets access.

3. **Required findings for new limitations.** Any new statute that limits access must include findings demonstrating the interest being protected and the need for that protection. The Legislature cannot quietly close a door — it must explain why.

4. **Preserved the privacy balance.** Paragraph (3) explicitly states that the right of access does not override the right of privacy in Section 1. This is the tension at the heart of California open-government law: maximum access, but not at the cost of individual privacy.

### IV. The Local Compliance Mandate (2014 — Prop. 42)

In 2014, the voters added paragraph (7) — requiring local agencies to comply with the California Public Records Act and the Brown Act as a constitutional mandate. Before Prop. 42, local agencies could argue that PRA compliance was an unfunded state mandate. After Prop. 42, it is a constitutional obligation that cannot be avoided on funding grounds.

### V. For the Steward's Case

The steward submits PRA requests to police departments. When Antioch PD or Oakland PD delays, obstructs, or denies a records request, they are not just violating a statute — they are violating the California Constitution. Section 3(b)'s broad-construction rule means every ambiguity in the PRA is resolved in favor of disclosure. Section 3(b)(7) means local agencies cannot claim they lack resources to comply.

The petition clause in Section 3(a) protects the steward's pro se litigation as constitutionally protected petition activity. Filing a lawsuit IS petitioning the government for redress of grievances. Anyone who retaliates against the steward for filing lawsuits is retaliating against constitutionally protected petition activity — which is exactly what anti-SLAPP exists to prevent.

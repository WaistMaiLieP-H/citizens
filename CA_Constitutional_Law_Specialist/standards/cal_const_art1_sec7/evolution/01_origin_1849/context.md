# Art. I §8 (1849) — Origin Context

## The Wound

California's first Constitution was drafted in September-October 1849 at the Monterey Convention, before California was even admitted as a state (statehood came September 9, 1850). The delegates had just emerged from military rule — California had been governed by U.S. military governors since the Mexican-American War ended in 1848. They knew what it meant when government acted without legal process.

## The Text

The 1849 due process clause was in **Section 8** of Article I, not a standalone section. It was bundled with the double jeopardy prohibition, the self-incrimination prohibition, and the takings clause — all in one section, following the structure of the Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution almost verbatim.

The key phrase: **"nor be deprived of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law."**

This is a near-exact copy of the Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause. The 1849 delegates did not innovate on due process — they imported it wholesale from the federal Bill of Rights into California's first state constitution.

## What Is NOT Here

- No equal protection clause. The Fourteenth Amendment (which added equal protection at the federal level) would not be ratified until 1868. The 1849 California Constitution has due process but not equal protection.
- No standalone section. Due process is embedded in a multi-purpose section, not given its own provision.
- No "privileges and immunities" clause. That would come in 1879.
- "All men" in Section 1, not "all people." The gendered language would persist until the 1974 revision.

## The Section 1 Context

The 1849 Article I, Section 1 reads: "All men are by nature free and independent, and have certain unalienable rights, among which are those of enjoying and defending life and liberty: acquiring, possessing and protecting property: and pursuing and obtaining safety and happiness."

Note: no "privacy." Privacy would not be added until 1974. The 1849 natural-rights provision enumerates life, liberty, property, safety, and happiness — but not privacy.

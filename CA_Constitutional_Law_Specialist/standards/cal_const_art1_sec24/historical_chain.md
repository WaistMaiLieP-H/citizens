# Article I, Section 24 — Historical Chain

## The Standard That Makes California Rights Independent

---

### I. The Wound

The wound behind Section 24 is the perpetual tension between state sovereignty and federal supremacy in rights protection. When the US Supreme Court narrows a federal right, does the state right narrow with it? The answer in California — until 1990 — was no. The California Supreme Court under Chief Justice Rose Bird (1977-1986) aggressively interpreted the California Constitution as providing broader protections than the federal Constitution in criminal cases. This produced a decade of decisions that expanded exclusionary rule protections, broadened privacy rights, and limited police powers beyond what the US Supreme Court required.

### II. The Original Promise (1974)

Section 24 as originally enacted by Proposition 7 in 1974 stated the independent state grounds doctrine in its purest form: "Rights guaranteed by this Constitution are not dependent on those guaranteed by the United States Constitution." Full stop. No exceptions. California rights stand on their own.

The third sentence preserved California's analogue to the Ninth Amendment: "This declaration of rights may not be construed to impair or deny others retained by the people." Rights not enumerated are still retained.

### III. The 1990 Amendment (Prop. 115)

Proposition 115 — the Crime Victims Justice Reform Act — was a direct response to the Bird Court's expansive criminal-case interpretations. The voters added the second paragraph requiring that in criminal cases, defendant rights be construed "in a manner consistent with the Constitution of the United States." This was the lockstep provision: California criminal courts could no longer give defendants MORE protection than the US Constitution provides.

The political context matters. Chief Justice Bird and two associate justices were voted off the bench in 1986. Proposition 115 in 1990 was the legislative completion of the political revolution that began with that recall. The voters were explicit: they wanted criminal case rights aligned with federal standards, not exceeding them.

### IV. What Survived

The first sentence survived intact: "Rights guaranteed by this Constitution are not dependent on those guaranteed by the United States Constitution." This sentence has never been amended.

The lockstep provision applies ONLY to "criminal cases" and ONLY to criminal defendants' rights. It does not apply to:

- **Civil cases** — including civil rights litigation, conservatorship proceedings, custody proceedings, property disputes, privacy actions, or any other civil matter
- **Non-defendant rights** — victims' rights, witnesses' rights, or the rights of third parties are not covered by the lockstep provision
- **Affirmative rights** — Section 1's privacy right, Section 3's access right, Section 7's due process guarantee in civil contexts all retain full independence

### V. For the Steward's Case

The steward's primary litigation posture is civil, not criminal. He is a plaintiff, not a defendant. The lockstep provision of Section 24 does not apply to him.

This means every California constitutional right the steward invokes — privacy (§1), free speech (§2), petition and access (§3), due process (§7), search and seizure (§13) — operates at its full independent scope. If the US Supreme Court narrows a federal right, the California right is unaffected. If the Ninth Circuit interprets the Fourth Amendment narrowly, California Section 13 in a civil case is not bound by that interpretation.

Section 24 is the interpretive master key. In the steward's hands, it unlocks the full breadth of California constitutional protection that the lockstep provision closed to criminal defendants.

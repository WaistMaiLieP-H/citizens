# Article I, Section 2 — Historical Chain

## The Standard That Says You Can Say It

---

### I. The Wound

Every government that has ever existed has tried to silence the people it governs. The California framers knew this because they lived it. The 1849 Constitution was written by men who had just come through a military government in California that exercised prior censorship. The gold rush brought newspapers that the military governor tried to control. The right to speak freely was not theoretical to them — it was the first thing they needed to function as a self-governing people.

### II. The Original Promise (1849/1879)

The 1849 California Constitution included a free speech provision modeled on the First Amendment but written as an affirmative grant: "Every person may freely speak, write, and publish." The First Amendment says what Congress shall NOT do. California says what every person MAY do. This is not a stylistic difference — it is a structural one. California's free speech right is held by the person, not constrained from the government.

The 1879 second Constitution carried this forward. The Workingmen's Party that drove the 1879 Convention knew what it meant when speech was suppressed — they had been the targets of it. Denis Kearney was arrested for incitement. The newspapers aligned with the railroad and mining interests campaigned for his suppression. The 1879 Convention preserved the affirmative speech right because the delegates had personal experience of what happens when it is taken away.

### III. The 1974 Revision (Prop. 7)

Proposition 7 in 1974 comprehensively revised Article I. Section 2(a) preserved the original free speech text with minor modernization. The structure remained: an affirmative grant to every person, plus a prohibition on laws restraining speech or press.

### IV. The Shield (1980 — Prop. 5)

In 1980, the voters added subdivision (b) — the constitutional shield law. This was not a new idea. The statutory shield law (Evidence Code §1070) had existed since 1965. But the Legislature can repeal a statute. The voters elevated the shield to constitutional status because they understood that a statutory shield is only as durable as the next legislative session.

The shield protects publishers, editors, reporters, and persons connected with news media from being held in contempt for refusing to disclose sources or unpublished information. The constitutional elevation means no statute, no court order, and no administrative body can override the shield without amending the Constitution itself.

### V. For the Steward's Case

The steward publishes compliance audits. He files pro se litigation that documents government misconduct. He operates a platform that surfaces public-record evidence of agency failures. Every one of these acts is protected by Section 2(a)'s affirmative right to "freely speak, write and publish."

The shield law provision in Section 2(b) may protect the steward's unpublished investigative materials — notes, drafts, source documents gathered in the process of preparing publications — from compelled disclosure. The question is whether the steward's activities constitute "gathering, receiving or processing of information for communication to the public." If they do, the constitutional shield applies.

Section 2 is the textual foundation that makes the steward's work legal. Without it, the entire enterprise of publishing government failures is an act of courage. With it, it is a constitutional right.

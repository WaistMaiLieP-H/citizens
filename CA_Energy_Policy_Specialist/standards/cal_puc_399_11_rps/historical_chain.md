# Historical Chain — California Renewables Portfolio Standard (RPS)
## Cal. Public Utilities Code § 399.11 — Stats. 2002, Ch. 516 (SB 1078); updated Stats. 2018, Ch. 312 (SB 100)

---

## The wound

Before 2002, California electric utilities had no obligation to procure electricity from renewable sources. Procurement decisions were driven by least-cost fuel selection: natural gas, coal-fired power imported from neighboring states, large hydro, and nuclear made up the overwhelming majority of the generation mix. Renewable technologies — wind, solar, geothermal, small hydro, biomass — existed at the margins, supported only by voluntary programs and federal tax incentives that were inconsistent and subject to political expiration.

The harm was diffuse but accumulating. California's air quality remained poor, with electricity generation contributing to regional smog and particulate emissions. The state had no mechanism to build renewable generation at scale because utilities had no contractual reason to sign long-term power purchase agreements with renewable developers. Without those agreements, developers could not secure project financing. Without project financing, renewable capacity could not be built. The market would not solve this on its own: fossil fuel generation was cheaper in the short run, and utilities had no structural incentive to pay a premium for the environmental attributes of renewable power.

The 2000–2001 California electricity crisis — rolling blackouts, price spikes, the collapse of wholesale electricity markets deregulated in 1996 — also exposed California's vulnerability to fuel-price volatility and the concentrated market power of a small number of large generators. Diversifying the generation mix became a matter of grid reliability, not only environmental policy.

---

## The legislative response

In 2002, the Legislature enacted SB 1078 (Stats. 2002, Ch. 516), adding Article 16 to the California Public Utilities Code. Section 399.11 established the legislative findings for the California Renewables Portfolio Standard. The findings enumerated nine independent justifications for the program: displacing fossil fuel, adding new generation capacity within the Western Electricity Coordinating Council, reducing air pollution and toxics, meeting climate goals, promoting stable retail rates, diversifying the generation resource base, supporting resource adequacy, ensuring grid reliability, and facilitating transmission and land-use planning.

The original RPS set a target of 20% renewable generation by 2017. Successive amendments accelerated the timeline and raised the targets. By the time of the most recent amendment, SB 100 (Stats. 2018, Ch. 312, Sec. 2, effective January 1, 2019), the targets were 50% by December 31, 2026; 60% by December 31, 2030; and a 100% zero-carbon electricity supply by December 31, 2045.

---

## The current state

The California RPS is administered by the California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC) for investor-owned utilities and by the California Energy Commission (CEC) for publicly owned utilities. Utilities must demonstrate compliance through Renewable Energy Credits (RECs) and long-term power purchase agreements with eligible renewable generators.

The operative provisions downstream of § 399.11 — §§ 399.12 through 399.33 — define eligible renewable energy resources, compliance percentages, REC banking rules, tradeable REC standards, and the alternative compliance mechanism. Disadvantaged community air quality considerations (§ 399.11(e)) require that RPS implementation account for the air quality co-benefits in communities already burdened by poor air.

Section 399.11 is the doctrinal anchor: every procurement decision, every compliance filing, and every enforcement action under the RPS traces back to the nine legislative findings enumerated here.

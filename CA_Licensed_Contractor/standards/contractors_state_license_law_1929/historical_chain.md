# Historical Chain — Contractors State License Law (1929)
## Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §§ 7000 et seq. — origin: Stats. 1929, Ch. 791

---

## The wound

The 1920s building boom in California was one of the largest in the state's history. The population of Los Angeles doubled in the decade. Housing tracts, commercial buildings, and industrial facilities were being constructed at a pace that outran any systematic oversight of who was doing the work. Anyone could hold themselves out as a contractor. There was no license to obtain, no examination to pass, no bond to post, and no agency to file a complaint with when a contractor disappeared with a deposit, delivered defective work, or abandoned a project half-finished.

The documented harms were financial and physical. Homeowners paid for work that was never completed or was so poorly executed that it had to be torn out. Subcontractors were not paid because the general contractor had diverted the funds. Property owners had no public registry to consult to verify that a contractor existed as a business entity, had any track record, or could be located after a dispute arose. The combination of a rapidly growing, largely anonymous construction labor market and the absence of any licensing requirement created systematic opportunities for fraud and abandonment that individual contracting parties could not police on their own.

---

## The legislative response

The Legislature enacted Chapter 791 of the 1929 Statutes, approved June 13, 1929 by Governor Clement Calhoun Young. The act was titled "An act providing for the registration of contractors" — it had no popular name at enactment; the name "Contractors' License Law" was not added until the 1933 amendment (Ch. 573, Sec. 14, which added a new § 15 to the original act stating the popular-name citation).

The original act required any contractor to obtain a license from the Director of the Department of Professional and Vocational Standards, who served as Registrar. The license application required identification of the applicant and the general nature of the contracting business. A public registry was established, open to inspection during office hours. The grounds for complaint and license revocation were enumerated: abandonment without legal excuse, diversion of funds with intent to defraud, fraudulent departure from plans or specifications, and willful acts causing substantial injury. Criminal penalties — fine up to $500 or county jail up to six months — applied to unlicensed contracting.

The law evolved rapidly. The 1931 amendment (Ch. 578) substantially expanded the Registrar's enforcement authority. The 1933 amendment (Ch. 573) added the popular name and reduced annual renewal fees during the Depression. The 1935 amendment (Ch. 816) created the Contractors' State License Board (CSLB) — replacing the Director/Registrar model with a seven-member industry board composed of practicing contractors appointed by the Governor. The CSLB name and structure have survived to the present day.

---

## The current state

The Contractors State License Law is codified at California Business and Professions Code §§ 7000–7191 (Division 3, Chapter 9). Administration is by the CSLB under the California Department of Consumer Affairs. The modern law requires a license for any contractor working on projects with an aggregate contract price above $500 (increased from the original 1929 exemption of $200), requires a qualifying individual examination, mandates workers' compensation coverage and a contractor's bond, and provides an administrative complaint and disciplinary process that has handled hundreds of thousands of complaints since the CSLB's formation.

The 1929 act's structural innovations — public registry, enumerated grounds for discipline, criminal penalty for unlicensed practice, and fee-funded administration — all survive in the modern BPC framework. The shift from Director/Registrar to an industry board (1935) is itself a governance doctrine: the people most knowledgeable about what constitutes acceptable contractor conduct became the regulators, subject to gubernatorial appointment and public accountability.

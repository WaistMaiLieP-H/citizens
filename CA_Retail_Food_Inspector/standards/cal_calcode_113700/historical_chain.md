# Historical Chain — California Retail Food Code (CalCode)
## Cal. Health & Safety Code § 113700 et seq. — Stats. 2006, Ch. 23; operative July 1, 2007

---

## The wound

For most of the twentieth century, food safety regulation at California retail food facilities — restaurants, grocery stores, school cafeterias, mobile food vendors, farmers markets, temporary food stalls — was administered county by county. Each county environmental health department wrote its own retail food facility ordinance. The result was a patchwork: the standard for a commercial kitchen in San Francisco was different from the standard in Fresno, which was different from the standard in San Bernardino. A restaurant chain operating in multiple counties had to comply with multiple, sometimes conflicting, sets of requirements.

The harms from this fragmentation were concrete. Foodborne illness outbreaks — Salmonella, E. coli, hepatitis A, norovirus — did not respect county lines. Food workers moved between counties. Supply chains were statewide. When an outbreak occurred, investigation and enforcement required coordinating between county agencies applying different standards, different documentation requirements, and different inspection schedules. No uniform statewide basis existed for comparing inspection results, identifying systemic patterns, or ensuring that a food facility operator who had been shut down in one county for critical violations was held to the same standard when they opened in another county.

The prior California law, the California Uniform Retail Food Facilities Law (CURFFL), had been in place but had not achieved true statewide uniformity because counties retained significant authority to adopt local variations. The voluntary nature of many provisions and the absence of standardized inspection grading had left gaps.

---

## The legislative response

In 2006, the Legislature enacted Stats. 2006, Ch. 23, repealing the CURFFL and replacing it with the California Retail Food Code (CalCode), effective January 1, 2007 and operative July 1, 2007. Section 113700 established the short title and citation form for the entire code. Part 7 of Health and Safety Code Division 104 (§§ 113700–114437) set a uniform statewide standard applicable to every retail food facility in California.

CalCode established statewide definitions of food safety terms, standardized inspection procedures, created a uniform grading and posting system, defined critical violations and minor violations with uniform consequence levels, mandated operator and food handler certification requirements, and gave local enforcement agencies (county environmental health departments) the authority to administer the code while operating under a single statewide standard.

The adoption tracked FDA's Model Food Code — California's approach of adopting and adapting the federal model, then adding state-specific requirements, is the same structural pattern used in the Sherman Law and other California consumer protection statutes.

---

## The current state

Every restaurant, grocery store, food truck, school cafeteria, mobile food vendor, and temporary food facility in California is subject to CalCode. Local environmental health departments conduct inspections, issue permits, impose corrective action requirements, and suspend or revoke permits for critical violations.

The inspection grading system — displayed in the public-facing posting requirement — is CalCode's most visible consumer protection mechanism. Critical violations (those that create an immediate health hazard) require immediate correction or closure. Non-critical violations are documented and require correction by the next inspection.

For the CA Retail Food Inspector, § 113700 is the anchor for the entire regulatory framework. Every inspection citation, every permit action, and every food-safety consultation flows from the statewide standard this section establishes. The pre-CalCode fragmentation is the reason the statewide uniform standard was enacted — and the reason why maintaining that uniformity across 58 counties with their own local health departments is itself an ongoing compliance challenge.

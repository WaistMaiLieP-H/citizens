# Historical Chain — California Evidence Code §§ 1450-1454 Self-Authentication

## The wound

Before the 1965 Evidence Code, California courts required authentication of virtually every document through witness testimony. This was the common law default — a writing was not admitted unless someone with personal knowledge testified to its authenticity. For routine, uncontested documents like published statutes, certified government records, and commercial invoices, this requirement was wasteful, formalistic, and a drain on judicial resources.

The specific wound: in an era of expanding government bureaucracy and commercial transactions, the requirement to authenticate every certified government record or official publication through live testimony created unnecessary delay and expense. Courts were being asked to require police officers to appear and testify that their own official reports were genuine — when those reports bore the officer's signature and the department's seal. Lawyers were required to authenticate official statutes by calling a witness to say that the printed publication was indeed a real publication.

## The legislative response

The 1965 Evidence Code (Stats. 1965, Ch. 299) introduced §§ 1450-1454 as a set of presumptions for categories where the need for authentication was self-evident from the document's form and origin. The California Law Revision Commission's deliberate choice was to codify the categories narrowly — limited to official seals and signatures, certified copies, official publications, general printed publications, and trade inscriptions.

The presumption structure was chosen rather than a flat exception. A presumption is rebuttable: if the opponent has evidence that defeats the presumption (forged signature, altered record, fraudulent certification), they can rebut it and require the proponent to go back to § 1400(a) extrinsic evidence. This balanced efficiency (no testimony needed for routine documents) with safeguards (fraud can still be exposed).

## Federal comparison — FRE 902 (1975)

The Federal Rules of Evidence, effective 1975, codified self-authentication in FRE 902. The federal rule was broader than California's — it included trade inscriptions, certified domestic and foreign records, and newspapers. A 2017 amendment to FRE 902 added certified electronic records (FRE 902(13) and (14)), recognizing that digital evidence needed a self-authentication pathway. California's §§ 1450-1454 have not been similarly updated, which means electronic records in California state court must generally be authenticated through other means.

## The current state

Sections 1450-1454 remain in their original 1965 enacted form for the core provisions. The self-authentication categories in California are narrower than in federal court, with no direct equivalent to the FRE 902(13)/(14) certified electronic record pathway. The rebuttable-presumption structure has been confirmed by California courts: once an opponent introduces competent rebuttal evidence, the presumption is defeated and the proponent bears the §1400(a) extrinsic evidence burden.

## Steward relevance

For contested official documents in the steward's cases, §§ 1450-1454 are the gates that must be attacked. If an opposing party offers a purported police report, court order, or government record, that document enters with the §1450 presumption. But the presumption is rebuttable — and evidence that the claimed originating agency does not have the document in its records (the OPD records pickup) is exactly the kind of competent rebuttal evidence that defeats §1450 and forces the proponent to §1400(a). Once the presumption is rebutted, the document cannot be authenticated without independent extrinsic evidence that it is genuine.

# Historical Chain — California Evidence Code § 1552 Computer Printouts

## The wound

The 1965 Evidence Code was drafted in an era of paper records. Section 1552 was originally enacted in 1965 as a provision about business records printed from data processing equipment — IBM punch-card systems and early mainframes were in use, and California courts needed a rule for authenticating "machine-generated" printouts that no human had personally prepared in the traditional sense.

The original 1965 wound: there was no established evidentiary rule for machine-generated records. Common law authentication required testimony from someone with personal knowledge. But for data processing output, the "author" was a machine, and the relevant knowledge was about how the machine worked and whether it was accurate — technical knowledge that lay witnesses typically did not have. This created a gap: accurate machine-generated records were being excluded from evidence or required extensive foundation testimony.

By the 1990s, the wound had deepened. Computers were no longer novelty devices but the backbone of commercial transactions, government record-keeping, and communications. Email, database records, spreadsheets, and electronically stored information were the primary documentary evidence in commercial litigation. The 1965 § 1552 (originally designed for punch-card printouts) needed updating for modern computing and digital imaging.

## The 1998 amendment and § 1553

Stats. 1998, Ch. 100 amended § 1552 to modernize the computer printout rule for the digital era and added § 1553 specifically for digital image records (photographs, video captures, digital prints). The 1998 amendment recognized that modern electronic records required a more flexible presumption framework — requiring a live custodian for every electronic record was no longer practicable.

The 1998 legislature also recognized the need for the official-records exception in § 1552(b): official government records should be authenticated through the official certification framework (§ 1530, § 452.5), not through the general computer printout presumption, to maintain the integrity and trust associated with certified official documents.

## The current state

Section 1552 (computer data) and § 1553 (digital images) provide a presumption of accuracy for printed representations of electronic content, subject to a rebuttable challenge mechanism. Once challenged, the proponent bears the preponderance burden.

California's framework remains slightly behind the federal FRE 902(13)/(14) framework, which allows self-authentication of certified electronic records without even the need to call a custodian in many circumstances. California requires either the § 1552 presumption (rebuttable) or the §§ 1450-1451/1530 certified-record pathway (for official records).

## Steward relevance

Every electronic record in the steward's cases — pension database printouts, email records, electronic medical records, digital communications, system logs — is governed by § 1552 or § 1553. The strategic use of these sections is bidirectional:

1. The steward uses § 1552/1553 to admit his own electronic records (emails, screenshots, system logs showing unauthorized activity) without full expert foundation
2. The steward challenges opposing parties' electronic records by introducing evidence of inaccuracy or unreliability — any evidence that the data was entered without authorization, that the system was manipulated, or that the printout was modified after the fact rebutts the presumption and shifts the proof burden back to the proponent

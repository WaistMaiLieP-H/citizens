# Historical Chain — California Evidence Code § 1400 Authentication of a Writing

## The wound

Before 1965, California evidence law was scattered across the Code of Civil Procedure, common law decisions, and a patchwork of statutes that had accumulated since the 1872 Field Code. The authentication rules were inconsistent and incomplete. Different courts applied different standards. Lawyers disagreed about what showing was sufficient to authenticate a document, and trial judges applied varying burdens depending on the document type and the forum.

The specific wound that § 1400 addresses: a writing offered as evidence but not proven to be what it purports to be could slip into the record unchallenged simply because no one had formulated a clear, universal standard for what the proponent had to show. Authentication doctrine was informal, judge-made, and inconsistently applied. This allowed fabricated, altered, and misrepresented documents to be admitted into evidence — with the opponent having no clear procedural basis to object.

By the mid-20th century, California had one of the most complex and fragmented evidentiary systems in the country. The California Law Revision Commission was established in 1953 specifically to study and recommend revision of California's evidence law.

## The legislative response

The California Evidence Code was enacted in 1965 (Stats. 1965, Ch. 299), effective January 1, 1967. The Evidence Code was a comprehensive recodification — the most ambitious revision of California evidence law in the state's history. It replaced the scattered provisions in the Code of Civil Procedure and unified California evidence law into a single, logically organized code.

Division 11 (Writings, §§ 1400-1605) contained the authentication rules. The Law Revision Commission's deliberate choice in drafting § 1400 was a single, inclusive definition of "authentication" that would govern all writings — not a collection of type-specific rules. The definition has two prongs:

(a) Extrinsic evidence sufficient to sustain a finding — leaving the specific modes of authentication to §§ 1410-1454 (means of authentication, distinctive characteristics, handwriting comparison, official records, self-authentication)

(b) Any other means provided by law — a catch-all that preserves statutory presumptions, judicial notice, stipulation, and any future mechanisms

The "sufficient to sustain a finding" standard was deliberately chosen as a threshold screening test — lower than proof by a preponderance, but requiring enough evidence that a reasonable jury could find the writing authentic. The California Supreme Court has confirmed that § 1400 sets a low bar, and that contested authenticity goes to weight rather than admissibility once the threshold is crossed.

## Never amended

Section 1400 was enacted in 1965 and has never been amended. It is one of the few California statutes that has remained in its original form for 60 years. This reflects the foundational character of the provision — it defines "authentication" itself, and changing that definition would require rewriting California evidence doctrine from the ground up.

## The current state

Section 1400 is the universal definition of authentication for all California state court proceedings. Every writing offered as evidence in California — police reports, medical records, real estate transaction documents, pension applications, electronic records, photographs — must pass through § 1400's gateway: it must be shown to be what the proponent claims it is.

The proponent bears the burden. The opponent does not have to prove the writing is fabricated; the proponent has to prove it is real. This burden allocation has structural significance in contested-document litigation: the party offering a document must be prepared to satisfy § 1400 before the document is admitted.

## Steward relevance

Section 1400 is the procedural lever for every contested writing in the steward's cases. The OPD records pickup (October 2025, confirming that a purported 6/2/2009 OPD report does not exist in OPD's records) is a concrete piece of § 1400(a) disproving evidence: the agency that purportedly issued the document does not have it in its records system, making it impossible for the proponent to satisfy § 1400(a). The 19 unsigned documents in the Honeysuckle sale and the pension application without participant signature are also § 1400 authentication failures — documents that purport to bear a participant's authorization but cannot be authenticated as actually having been signed by that person.

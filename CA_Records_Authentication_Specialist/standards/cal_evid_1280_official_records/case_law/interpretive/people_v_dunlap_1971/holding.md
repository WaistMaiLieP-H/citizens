# People v. Dunlap, 18 Cal.App.3d 1 (1971)

## The Case

The prosecution offered an official record that contained information not based on the recording public employee's personal knowledge. The record included statements and observations reported to the public employee by third parties — some of whom had no official duty to report accurately. The defendant challenged the record's admissibility under § 1280, arguing that the trustworthiness requirement was not met because the information sources were unreliable.

## What the Court Held

The Court of Appeal:

1. **Official records admitted under § 1280 must be based on the personal knowledge of the recording public employee or on information provided by a person who had a duty to report the information accurately.** The official records exception does not permit the admission of hearsay-within-hearsay where the underlying source had no duty to report and no personal knowledge.

2. **The duty-to-report requirement is a component of the trustworthiness analysis under § 1280(c).** When a public employee records information received from a third party, the trustworthiness of the record depends on the trustworthiness of the source. A source with a legal or professional duty to report accurately (another public employee in the chain of command, a medical professional reporting a cause of death, a records custodian certifying business records) provides trustworthy information. A bystander, an interested party, or an anonymous tipster does not.

3. **The level of hearsay in an official record must be examined.** An official record that simply transcribes the statements of interested parties — without independent verification by the recording employee — does not meet the trustworthiness requirement, even though the record itself was created by a public employee within the scope of duty and near the time of the event.

## The Rule

**Official records under § 1280 must be based on the personal knowledge of the public employee who created them or on information from a source with a duty to report accurately. Records that merely transcribe the unverified statements of interested parties or bystanders fail the trustworthiness requirement of § 1280(c).**

## Why This Case Matters

For the steward's case, Dunlap is directly relevant to police reports and other official records that contain statements from interested parties. When a police report records the statements of one party in a custody dispute, a neighbor with an axe to grind, or an anonymous caller whose identity was never verified, the report's admissibility under § 1280 depends on whether the source had a duty to report accurately. The OPD and APD reports in the steward's case involve reports of statements from interested parties — under Dunlap, the trustworthiness of those records depends on the trustworthiness of those sources. A police report that simply transcribes the allegations of one party, without independent officer observation or verification, is vulnerable to a § 1280(c) challenge.

# People v. Martinez, 22 Cal. 4th 106 (2000)

## The Case

The prosecution offered computer-generated records from a law enforcement database as evidence. The defendant challenged the records' admissibility under the official records exception (Evidence Code §1280), arguing the records were not trustworthy.

## What the Court Held

The California Supreme Court:

1. **Computer-generated records from a law enforcement database qualify as "official records" under §1280 if they meet the three requirements:** (a) made by or under the direction of a public employee, (b) within the scope of duty, (c) sources and method of preparation indicate trustworthiness.

2. **The trustworthiness requirement (§1280(c)) is the critical gatekeeper.** The proponent must show that the system that generated the records is reliable — that data is entered accurately, maintained properly, and retrieved without alteration. A foundation must be laid showing the system's reliability.

3. **The mere fact that records are computer-generated does not make them unreliable**, but the proponent must establish the reliability of the specific system that created them.

## The Rule

**Computer-generated law enforcement records are admissible under the official records exception (§1280) if the proponent establishes the reliability of the system that generated them. The trustworthiness requirement demands a foundation showing accurate data entry, proper maintenance, and unaltered retrieval.**

## Why This Case Matters

For the steward's case, CLETS records, police reports, and court records are all "official records" under §1280. Their admissibility depends on the trustworthiness of the system that generated them. If records were entered inaccurately, maintained improperly, or altered after creation, the §1280(c) trustworthiness requirement fails — and the records are inadmissible.

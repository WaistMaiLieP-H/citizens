## The Wound

Government records have always carried an aura of authority. A police report, a court filing, a vital statistics record — these documents arrive bearing the weight of the state. Before the 1965 Evidence Code, California law treated official records with a general presumption of reliability but without a clear, testable framework for when that presumption should hold and when it should break.

The result was that official records were admitted almost reflexively. If a document bore an agency letterhead and a public employee's name, courts accepted it. The problem was that official records are only as trustworthy as the people who create them and the systems that produce them. A police report based on hearsay from an interested party is not the same as a police report based on the officer's own observations. A court record entered months after the event it purports to record is not the same as one entered contemporaneously. A government database record generated by a system with known data-entry errors is not the same as one generated by a verified, audited system.

Without a testable trustworthiness requirement, unreliable official records entered the evidentiary record and were treated as presumptively true — precisely because they were official. The state's imprimatur became a substitute for actual reliability.

## The Promise

Section 1280 replaced reflexive acceptance with a three-part test. The writing must be (a) made by a public employee within the scope of duty, (b) made at or near the time of the recorded event, and (c) created under circumstances indicating trustworthiness. All three requirements must be met. If any one fails, the hearsay exception does not apply and the record cannot be admitted for the truth of its contents.

The trustworthiness requirement in subsection (c) is the critical innovation. It acknowledges that official records can be unreliable — that public employees can record false information, that systems can be compromised, that the mere fact of government authorship does not guarantee accuracy. The proponent of the record must show that the sources of information and the method and time of preparation indicate trustworthiness. If the circumstances indicate the opposite — if the information came from an unreliable source, if the method of preparation was sloppy, if the record was created long after the fact — the exception fails.

## The Arc

Section 1280 was enacted in 1965 and amended once, in 1996 (Stats. 1996, Ch. 642, Sec. 4), to add "in any civil or criminal proceeding" to the preamble. The 1996 amendment resolved a split in authority about whether the official records exception applied in criminal cases — it does.

The section's case law has steadily reinforced the trustworthiness requirement as a real gate, not a rubber stamp. People v. Martinez (2000) held that computer-generated law enforcement records must be supported by a foundation showing the system's reliability. People v. Dunlap (1971) held that official records must be based on personal knowledge of the recording employee or information from someone with a duty to report — hearsay from bystanders does not qualify. Rupf v. Yan (2000) held that § 1280 does not apply where circumstances indicate untrustworthiness despite technical compliance with the other requirements.

For the steward's case, the trustworthiness requirement is where the real work happens. Official records that were created based on false information from interested parties, that contradict other official records, that were entered into systems with documented reliability problems, or that do not exist in the issuing agency's records system (the OPD situation) — all fail the § 1280(c) trustworthiness test. The official seal does not save an untrustworthy record.

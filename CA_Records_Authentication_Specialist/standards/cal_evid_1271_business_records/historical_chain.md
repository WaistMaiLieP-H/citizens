## The Wound

Before the modern business records exception, the hearsay rule excluded virtually all business records from evidence unless the person who actually created the record appeared in court to testify. In an era of large organizations — hospitals, banks, insurance companies, government agencies — this was paralyzing. The person who entered a transaction in a ledger, recorded a diagnosis in a chart, or logged a payment in a system might be unavailable, unidentifiable, or one of dozens of employees who touched the record during its lifecycle.

The common law "shop book rule" was a partial fix, but it was narrow and inconsistent across jurisdictions. Some courts required the bookkeeper to testify. Some accepted the records only if the business was small enough that one person kept all the books. Some excluded records entirely if the original recorder could not be identified. The result was that reliable, routine business records — the very records businesses depended on to operate — were frequently excluded from evidence, while unreliable oral testimony about the same events was admitted.

The harm was asymmetric. Parties with resources could locate and produce every witness in the record-keeping chain. Parties without resources could not. The hearsay rule, designed to ensure reliability, was instead ensuring that the party with more money could prove its case and the party with less money could not.

## The Promise

Section 1271 created a structured exception: business records are admissible if the proponent establishes four things. The record was made in the regular course of business — not created for litigation. The record was made at or near the time of the event — not reconstructed from memory later. A custodian or qualified witness testifies about what the record is and how it was prepared — the foundation witness requirement. And the sources and methods indicate trustworthiness — the ultimate reliability gate.

The four-part structure is the promise: reliable business records come in; unreliable ones do not. The custodian-witness requirement ensures that someone familiar with the record-keeping system is available for cross-examination. The trustworthiness requirement ensures that records created under suspicious circumstances — prepared for litigation, based on unreliable sources, maintained in a system with known errors — can be excluded despite meeting the other three requirements.

## The Arc

Section 1271 was enacted in 1965 as part of the Evidence Code recodification. It was amended in 2006 (Stats. 2006, Ch. 69, Sec. 20) in a non-substantive technical revision. The operative requirements have not changed since 1965.

The section's case law has developed two important lines. First, the custodian-witness requirement has been interpreted to allow any "qualified witness" — not necessarily the custodian of records — to lay the § 1271 foundation, as long as the witness is familiar with the record-keeping system. This expanded the pool of available foundation witnesses. Second, the trustworthiness requirement has been applied to exclude records that technically qualify as business records but were created under circumstances that undermine reliability — records prepared in anticipation of litigation (People v. Dean), records maintained by a party that did not create them and cannot vouch for the original system (Midland Funding v. Romero), and records with internal inconsistencies that suggest alteration.

For the steward's case, § 1271 is the foundation for challenging or supporting every business record: medical billing records, financial transaction records, debt collection records, insurance records, and employment records. The custodian-witness requirement means that records offered without foundation testimony are inadmissible. The trustworthiness requirement means that records with documented inconsistencies, records maintained by entities that did not create them, and records prepared outside the regular course of business can be challenged and excluded.

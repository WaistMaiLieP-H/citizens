# People v. Dean, 174 Cal.App.4th 186 (2009)

## The Case

The prosecution offered business records as evidence, and the defendant challenged the foundation laid for their admission under Evidence Code § 1271. The issue was whether the proponent had properly established all four requirements of the business records exception — particularly the custodian-witness requirement and the trustworthiness of the records.

## What the Court Held

The Court of Appeal:

1. **The foundation for business records under § 1271 requires testimony from a custodian or other qualified witness who can testify to the identity of the record and the mode of its preparation.** The witness must be familiar with the record-keeping system — not necessarily the person who created the specific record, but someone who understands how the business creates and maintains its records.

2. **The qualified witness must have sufficient knowledge to testify about the regular course of business, the timing of record creation, and the sources of information.** A witness who merely identifies a document by its appearance, without knowledge of the system that produced it, does not satisfy the foundation requirement.

3. **Records prepared in anticipation of litigation, rather than in the regular course of business, do not qualify under § 1271.** The "regular course of business" requirement excludes documents created specifically to build a legal case, even if the business routinely creates similar documents for non-litigation purposes. The test is whether this particular record was created as part of the business's routine operations or as part of a litigation strategy.

4. **The trustworthiness requirement in § 1271(d) is an independent gate.** Even records that satisfy (a), (b), and (c) can be excluded if the sources of information and method of preparation indicate the record is untrustworthy.

## The Rule

**The business records foundation under § 1271 requires a qualified witness with actual knowledge of the record-keeping system. The witness must be able to testify about regular course of business, timing, and information sources. Records created in anticipation of litigation do not qualify. Trustworthiness is an independent requirement that can defeat admission even when the other three elements are met.**

## Why This Case Matters

For the steward's case, Dean establishes two critical points. First, records offered without a qualified foundation witness are inadmissible — the opposing party cannot simply hand the court a stack of business records and ask that they be admitted without someone testifying to how they were created. Second, records created in anticipation of litigation — such as reports prepared specifically for use in custody proceedings, documents assembled to support a legal position, or records compiled by a party building a case — do not qualify as regular-course-of-business records and cannot enter evidence through § 1271.

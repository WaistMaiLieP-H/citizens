## The Wound

Before the 1965 Evidence Code, California had no single, clear rule requiring authentication before a writing could be admitted into evidence. The rules were scattered across the Code of Civil Procedure, inconsistent in application, and riddled with gaps. Trial courts applied different standards in different departments. Some judges required strict proof of authorship; others accepted documents at face value when no one objected. The result was that forged, altered, and misattributed documents entered the record regularly — not because judges were corrupt, but because there was no uniform gate that said: you must prove this document is what you claim it is before I will consider it.

The harm was not abstract. Unauthenticated documents were used to take property, deny custody, commit people to institutions, and convict the innocent. Every time a court relied on a writing that nobody had proved was genuine, the court's own legitimacy was borrowed against a document that might be a fabrication.

## The Promise

Section 1401 made the authentication requirement mandatory and universal. Subsection (a) says: authentication is required before a writing may be received in evidence. No exceptions. No discretion. No "unless the other side doesn't object." Subsection (b) extends the requirement to secondary evidence — copies, summaries, testimony about contents. Even if the original is lost, the secondary evidence still must be authenticated.

The promise is structural: no writing enters the evidentiary record of any California proceeding without first being proven to be what it claims to be. The proponent bears the burden. The court enforces the gate. And the opposing party has the right to demand that the gate be enforced.

## The Arc

Section 1401 was enacted in 1965 as part of the comprehensive Evidence Code recodification. It has never been amended — not once in over 60 years. This stability reflects its foundational nature: the mandatory authentication requirement is so basic to the integrity of judicial proceedings that no legislature has seen reason to modify it. The section works in tandem with § 1400 (which defines authentication) and §§ 1410-1421 (which provide the means). Together, they form the authentication triad that governs every writing offered in every California state court proceeding.

In the digital age, § 1401's mandatory gate has become more important, not less. Digital documents are easier to fabricate, alter, and misattribute than paper documents. Courts applying § 1401 to digital evidence (see People v. Goldsmith, 59 Cal.4th 258 (2014); People v. Valdez, 201 Cal.App.4th 1429 (2011)) have confirmed that the authentication requirement applies with full force to photographs, videos, social media posts, computer printouts, and all other digital writings. The gate does not weaken because the writing is electronic.

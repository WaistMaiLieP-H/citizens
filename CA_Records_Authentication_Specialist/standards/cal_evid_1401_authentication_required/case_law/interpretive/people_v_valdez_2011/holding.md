# People v. Valdez, 201 Cal.App.4th 1429 (2011)

## The Case

The prosecution offered printouts of the defendant's MySpace page as evidence of gang affiliation and motive. The defendant objected on authentication grounds, arguing that anyone could have created the MySpace page or posted the content attributed to the defendant, and that the prosecution had not established that the defendant personally created or controlled the page.

## What the Court Held

The Court of Appeal:

1. **Social media evidence must be authenticated under §§ 1400-1401 just like any other writing.** The fact that content appears on a social media page associated with a particular name does not, by itself, establish that the named person actually created or posted the content.

2. **The proponent of social media evidence must present evidence that the purported author actually created the content.** A MySpace page bearing someone's name and photograph is not self-authenticating. Anyone can create a page in another person's name. The proponent must show something beyond the page itself — IP address evidence, testimony from someone who saw the defendant create or use the page, content that only the defendant would know, or other circumstantial evidence linking the defendant to the authorship.

3. **The risk of fabrication is heightened with social media evidence.** Unlike a signed letter or a business record created in the regular course of business, social media content can be created by anyone claiming to be anyone. This heightened risk does not change the legal standard (still "sufficient to sustain a finding"), but it affects what evidence is needed to meet that standard.

## The Rule

**Social media evidence requires authentication showing that the purported author actually created the content. A page bearing someone's name is not enough. The proponent must produce evidence — circumstantial or direct — linking the purported author to the actual creation of the content.**

## Why This Case Matters

For the steward's case, Valdez is directly relevant wherever social media or electronic communications are attributed to a particular person. If opposing parties have offered communications, posts, or electronic documents attributed to the steward or others, Valdez requires that the attribution be authenticated — not assumed. This is particularly important where the steward's case involves allegations of SIM swaps, intercepted communications, and impersonation: if someone else controlled the steward's phone number or accounts, evidence attributed to the steward through those channels may fail authentication under the Valdez standard.

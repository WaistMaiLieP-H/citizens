# Qualified Immunity Analysis

Operational workflow for analyzing a qualified immunity defense raised against a Section 1983 claim.

---

## Step 1: Was There a Constitutional Violation?

This is the merits question. Look at the facts and determine whether the defendant actually violated a constitutional right.

If no violation occurred, the case fails on the merits and qualified immunity never matters. Analyze this prong fully before moving on.

Corpus reference: `standards/42_usc_1983/`

---

## Step 2: Was the Right "Clearly Established" at the Time?

This is the qualified immunity question. It asks whether the law was clear enough at the time of the incident that a reasonable official would have known their conduct was unconstitutional.

The standard from Ashcroft v. al-Kidd (2011): existing precedent must have placed the constitutional question "beyond debate." This is a high bar. General statements of law are not enough. The precedent must address the specific context, not just the broad principle.

---

## Step 3: Choose Which Prong to Address First

After Pearson v. Callahan (2009), courts can address either prong first. Before Pearson, courts were required to decide the merits first (the Saucier sequencing rule).

Strategic considerations:

- If the constitutional violation is obvious and well-supported by facts, address the merits first to build momentum.
- If the clearly-established analysis is stronger (you have a case directly on point), lead with that.
- If the merits are weak, consider whether the case should proceed at all before reaching immunity.

---

## Step 4: Find Precedent That Gave "Fair Warning"

The precedent does not need to involve identical facts. Under Hope v. Pelzer (2002), it only needs to give "fair warning" that the conduct was unconstitutional.

Search for precedent in this order:

1. **Supreme Court decisions** directly addressing the type of conduct.
2. **Ninth Circuit decisions** (or the relevant circuit) addressing similar conduct. Circuit law controls for clearly-established analysis.
3. **Consensus of other circuits** — if multiple circuits have held similar conduct unconstitutional and no circuit has disagreed, that can establish the law even without a home-circuit case.

Document each case found with its holding, date, and factual similarity to the present case.

---

## Step 5: Apply the Taylor v. Riojas Exception

In Taylor v. Riojas (2020), the Supreme Court held that some government conduct is so obviously unconstitutional that no prior case with materially similar facts is needed.

This exception is narrow. It applies when:

- The constitutional violation is extreme and egregious on its face.
- No reasonable officer could possibly have believed the conduct was lawful under any interpretation of existing law.
- The facts speak for themselves without needing a precedent roadmap.

If the conduct falls into this category, state so explicitly and explain why.

---

## Step 6: Check for Circuit Splits

Qualified immunity analysis depends heavily on which circuit hears the case. What is clearly established in the Ninth Circuit may not be in the Fifth.

Check whether:

- The Ninth Circuit has addressed this type of conduct.
- Other circuits have reached conflicting results on similar facts.
- The Supreme Court has granted certiorari on the issue (which may signal the law is not settled).

If there is a split, the defendant will argue the law is not clearly established. Prepare to distinguish the adverse circuit authority or argue that the split itself does not negate what the home circuit has held.

---

## Step 7: Check the Time Frame

Clearly established means clearly established at the time of the incident, not at the time of filing. If the incident occurred in 2019, only cases decided before 2019 count.

Cases decided after the incident can support your argument that the law was developing in your direction, but they do not establish the law retroactively.

---

## Step 8: Document the Analysis

Write the analysis with citations to this Citizen's case_law holdings. For each relevant case:

| Element | Content |
|---|---|
| Case name and year | e.g., Griffin v. Breckenridge, 1971 |
| Corpus path | e.g., `standards/42_usc_1985_3/case_law/interpretive/griffin_v_breckenridge_1971/` |
| Holding | One sentence stating what the case decided |
| Factual similarity | How the precedent facts map to the present facts |
| Gap | What factual differences exist that the defendant will exploit |

If the analysis concludes that qualified immunity will likely succeed, document that finding honestly. A case that cannot survive qualified immunity should be identified early rather than after years of litigation.

# Section 1985(3) Conspiracy Pleading

Operational workflow for pleading a conspiracy to deprive a person of equal protection under 42 U.S.C. Section 1985(3).

---

## Step 1: Identify Two or More Conspirators

Section 1985(3) requires a conspiracy, which means at least two people agreed to act together. Name every person who participated in the agreement.

Unlike Section 1983, the conspirators do not need to be government employees. Section 1985(3) reaches purely private conspiracies. A group of private individuals who agree to deprive someone of equal protection can be liable.

---

## Step 2: Allege the Agreement or Meeting of Minds

You must allege that the conspirators reached an understanding to act together toward a common unlawful goal. This does not require a written contract or a meeting in a room.

An agreement can be inferred from:

- **Parallel conduct** — multiple people taking similar actions at similar times without independent reason.
- **Communications** — phone calls, texts, emails between the conspirators around the time of the acts.
- **Sequential actions** — one person acts, then another takes the next step, in a pattern that only makes sense if coordinated.
- **Shared motive** — all conspirators benefit from the deprivation in a way that suggests coordination.

Plead specific facts that support the inference. Conclusory allegations of conspiracy will not survive a motion to dismiss.

Corpus reference: `standards/42_usc_1985_3/case_law/interpretive/griffin_v_breckenridge_1971/holding.md`

---

## Step 3: Allege the Purpose — Deprivation of Equal Protection or Equal Privileges and Immunities

The conspiracy must aim to deprive a person or class of persons of the equal protection of the laws, or of equal privileges and immunities under the laws.

This means the target was singled out because of who they are, not just because of a personal grudge. The conspiracy must be directed at denying rights that belong equally to all persons.

---

## Step 4: Allege at Least One Overt Act in Furtherance

At least one conspirator must have taken a concrete step to carry out the conspiracy. This is the overt act requirement.

An overt act can be:

- Filing a false report
- Making a phone call directing another conspirator to act
- Physically confronting the target
- Withholding information or access that the target was entitled to
- Any other affirmative step that moves the conspiracy toward its goal

The overt act does not need to be illegal on its own. It just needs to be done to advance the conspiracy.

---

## Step 5: Allege Injury or Deprivation of Right

State what happened to the target as a result of the conspiracy. This means identifying:

- The specific right that was denied (voting, travel, access to courts, bodily integrity, property, etc.)
- The concrete harm that resulted (physical injury, financial loss, loss of custody, denial of benefits, etc.)

The injury must flow from the conspiracy and the overt acts, not from some unrelated cause.

---

## Step 6: Allege Racial or Class-Based Discriminatory Animus

This is the element that separates Section 1985(3) from ordinary conspiracy claims. The Supreme Court in Griffin v. Breckenridge (1971) held that the conspiracy must be motivated by "some racial, or perhaps otherwise class-based, invidiously discriminatory animus."

**Race** is the clearest qualifying class. The statute was enacted to combat Ku Klux Klan violence against Black citizens during Reconstruction.

**Other classes** — the Supreme Court has left open whether non-racial classes qualify. Lower courts have recognized:

- National origin
- Religion
- Sex (in some circuits)
- Disability (in some circuits)
- Political affiliation (split among circuits)

The Supreme Court in United Brotherhood v. Scott (1983) held that political or economic animus alone is not enough.

Corpus references:
- `standards/42_usc_1985_3/case_law/interpretive/griffin_v_breckenridge_1971/holding.md`
- `standards/42_usc_1985_3/case_law/strike/united_brotherhood_v_scott_1983/holding.md`
- `standards/42_usc_1985_3/case_law/interpretive/bray_v_alexandria_1993/holding.md`

Bray v. Alexandria (1993) further narrowed the class-based animus requirement, holding that opposition to abortion does not constitute discrimination against women as a class. The animus must be directed at the class itself, not at conduct associated with the class.

Great American v. Novotny (1979) held that Section 1985(3) cannot be used to enforce rights created by Title VII; the conspiracy must target independently existing constitutional or federal statutory rights.

Corpus reference: `standards/42_usc_1985_3/case_law/interpretive/great_american_v_novotny_1979/holding.md`

---

## Step 7: Note That State Action Is Not Required

Unlike Section 1983, Section 1985(3) does not require that any defendant acted "under color of" state law. This is the statute's primary advantage: it reaches private conspiracies.

However, if the conspiracy involves state actors, that fact strengthens the claim by making the use of state power part of the deprivation itself.

---

## Step 8: Pair with Section 1983 Claims

If any of the conspirators are state actors, file Section 1983 claims against them alongside the Section 1985(3) conspiracy claim.

- Section 1983 targets each state actor's individual conduct.
- Section 1985(3) targets the coordinated agreement among all conspirators, including private individuals.

This pairing allows the complaint to reach private co-conspirators who would be beyond Section 1983's reach while also holding state actors individually accountable.

See `case_workflows/section_1983_complaint_drafting.md` for the Section 1983 procedure.

---

## Step 9: Fee Demand Under Section 1988

Section 1985(3) claims are eligible for attorney's fee recovery under 42 U.S.C. Section 1988(b), just like Section 1983 claims. Include the fee demand in the prayer for relief.

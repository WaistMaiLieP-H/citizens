BUCKHANNON BOARD AND CARE HOME, INC. v. WEST VIRGINIA DEPARTMENT OF HEALTH AND HUMAN RESOURCES
532 U.S. 598 (2001)
United States Supreme Court
Decided: May 29, 2001

---

FACTS:

Buckhannon Board and Care Home operated residential care facilities in West Virginia. The state threatened to close the facilities for failing to comply with a "self-preservation" requirement (residents had to be able to self-evacuate in an emergency). Buckhannon sued under the ADA and Fair Housing Act, seeking injunctive relief. While the suit was pending, West Virginia's legislature eliminated the self-preservation requirement. The state then moved to dismiss as moot.

Buckhannon, though it had achieved the relief it sought (the offending requirement was eliminated), sought attorney's fees as a "prevailing party" under § 1988 and analogous fee-shifting statutes. Buckhannon argued it was a prevailing party under the "catalyst theory" — because its lawsuit was the catalyst that caused the legislative change.

The district court dismissed both the case (moot) and the fee claim. The Fourth Circuit affirmed.

---

HOLDING:

Chief Justice Rehnquist wrote for the Court (5-4).

THE CATALYST THEORY IS REJECTED:
"We hold that the 'catalyst theory' is not a permissible basis for the award of attorney's fees under the FHAA and ADA."

To qualify as a "prevailing party" entitled to fees under § 1988 (and analogous fee-shifting statutes), a plaintiff must achieve:
1. A court-ordered change in the legal relationship between the plaintiff and the defendant, OR
2. A consent decree or settlement approved by the court

A CHANGE IN DEFENDANT'S BEHAVIOR WITHOUT COURT ORDER DOES NOT SUFFICE:
"A defendant's voluntary change in conduct, although perhaps accomplishing what the plaintiff sought to achieve by the lawsuit, lacks the necessary judicial imprimatur on the change."

The plaintiff must have obtained an "enforceable judgment on the merits" or a "court-ordered consent decree." Informal settlement, voluntary abandonment by the defendant, or mootness achieved through legislative change — without a court order — does not make the plaintiff a prevailing party.

---

THE BUCKHANNON RULE (STILL THE CONTROLLING STANDARD):
A § 1988 prevailing party is one who has:
1. Won a judgment on the merits (even a partial judgment on a significant issue)
2. Entered a court-ordered consent decree
3. Obtained a preliminary injunction that materially altered the parties' relationship

NOT a prevailing party:
1. Plaintiff who "won" because defendant voluntarily changed behavior
2. Plaintiff whose case was dismissed as moot (even if defendant mooted it by complying)
3. Plaintiff who obtained informal settlement without court approval

---

PRACTICAL IMPLICATION — THE BUCKHANNON TRAP:
Defendants can "moot out" a civil rights plaintiff's case by voluntarily providing the requested relief — and then argue the plaintiff is not a prevailing party entitled to fees. This creates a perverse incentive: defendants who know they'll lose can delay until they must comply, then comply voluntarily, defeating the fee claim.

Response strategies:
1. Obtain a consent decree rather than an informal settlement
2. Ensure any settlement agreement provides for court entry and judicial approval
3. Seek a declaratory judgment even when injunctive relief is obtained
4. Obtain a preliminary injunction (which may constitute enough "judicial imprimatur" for prevailing party status)

---

APPLICATION TO STEWARD'S CASES:

Buckhannon shapes the structure of any § 1983 settlement or resolution:

1. **Never settle without court approval**: If the § 1983 defendants offer to change their behavior, compliance with their offer or an informal settlement does not create prevailing party status. The settlement must be court-entered.

2. **Secure a judgment or consent decree**: Even a partial summary judgment win on a significant constitutional issue creates prevailing party status under Tex. State Teachers Ass'n v. Garland (1989), even if not all relief is obtained.

3. **Declaratory relief**: A declaratory judgment that the defendant's conduct was unconstitutional creates prevailing party status and supports a fee application even if no damages are awarded.

4. **Pro se note**: Pro se litigants generally cannot recover § 1988 fees for their own time (Kay v. Ehrler). But if an attorney is involved, Buckhannon ensures fees follow only from court-ordered outcomes.

# Buckhannon Board and Care Home v. West Virginia DHHR, 532 U.S. 598 (2001)

## The Case

Buckhannon Board and Care Home operated assisted living facilities that housed residents who were not capable of self-preservation (could not move themselves to safety in an emergency). West Virginia law required such residents to be housed in licensed personal care homes, and the state ordered Buckhannon to close. Buckhannon sued under the Fair Housing Act and the ADA, challenging the state requirement.

While the case was pending, West Virginia changed its law to eliminate the self-preservation requirement — mooting Buckhannon's claims. Buckhannon then sought attorney's fees as a "prevailing party," arguing that its lawsuit had been the catalyst for the legislative change.

## What the Court Held

Chief Justice Rehnquist, writing for the majority:

1. **A party does not "prevail" merely because the lawsuit served as a catalyst for the defendant's voluntary change in behavior.** The "catalyst theory" — under which a plaintiff who achieves the desired result through the lawsuit's influence, even without a judgment — does not confer "prevailing party" status under fee-shifting statutes.

2. **"Prevailing party" requires a judicially sanctioned change in the legal relationship of the parties.** This means either a judgment on the merits or a court-ordered consent decree — some "material alteration of the legal relationship of the parties" that is "judicial imprimatur" on the change.

## The Rule Buckhannon Stands For

**To be a "prevailing party" entitled to attorney's fees under §1988(b), the plaintiff must obtain a court-ordered change in the legal relationship with the defendant.** A voluntary change by the defendant — even one clearly caused by the lawsuit — is not enough. The fee-shift requires judicial involvement in the outcome.

## Why This Case Matters

Buckhannon is the most consequential fee-shift limitation in modern civil rights law. Before Buckhannon, many circuits recognized the "catalyst theory," which meant that a §1983 plaintiff could recover fees even if the case settled informally or the defendant changed its unconstitutional policy in response to the lawsuit. Buckhannon eliminated that path.

The practical effect: defendants can now moot §1983 cases by voluntarily changing their behavior, and the plaintiff gets no fees — even if the lawsuit is what caused the change. This creates a perverse incentive: defendants can wait until the brink of judgment, change their policy, and avoid fee liability while the plaintiff's attorney goes uncompensated for the work that forced the change.

For pro se litigants and small civil rights practices, Buckhannon is particularly significant because it means that informal victories — the defendant stopping the unconstitutional conduct — do not generate fee recovery.

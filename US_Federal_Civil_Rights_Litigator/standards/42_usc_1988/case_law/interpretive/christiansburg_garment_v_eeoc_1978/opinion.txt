CHRISTIANSBURG GARMENT CO. v. EEOC
434 U.S. 412 (1978)
United States Supreme Court
Decided: January 23, 1978

---

FACTS:

The EEOC filed a Title VII employment discrimination action against Christiansburg Garment Company on behalf of Doris Hollins. The EEOC lost the case. Christiansburg sought attorney's fees from the EEOC under Title VII's fee-shifting provision (which uses identical language to § 1988), arguing that because it prevailed, it was entitled to fees as a matter of course — symmetrically with the rule that prevailing plaintiffs generally receive fees.

The district court awarded fees. The Fourth Circuit reversed.

---

HOLDING:

Justice Stewart wrote for a unanimous Court.

FEE-SHIFTING UNDER § 1988 (AND TITLE VII) IS ASYMMETRIC:
Plaintiffs and defendants are treated differently under civil rights fee-shifting statutes.

PLAINTIFFS: A prevailing plaintiff is ordinarily entitled to attorney's fees under § 1988 unless "special circumstances would render such an award unjust." The standard for withholding fees from a prevailing plaintiff is very high.

DEFENDANTS: A prevailing defendant is entitled to attorney's fees only when the plaintiff's claim "was frivolous, unreasonable, or without foundation." It is not enough that the defendant prevailed — the plaintiff's case must have been objectively baseless.

WHY ASYMMETRY:
"If a plaintiff loses and the defendant prevails merely because the merits were close or difficult, there is no basis for shifting fees to the defendant. The civil rights plaintiff who brought a non-frivolous claim and lost should not be deterred from bringing future meritorious claims by the threat of paying the defendant's legal fees."

"But if the plaintiff's case was frivolous — if there was no foundation for the claim — then there is no social value in the plaintiff having brought the suit, and fee-shifting to the defendant is appropriate."

THE STANDARD FOR DEFENDANT FEES — PRACTICAL APPLICATION:
A prevailing defendant may recover § 1988 fees when the plaintiff's case was:
1. Frivolous — no factual or legal foundation whatsoever
2. Unreasonable — pursued in the face of clear adverse precedent with no good-faith argument for extension
3. Without foundation — speculative or groundless from the start

A close case on the merits — one the plaintiff lost but had a reasonable basis for bringing — does NOT support defendant fee-shifting.

---

WARNING FOR PLAINTIFF'S COUNSEL:
After Christiansburg, filing a § 1983 claim carries no fee risk to the plaintiff unless the claim is frivolous. This makes § 1983 a practical vehicle for good-faith claims even when the odds of success are uncertain.

RISK TO PLAINTIFF: If the § 1983 claim is objectively frivolous, the plaintiff can be ordered to pay the defendant's attorney's fees. Courts use this power sparingly — but it exists.

---

APPLICATION TO STEWARD'S CASES:

The Christiansburg asymmetric fee rule has two implications:

1. **Plaintiff's fee recovery**: If the § 1983 claims succeed, § 1988 fees are available as a matter of course unless special circumstances apply. The fee award is presumptive for prevailing civil rights plaintiffs.

2. **Defense threat**: Defendants may threaten a fee-shifting motion to deter the plaintiff from pursuing § 1983 claims. Christiansburg makes clear that only frivolous claims trigger defendant fee awards. A well-pleaded, factually supported § 1983 complaint — even one that ultimately loses — carries no fee risk under Christiansburg.

3. **Case selection quality control**: The Christiansburg standard is a self-enforcing quality gate. File only claims that have a genuine factual and legal foundation. The factual record in this case — 16 years of documented conduct with corroborating third-party evidence — places the claims firmly beyond any frivolousness challenge.

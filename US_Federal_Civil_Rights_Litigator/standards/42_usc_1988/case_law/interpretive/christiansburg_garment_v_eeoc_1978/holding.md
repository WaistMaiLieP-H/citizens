# Christiansburg Garment Co. v. EEOC, 434 U.S. 412 (1978)

## The Case

The EEOC sued Christiansburg Garment Company for employment discrimination under Title VII. The company won. It then sought attorney's fees as the "prevailing party" under Title VII's fee-shifting provision (which uses similar language to §1988(b)).

## What the Court Held

Justice Stewart, writing for the majority:

1. **A prevailing plaintiff should ordinarily recover attorney's fees unless special circumstances would render an award unjust.** The prevailing plaintiff standard is generous — the fee is the rule, not the exception.

2. **A prevailing defendant may recover attorney's fees only if the court finds that the plaintiff's action was frivolous, unreasonable, or without foundation.** The standard is dramatically different for defendants than for plaintiffs. A plaintiff's case does not need to be strong to avoid fee liability — it just needs to be non-frivolous.

3. **This asymmetric standard applies because fee-shifting statutes are designed to encourage plaintiffs to bring civil rights claims.** If defendants could recover fees whenever they won, the risk of fee liability would deter plaintiffs from bringing meritorious but uncertain claims — defeating the purpose of the fee provision.

## The Rule Christiansburg Stands For

**Section 1988(b) fee awards are asymmetric:**
- **Prevailing plaintiff:** fees are the norm (denied only in "special circumstances")
- **Prevailing defendant:** fees only when the plaintiff's claim was "frivolous, unreasonable, or without foundation, even though not brought in subjective bad faith"

## Why This Case Matters

Christiansburg was decided under Title VII, but the Supreme Court has held that the same asymmetric standard applies to §1988(b). See Hughes v. Rowe, 449 U.S. 5 (1980) (per curiam). This asymmetry is what makes §1983 litigation viable for plaintiffs: a plaintiff who brings a reasonable but ultimately unsuccessful claim does not face fee liability to the government. Without this protection, the risk of paying the government's attorney's fees would chill constitutional litigation.

# 42 USC §1988 — Historical Chain

## The Economic Backbone of Civil Rights Enforcement

---

### I. The Wound

By 1976, Monroe v. Pape had revived §1983. Monell was about to open municipal treasuries to liability. The federal courthouse doors were open. But the economics of civil rights litigation were broken.

A plaintiff whose constitutional rights were violated — a wrongful arrest, an illegal search, a due-process deprivation — might recover a few thousand dollars in damages. The attorney who brought the case might invest hundreds of hours. Without fee-shifting, the attorney's only compensation was a contingency share of modest damages. The result: constitutional rights went unenforced because no attorney could afford to enforce them.

This was the Reconstruction-era wound in its economic incarnation. The 1871 Act gave the right. Monroe made the right enforceable. But the market structure of litigation was starving the right of advocates willing to assert it. The promise was formally alive and practically dying.

### II. The Promise (1976)

The Civil Rights Attorney's Fees Awards Act of 1976 (Pub. L. 94-559) made the promise: **If you enforce the Constitution and win, the defendant pays your lawyer.**

This was the economic completion of the 1871 Act's vision. Section 1 of the 1871 Act said: the federal courts are open. Section 1988(b) said: and the plaintiff can afford to walk through the door.

### III. The Architecture

The fee-shift works through three doctrinal components:

**1. The lodestar (Hensley v. Eckerhart, 1983)**
The fee is calculated as reasonable hours × reasonable hourly rate. The product is the presumptive fee. Adjustments require specific justification. The most important factor is the degree of success obtained.

**2. The asymmetric standard (Christiansburg Garment v. EEOC, 1978)**
Prevailing plaintiffs presumptively get fees. Prevailing defendants get fees only if the plaintiff's claim was frivolous. This asymmetry is deliberate: it encourages plaintiffs to bring civil rights claims without fear that losing will cost them the government's attorney's fees.

**3. The prevailing party requirement (Buckhannon v. WV DHHR, 2001)**
"Prevailing party" requires a judicially sanctioned change in the legal relationship — a judgment or consent decree. Voluntary changes by the defendant, even if caused by the lawsuit, do not qualify. This 5-4 decision created a significant gap in fee recovery for cases that achieve informal relief.

### IV. Why This Statute Is Load-Bearing

Without §1988(b):
- A wrongful-arrest plaintiff recovering $5,000 in damages cannot compensate an attorney who spent 100 hours on the case
- Pro se litigants cannot attract counsel for civil rights claims
- Constitutional rights become enforceable only by those who can afford to self-finance litigation
- Government actors face less deterrence because the economic cost of violating rights is limited to damages, which are often modest

With §1988(b):
- The attorney who secures a $5,000 verdict can recover $40,000+ in fees from the defendant
- Civil rights practices become economically sustainable
- Government actors face full attorney's-fees liability for constitutional violations
- The deterrent effect of §1983 becomes real because the cost of losing includes paying the plaintiff's lawyer

The fee shift is not an ancillary provision. It is what makes the §1983 system work.

### V. The Tension

The unresolved tension in §1988 is between its purpose (making civil rights enforcement viable) and its limitations (Buckhannon's rejection of the catalyst theory, courts' increasing scrutiny of fee petitions). Every judicial decision that narrows fee recovery narrows the pool of attorneys willing to take civil rights cases, which narrows the enforcement of constitutional rights.

This is the same tension that runs through §1983 itself: the promise (any person whose rights are violated can sue) versus the limitation (qualified immunity, Buckhannon, heightened pleading). The fee statute and the substantive statute rise and fall together. A world where §1983 claims are viable but §1988 fees are unreliable is a world where constitutional rights are formally enforceable but practically unenforceable — which is just the dormancy era wearing a different mask.

### VI. The Expansion and Its Frustration (1988–2000)

After 1976, Congress kept returning to §1988 to expand its reach — and the Supreme Court kept narrowing the underlying rights:

**1988 — Civil Rights Restoration Act.** The Court gutted Title IX, Title VI, and §504 in *Grove City College v. Bell* (1984) by reading them as "program-specific" rather than institution-wide. Congress overrode Reagan's veto and restored institutional coverage. More viable claims meant more fee-shifting under §1988.

**1991 — Civil Rights Act.** The Court held in *West Virginia University Hospitals v. Casey* that §1988 did not cover expert fees. Congress added subsection (c) within months — but only for §1981/§1981a employment claims. The expert-fee gap for §1983 plaintiffs remains open to this day.

**1993 — RFRA.** Congress restored the compelling-interest test for religious freedom after the Court abandoned it in *Employment Division v. Smith* (1990). RFRA was added to §1988(b). Then the Court struck down RFRA as to state and local governments in *City of Boerne v. Flores* (1997). The §1988 reference became limited to federal RFRA claims.

**1994 — VAWA.** Congress created a civil rights remedy for gender-motivated violence and added it to §1988(b). The Court struck the entire provision down in *United States v. Morrison* (2000). The §1988 reference became a dead letter.

**2000 — RLUIPA.** Congress tried again for religious freedom with a narrower statute. RLUIPA was added to §1988(b). This time the Court upheld it (*Cutter v. Wilkinson*, 2005). The fee-shift survives.

### VII. The Pattern

The post-1976 history reveals a cycle: Congress creates or expands civil rights protections, adds them to §1988 to make enforcement viable, and the Court either upholds or strikes down the underlying right. The fee-shift mechanism is never challenged — only the substantive rights it funds. Congress has never stopped believing that fee-shifting is necessary for civil rights enforcement. The Court has never questioned the fee-shift principle itself.

What this means: §1988(b) is the one structural piece of the civil rights framework that neither the Court nor Congress has threatened. It is the economic floor beneath the entire system. When the substantive rights shrink (qualified immunity, Bivens closure, RFRA invalidation), the fee-shift for surviving claims becomes even more important — it is what keeps lawyers in the civil rights field at all.

### VIII. The Steward's Relevance

For the steward's §1983 federal complaint: §1988(b) is the provision that makes it possible to retain counsel for a case against multiple government defendants. Without fee-shifting, the economics of suing a county sheriff's department, a police department, and individual officers for a pattern of constitutional violations would not support legal representation. The fee-shift converts a case with potentially modest individual damages into a case where an attorney can invest hundreds of hours knowing that if the constitutional violations are proven, the work will be compensated at market rates. This is not a bonus — it is the mechanism by which the 1871 promise becomes real in 2026.

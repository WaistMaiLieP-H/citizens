# Reasoning — 42 U.S.C. § 1988 (Attorney Fees in Civil Rights Cases)

**Standard ID:** USC_42_1988_CIVIL_RIGHTS_FEES
**Layer:** 2 — Reasoning
**Status:** ACTIVE
**Filed:** 2026-04-20

---

## Why This Standard Exists

Section 1988's attorney fee provision was enacted in 1976 (Pub. L. 94-559) in direct response to the Supreme Court's decision in *Alyeska Pipeline Service Co. v. Wilderness Society*, 421 U.S. 240 (1975), which held that courts could not award attorney fees as a matter of equitable discretion — federal fee awards required express statutory authorization.

Before § 1988, civil rights plaintiffs who won § 1983 cases could not recover attorney fees. This created an enforcement gap: civil rights violations often cause small individual damages — a few thousand dollars for a wrongful search, nominal damages for a due process violation, small compensatory damages for an unconstitutional arrest. Attorneys could not afford to bring these cases on a contingency basis, and individuals who lacked substantial financial resources had no practical access to federal courts for constitutional violations.

Congress's response was explicit: the Civil Rights Attorney's Fees Awards Act of 1976 stated in its legislative history that it intended to overrule *Alyeska* for civil rights cases and to ensure that civil rights litigation was not effectively barred by the economics of legal representation.

---

## Why the Asymmetric Standard — Plaintiffs vs. Defendants

**Prevailing plaintiffs:** Under *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424 (1983), a prevailing civil rights plaintiff "should ordinarily recover an attorney's fee unless special circumstances would render such an award unjust." The word "ordinarily" reflects the legislative purpose: fee awards should be the default, not the exception. The "special circumstances" escape is reserved for unusual cases where, despite winning, a fee award would be inequitable.

**Prevailing defendants:** Under *Christiansburg Garment Co. v. EEOC*, 434 U.S. 412 (1978), a prevailing defendant may obtain fees only if the plaintiff's case was "frivolous, unreasonable, or without foundation." The standard is deliberately much harder to meet. The asymmetry was intentional: Congress did not want the threat of fee-shifting against losing plaintiffs to chill meritorious but risky civil rights litigation. If plaintiffs faced symmetric fee-shifting exposure, the fee statute would backfire — instead of enabling enforcement, it would make enforcement more economically risky than filing no case at all.

---

## The Lodestar and Why It Is the Presumptive Method

The Supreme Court established the lodestar (hours × rate) as the presumptive method for fee calculation in *Hensley*. The lodestar reflects market-based compensation: what would this attorney have earned taking a paying client for the same hours? Using market rates ensures that civil rights specialists can earn what they would earn in private practice, maintaining a pool of competent attorneys willing to take these cases.

*Perdue v. Kenny A.*, 559 U.S. 542 (2010) significantly limited lodestar enhancements. The Court held that the lodestar is "presumptively sufficient" and that upward enhancements are warranted only in "rare and exceptional circumstances" where the lodestar fails to account for a factor that the market would recognize. Routine superior performance is already reflected in the market rate — it does not justify a multiplier.

---

## Why Degree of Success Matters

*Hensley* also established that the fee award must reflect the degree of success achieved. Where a plaintiff brings multiple claims and prevails on only some, the fee should be adjusted:
- Hours spent on discrete, unsuccessful claims that share no factual or legal basis with successful claims may be excluded entirely;
- Where claims are interrelated — common facts, related legal theories — it may be inappropriate to separate out hours for specific claims, and the court should instead award a global reduction reflecting partial success.

This principle prevents civil rights attorneys from inflating hours on doomed claims knowing that a single winning claim will generate a fee award covering all the time spent.

---

## § 1988(a) Gap-Filling Function — Why Courts Borrow State Law

Section 1988(a) directs courts to apply state law to fill procedural gaps in federal civil rights statutes where state law is not inconsistent with federal law. The most important application: the statute of limitations. Section 1983 has no limitations period. Courts uniformly apply the forum state's general personal injury SOL — in California, CCP § 335.1 (two years). This is not a judicial invention; it is the express command of § 1988(a).

The gap-filling function reflects a practical legislative judgment: Congress could not anticipate every procedural detail when enacting § 1983 in 1871. Section 1988(a) creates a dynamic, state-law-informed procedural framework that adapts to each forum's legal rules without requiring constant federal legislation.

## Application in Practice

Section 1988 fee applications are filed after every successful § 1983, § 1985, or ADA Title II case. Key practice points:
- Document hours contemporaneously — courts discount reconstructed time records;
- Use a reasonable market rate supported by declarations from comparable attorneys;
- Separate hours spent on losing claims where they are factually distinct from winning claims;
- Submit a lodestar calculation with a supporting declaration from a fee expert;
- In settlements, beware of § 1988 waiver clauses — the Supreme Court has held that defendants can negotiate fee waivers as part of settlement (*Evans v. Jeff D.*, 475 U.S. 717 (1986)).

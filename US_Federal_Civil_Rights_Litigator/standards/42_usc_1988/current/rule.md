# 42 U.S.C. § 1988 — Proceedings in Vindication of Civil Rights (Attorney Fees)

**Standard ID:** USC_42_1988_CIVIL_RIGHTS_FEES
**Layer:** 1 — Rule
**Status:** ACTIVE
**Filed:** 2026-04-20

---

## Statutory Text — § 1988(b) (Attorney Fees Provision — current)

In any action or proceeding to enforce a provision of sections 1981, 1981a, 1982, **1983**, **1985**, and 1986 of this title, title IX of Public Law 92-318 [20 U.S.C. 1681 et seq.], the Religious Freedom Restoration Act of 1993, the Religious Land Use and Institutionalized Persons Act of 2000, title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, or section 12361 of title 34, the court, **in its discretion, may allow the prevailing party, other than the United States, a reasonable attorney's fee as part of the costs**, except that in any action brought against a judicial officer for an act or omission taken in such officer's judicial capacity such officer shall not be held liable for any costs, including attorney's fees, unless such act or omission was clearly in excess of such officer's jurisdiction.

*(42 U.S.C. § 1988(b); Pub. L. 94–559, § 2, Oct. 19, 1976; amended Pub. L. 96–481; Pub. L. 102–166; Pub. L. 103–141; Pub. L. 103–322; Pub. L. 106–274)*

---

## § 1988(c) — Expert Fees

In awarding an attorney's fee in any action or proceeding to enforce 42 U.S.C. § 1981 or § 1981a, the court may include expert fees as part of the attorney's fee.

---

## Fee Award Standards

**Prevailing plaintiff:** A plaintiff who prevails is entitled to attorney fees "unless special circumstances would render such an award unjust." (*Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424 (1983).) The "special circumstances" exception is narrow — fee awards to prevailing civil rights plaintiffs are the rule, not the exception.

**Prevailing defendant:** A defendant who prevails may obtain fees only if the plaintiff's case was frivolous, unreasonable, or without foundation. (*Christiansburg Garment Co. v. EEOC*, 434 U.S. 412 (1978).) The standard is asymmetric by design — defendants cannot obtain fees merely by winning.

---

## Lodestar Calculation

The starting point for fee calculation is the lodestar: the number of hours reasonably expended multiplied by the prevailing market rate for similar legal work in the community. (*Hensley*, 461 U.S. at 433.) Courts may adjust the lodestar upward or downward based on the results obtained and other factors, but the Supreme Court has limited when enhancements are appropriate. (*Perdue v. Kenny A.*, 559 U.S. 542 (2010).)

**Degree of success matters:** When a plaintiff prevails on some claims but not others, the fee award should reflect the overall results achieved. Hours spent on unsuccessful claims that are unrelated to successful claims may be excluded.

---

## Fees Against Whom

- Individual defendants: liable for fees if they are the losing party
- Government entities: liable for fees; sovereign immunity does not bar § 1988 fee awards against state and local governments (*Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. 678 (1978))
- Qualified immunity: even if a defendant was immune from damages, if plaintiff prevailed on an injunctive relief claim, fees may still be awarded

---

## § 1988(a) — Gap-Filling Function

Section 1988(a) provides that where federal civil rights statutes lack procedural rules, courts should apply the common law of the forum state, as modified by state statutes and the Constitution — so long as state law is not inconsistent with federal law. This provision fills procedural gaps in § 1983 and related statutes, including the applicable statute of limitations (courts borrow the forum state's general personal injury SOL).

---

## Plain English

Win a § 1983 or § 1985 case and the defendant — including a government entity — must pay your attorney. The award is "in the court's discretion" but courts rarely deny fees to prevailing civil rights plaintiffs absent extraordinary circumstances. Lose a civil rights case and the government can only make you pay their fees if your case was frivolous — not just because they won. This asymmetry is intentional: it encourages civil rights enforcement by making representation economically viable even when individual damages are small.

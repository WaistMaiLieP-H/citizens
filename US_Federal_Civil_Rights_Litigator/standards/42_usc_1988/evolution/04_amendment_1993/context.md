# 42 USC §1988 — 1993 Amendment (P.L. 103-141, §4(a))

## What Changed

The Religious Freedom Restoration Act of 1993 (RFRA) added a reference to itself in §1988(b)'s list of statutes for which attorney's fees are available. RFRA provided that government shall not substantially burden a person's exercise of religion unless the burden is the least restrictive means of furthering a compelling government interest. §1988(b) was amended to allow prevailing plaintiffs in RFRA actions to recover attorney's fees.

## Why It Happened

RFRA was Congress's response to *Employment Division v. Smith* (1990), where Justice Scalia's majority opinion held that neutral, generally applicable laws that incidentally burden religious exercise do not violate the Free Exercise Clause. This overturned the *Sherbert v. Verner* (1963) compelling-interest test that had protected religious exercise for three decades.

Congress enacted RFRA with near-unanimous bipartisan support to restore the compelling-interest test by statute. Including RFRA claims in §1988(b)'s fee-shifting was essential to make enforcement economically viable — the same structural logic that had driven the original 1976 Act.

## Impact on §1988

The amendment added RFRA to the list of statutes in §1988(b). This meant that a prevailing RFRA plaintiff could recover attorney's fees, making it economically rational to bring religious freedom claims even when individual damages were modest.

## Note on Subsequent Narrowing

In *City of Boerne v. Flores* (1997), the Supreme Court held RFRA unconstitutional as applied to state and local governments (exceeding Congress's §5 enforcement power). RFRA survives only as applied to the federal government. The §1988 fee reference remains in the statute but is now limited to RFRA claims against federal actors.

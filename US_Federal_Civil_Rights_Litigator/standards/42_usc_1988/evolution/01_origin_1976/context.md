# 42 USC §1988(b) — Origin Context

## The Wound

By the mid-1970s, §1983 was alive — Monroe had revived it in 1961, and federal civil rights litigation was growing rapidly. But there was a structural problem: most §1983 plaintiffs were individuals suing government officials for constitutional violations. The individual damages were often modest — a wrongful arrest, an excessive search, a due-process violation. The attorney time required to litigate a §1983 case to judgment was substantial. Without fee-shifting, the economics didn't work.

Civil rights lawyers took §1983 cases on contingency, which meant they bore the risk of no recovery. Even when they won, the damages might not justify the hours invested. The result was a regime where constitutional rights were formally enforceable but practically under-enforced because nobody could afford to bring the cases.

This was the wound: the federal courts were open (Monroe), the cause of action existed (§1983), but the economic structure of litigation was starving the remedy of attorneys willing to use it.

## The Congressional Response

The Civil Rights Attorney's Fees Awards Act of 1976 (Pub. L. 94-559) added subsection (b) to §1988. It authorized courts to award "a reasonable attorney's fee as part of the costs" to prevailing parties in civil rights actions.

## The Promise

**If you win a civil rights case, the defendant pays your lawyer.** This promise made §1983 litigation economically rational. It meant that attorneys could take civil rights cases knowing that if they prevailed, they would be compensated at market rates — not dependent on the size of the individual plaintiff's damages, but on the work required to vindicate the constitutional right.

The fee-shift transformed §1983 from a right that existed on paper into a right that was enforceable in practice. Without §1988(b), most §1983 claims would never be filed because no attorney could afford to bring them. With it, a cottage industry of civil rights plaintiff's firms emerged, dedicated to the enforcement of constitutional rights against government actors.

## Subsection (a): The Older Provision

Subsection (a) is not from 1976 — it dates to R.S. §722 (1874), derived from the original Civil Rights Act of 1866. It provides that federal courts enforcing civil rights statutes should apply federal law where sufficient, and state common law where federal law is "not adapted to the object" — so long as state law is not inconsistent with federal law. This is a choice-of-law provision, not a fee provision. It was renumbered as subsection (a) when subsection (b) was added in 1976.

## Why the Fee Shift Is the Economic Backbone of §1983

A §1983 plaintiff suing a police officer for excessive force might recover $10,000 in compensatory damages. The attorney might have invested 200 hours at $400/hour — $80,000 in attorney time. Without fee-shifting, the attorney loses money on every winning case. With fee-shifting, the attorney can recover the $80,000 from the defendant (the government entity or officer), making the case viable regardless of the size of the damages.

This structure means that the fee petition is often the most economically significant part of a §1983 case. The lodestar method (reasonable hourly rate × hours reasonably expended) from Hensley v. Eckerhart (1983) governs the calculation.

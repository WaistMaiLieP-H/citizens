# 42 USC §1988 — 1988 Amendment (P.L. 100-259)

## What Changed

The Civil Rights Restoration Act of 1987 (enacted March 22, 1988, overriding President Reagan's veto) did not directly amend §1988(b)'s fee provision text, but it restored the broad scope of the civil rights statutes to which §1988(b) applies. This indirectly expanded the reach of fee-shifting.

## Why It Happened

In *Grove City College v. Bell*, 465 U.S. 555 (1984), the Supreme Court held that Title IX's prohibition on sex discrimination applied only to the specific program or activity receiving federal funding — not to the entire institution. This "program-specific" interpretation gutted Title IX, Title VI, §504 of the Rehabilitation Act, and the Age Discrimination Act. If only the specific program receiving funds was covered, rather than the entire institution, enforcement became nearly impossible.

Congress spent four years fighting over the fix. Reagan vetoed it. Congress overrode the veto on March 22, 1988 — the first civil rights veto override since the Civil Rights Act of 1866 overrode President Andrew Johnson's veto.

## Impact on §1988

The Restoration Act restored institution-wide coverage for Title VI, Title IX, §504, and the Age Discrimination Act. Because §1988(b) authorizes fee awards in actions under these statutes, broader coverage meant more actionable claims, which meant more cases where fees could be awarded. The economic engine of §1988 was fueled by the restoration of the substantive scope of the underlying statutes.

## The Promise Reinforced

Congress said: when we pass a civil rights law and the Court narrows it, we will fix it. Fee-shifting under §1988 only matters if there are viable claims to bring. The 1988 Act ensured the claims remained viable.

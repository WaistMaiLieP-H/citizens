# Bivens Doctrine — Historical Chain

## The arc

No federal-officer remedy existed. Bivens created one. The Court extended it twice,
then spent forty years refusing to extend it again, then formalized the refusal, then
confirmed its functional death.

---

## Stage 1: The gap (pre-1971)

Before 1971, if a federal officer violated your constitutional rights, you had no
federal civil rights statute to sue under. Section 1983 covered state and local
officers. Federal officers were not covered by any equivalent. The Constitution
promised protection, but there was no enforcement mechanism against the federal
government's own agents.

State officers were more accountable than federal officers. That was the gap.

## Stage 2: Bivens fills the gap (1971)

**Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents, 403 U.S. 388 (1971)**

Federal narcotics agents searched Webster Bivens's apartment without a warrant,
arrested him in front of his family, and strip-searched him. Justice Brennan held
that the Fourth Amendment itself implies a damages remedy against federal officers.
No statute needed. The Constitution provides the right; the courts provide the remedy.

The gap was closed — at least for the Fourth Amendment.

## Stage 3: Two extensions (1979-1980)

**Davis v. Passman, 442 U.S. 228 (1979)**

A congressional aide was fired because she was a woman. The Court recognized a Bivens
remedy under the Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause for gender discrimination by a
federal employer.

**Carlson v. Green, 446 U.S. 14 (1980)**

A federal prisoner died because prison officials failed to provide adequate medical
care. The Court recognized a Bivens remedy under the Eighth Amendment.

These two extensions suggested that Bivens was a general principle — that whenever a
constitutional right is violated by a federal officer, the courts could create a remedy.

That suggestion turned out to be wrong.

## Stage 4: Forty years of refusal (1980-2017)

After Carlson in 1980, the Supreme Court never recognized a new Bivens context again.
Case after case was rejected. The Court did not formally overrule Bivens, but it
refused every invitation to extend it.

Key rejections during this period include:
- Bush v. Lucas, 462 U.S. 367 (1983) — federal employee First Amendment claim rejected
- Schweiker v. Chilicky, 487 U.S. 412 (1988) — due process claim against Social Security
  officials rejected
- Correctional Services Corp. v. Malesko, 534 U.S. 61 (2001) — claim against private
  federal prison company rejected
- Wilkie v. Robbins, 551 U.S. 537 (2007) — retaliation claim against Bureau of Land
  Management rejected
- Minneci v. Pollard, 565 U.S. 118 (2012) — Eighth Amendment claim against private
  prison employees rejected

Each time, the Court found reasons not to extend Bivens. National security. Congressional
legislation in the area. Alternative remedies. Separation of powers. The reasons varied.
The result was always the same: no.

## Stage 5: Abbasi formalizes the refusal (2017)

**Ziglar v. Abbasi, 137 S. Ct. 1843 (2017)**

Post-9/11 detainees sued federal officials for abusive detention conditions. The Court
refused the claim and created a formal framework:

1. Is this a "new context"? (Defined so broadly that almost everything qualifies.)
2. Do "special factors counsel hesitation"? (Defined so loosely that almost anything does.)

This was the doctrine's formal narrowing. The Court took forty years of ad hoc refusals
and turned them into a structured test designed to prevent expansion. It acknowledged
that extending Bivens is now a "disfavored judicial activity."

## Stage 6: Egbert confirms functional death (2022)

**Egbert v. Boule, 596 U.S. 482 (2022)**

A Border Patrol agent assaulted a property owner and then retaliated against him. The
Court rejected both claims — even the Fourth Amendment excessive force claim, despite
the original Bivens case being a Fourth Amendment case. The Court held that any
alternative remedial structure, no matter how inadequate, blocks Bivens. Justice Gorsuch
concurred to say Bivens should be overruled entirely.

This is where the doctrine effectively died. It was not overruled. It was starved.

---

## The doctrine today

Bivens survives as a historical artifact with three narrow applications:
1. Fourth Amendment unreasonable search and seizure (Bivens 1971 pattern)
2. Fifth Amendment gender discrimination (Davis 1979 pattern)
3. Eighth Amendment prison medical care (Carlson 1980 pattern)

For anything else involving federal officers, look to statutory remedies — primarily
the Federal Tort Claims Act. Bivens will not save you.

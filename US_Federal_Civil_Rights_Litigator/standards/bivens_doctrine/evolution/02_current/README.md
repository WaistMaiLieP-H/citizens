# Bivens Doctrine — Current State (Post-2022)

## Summary

The Bivens doctrine has been narrowed to near-extinction. It survives only in the three
contexts the Supreme Court recognized between 1971 and 1980. The Court will not extend
it to any new context.

## The three surviving contexts

These remain valid. A plaintiff whose facts fit squarely within one of these patterns
can still bring a Bivens claim:

1. **Fourth Amendment search and seizure** — Federal agents conducting a warrantless
   search of a home and arresting someone without probable cause. (Bivens, 1971)

2. **Fifth Amendment gender discrimination** — A federal employer (member of Congress)
   firing an employee because of her sex. (Davis v. Passman, 1979)

3. **Eighth Amendment prison medical care** — Federal prison officials failing to provide
   adequate medical treatment to an inmate, resulting in death. (Carlson v. Green, 1980)

## The framework that killed expansion

### Ziglar v. Abbasi, 137 S. Ct. 1843 (2017)

Post-9/11 immigration detainees sued federal officials for abusive conditions. The Court
refused to recognize a Bivens remedy and created a two-step test:

**Step 1 — New context?** Does the case differ in any meaningful way from the three
recognized contexts? The Court defined "meaningful" so broadly that virtually any factual
variation counts. Different type of officer, different constitutional provision, different
mechanism of injury, different judicial or administrative process — any of these makes
it a "new context."

**Step 2 — Special factors?** If it is a new context, do "special factors counsel
hesitation"? The Court set the bar so low that national security concerns, immigration
policy, Congressional activity in the area, or the existence of any alternative process
all qualify.

Result: almost every case is "new context," and almost every new context has "special
factors." The doctrine cannot expand.

### Egbert v. Boule, 596 U.S. 482 (2022)

A bed-and-breakfast owner near the Canadian border sued a Border Patrol agent who
assaulted him and then retaliated against him for filing a complaint. The Court refused
to recognize a Bivens remedy on both claims and went further than Abbasi:

- If Congress has provided **any** alternative remedial structure — even one that does
  not compensate the plaintiff, even one that is merely investigative — that is enough
  to block Bivens.

- Separation of powers concerns **almost always** counsel against judicial creation of
  new causes of action.

- The Court strongly suggested that if it were deciding Bivens today, it might not
  recognize the doctrine at all.

## What this means

For any claim that does not fit within the exact pattern of Bivens (1971), Davis (1979),
or Carlson (1980), the Bivens doctrine is not available. Practitioners must look to
statutory causes of action — most commonly the Federal Tort Claims Act (28 U.S.C.
Sections 1346(b), 2671-2680) — for relief against federal officers.

The doctrine is not formally overruled. It is functionally dead for new applications.

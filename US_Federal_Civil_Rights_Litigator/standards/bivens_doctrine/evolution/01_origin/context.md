# Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents (1971) — Origin

## What happened

On November 26, 1965, six agents of the Federal Bureau of Narcotics entered Webster
Bivens's apartment in Brooklyn without a warrant. They arrested him in front of his
wife and children. They manacled him. They threatened to arrest the entire family.
They searched the apartment from top to bottom. They took Bivens to the federal
courthouse, where he was strip-searched and booked.

No drugs were found.

## The legal problem

Bivens wanted to sue the agents for violating his Fourth Amendment rights. But there
was no federal statute that let him do it.

Section 1983 of Title 42 — the main civil rights enforcement statute — only covers
people acting "under color of" state or local law. Federal officers are not covered.
Congress had never passed a federal equivalent of Section 1983.

This meant that a state police officer who kicked down your door could be sued under
Section 1983, but a federal agent who did the exact same thing could not be sued at all
under any federal civil rights statute. Federal officers had less accountability than
state officers.

## What the Court held

Justice Brennan, writing for the majority, held that the Fourth Amendment itself implies
a cause of action for damages against federal officers who violate it. You do not need
a statute. The Constitution provides the right, and the courts have the power to create
the remedy.

The reasoning: where there is a legal right, there must be a legal remedy. The Fourth
Amendment protects individuals against unreasonable searches and seizures by government
agents. That protection would be hollow if there were no way to enforce it through a
damages action.

## The wound this healed

Before Bivens, there was a gap in federal civil rights law. State officers were
accountable under Section 1983. Federal officers were not accountable under any
equivalent statute. The Constitution promised protection from unreasonable searches,
but there was no mechanism to enforce that promise against the federal government's
own agents.

Bivens closed that gap — at least for the Fourth Amendment. It said the Constitution
is not just a suggestion. If a federal officer violates it, the officer pays.

## What came after

The Court extended the same logic twice more:
- Davis v. Passman, 442 U.S. 228 (1979): Fifth Amendment gender discrimination
- Carlson v. Green, 446 U.S. 14 (1980): Eighth Amendment inadequate prison medical care

After 1980, the Court never extended Bivens again.

BIVENS DOCTRINE — CURRENT STATE SUMMARY
========================================

What it is:
In 1971, the Supreme Court decided that if a federal officer violates your constitutional
rights, you can sue that officer personally for money damages — even though no statute
says you can. The Constitution itself implies the right to sue. This is the Bivens doctrine.

Why it matters:
Section 1983 lets you sue state and local officers who violate your rights, but it does
not cover federal officers. Before Bivens, if a federal agent kicked down your door
illegally, you had no federal civil rights remedy. Bivens filled that gap.

The three recognized contexts:
1. Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents, 403 U.S. 388 (1971) — Fourth Amendment
   unreasonable search and seizure by federal narcotics agents.
2. Davis v. Passman, 442 U.S. 228 (1979) — Fifth Amendment gender discrimination
   by a member of Congress against a staff member.
3. Carlson v. Green, 446 U.S. 14 (1980) — Eighth Amendment failure to provide
   adequate medical care to a federal prisoner.

What happened next:
After those three cases, the Supreme Court never recognized Bivens in a new context
again. For over 40 years, every attempt to extend it to new facts was rejected.

The formal narrowing:
- Ziglar v. Abbasi, 137 S. Ct. 1843 (2017): The Court created a two-step test.
  First, ask whether the claim arises in a "new context" — meaning any factual
  difference from the three recognized cases. Second, ask whether "special factors
  counsel hesitation." The Court defined "new context" so broadly that virtually any
  case that is not identical to the original three counts as new. And the "special
  factors" bar is set so low that almost anything qualifies.

- Egbert v. Boule, 596 U.S. 482 (2022): The Court confirmed and extended the
  narrowing. It held that if Congress has provided any alternative remedial structure
  — even one that does not fully compensate the plaintiff — that alone is enough
  to block a Bivens claim. The Court also suggested that separation of powers
  concerns almost always counsel against extending Bivens.

Current state (as of 2022):
The doctrine is functionally dead for new claims. The three original contexts survive —
you can still bring a Bivens claim that fits squarely within the Fourth Amendment
search/seizure, Fifth Amendment gender discrimination, or Eighth Amendment prison
medical care patterns. But if your facts differ in any meaningful way from those
three cases, you will almost certainly lose.

What this means for practitioners:
If your case involves federal officers, check first whether the facts fit within one
of the three recognized Bivens contexts. If they do not, you need a statutory cause
of action (such as the Federal Tort Claims Act) rather than a Bivens claim. Do not
rely on Bivens for novel constitutional claims against federal officers.

# Bivens Doctrine — Constitutional Claims Against Federal Officers

**Standard ID:** BIVENS_DOCTRINE_FEDERAL_OFFICER_LIABILITY
**Layer:** 1 — Rule
**Status:** ACTIVE
**Filed:** 2026-04-20

---

## Core Rule (*Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents*, 403 U.S. 388 (1971))

The Constitution implies a private right of action for money damages against federal officers who violate a person's constitutional rights, even when no statute expressly creates that right. A federal officer is personally liable for constitutional violations committed in the performance of their federal duties.

---

## The Three Recognized Contexts

The Supreme Court has extended Bivens in only three cases, and has refused every extension since 1980:

| Case | Constitutional Provision | Claim |
|------|------------------------|-------|
| *Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents*, 403 U.S. 388 (1971) | Fourth Amendment | Unreasonable search and seizure by federal narcotics agents |
| *Davis v. Passman*, 442 U.S. 228 (1979) | Fifth Amendment (Due Process) | Gender discrimination by a member of Congress against a congressional staff member |
| *Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14 (1980) | Eighth Amendment | Deliberate indifference to federal prisoner's medical needs |

---

## Current Controlling Standard — Severely Narrowed

**Two-step test (*Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 582 U.S. 120 (2017)):**

**Step 1 — New context:** Does the claim arise in a new context? "New context" means any meaningful factual difference from the three recognized cases. The court has defined this standard so broadly that virtually any case not identical to *Bivens*, *Davis*, or *Carlson* is a "new context."

**Step 2 — Special factors:** If it is a new context, do "special factors counsel hesitation"? Any factor suggesting that Congress is better positioned than courts to extend a remedy, or any existing alternative remedial structure, qualifies as a special factor — even if the alternative does not fully compensate the plaintiff.

**Post-*Egbert* (2022):** *Egbert v. Boule*, 596 U.S. 482 (2022) further constricted Bivens, holding that if Congress has provided any alternative remedial structure — even a limited administrative remedy — that alone generally defeats a Bivens claim. The Court stated that if it "were called to decide Bivens today," it would not create the doctrine.

---

## Practical Current Status

- Claims within the three original contexts: available (rarely successful on the merits due to qualified immunity)
- Claims in any new context: functionally unavailable

---

## Distinctions from § 1983

| Factor | § 1983 | Bivens |
|--------|--------|--------|
| Defendants | State and local government officers | Federal officers only |
| Statutory basis | Express — 42 U.S.C. § 1983 | Implied — Constitution only |
| Municipal liability | Yes (*Monell*) | No — no entity liability |
| Current viability | Robust | Severely curtailed |

---

## Alternatives When Bivens Is Unavailable

- **Federal Tort Claims Act (28 U.S.C. §§ 1346(b), 2671–2680):** Sues the United States for negligent acts of federal employees; does not cover intentional torts by most federal employees; no constitutional claims
- **Administrative remedies:** Agency grievance procedures, Inspector General complaints, EEO proceedings
- **Congressional investigations and oversight**
- **State tort claims:** Available against federal officers personally for state law torts committed outside the scope of federal authority

---

## Plain English

Section 1983 covers state and local officers; Bivens covers federal officers. But Bivens has been gutted. If a federal agent violates your constitutional rights in a way that fits the Fourth Amendment unreasonable search pattern, the Fifth Amendment discrimination pattern, or the Eighth Amendment prison medical care pattern — you may have a Bivens claim. For anything else, Bivens is dead. Use FTCA for federal employee negligence and exhaust all administrative remedies.

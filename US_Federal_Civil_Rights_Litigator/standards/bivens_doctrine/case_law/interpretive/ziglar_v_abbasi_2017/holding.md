# Ziglar v. Abbasi (2017) — Holding

## Citation
137 S. Ct. 1843 (2017)

## What happened

After September 11, 2001, federal officials detained hundreds of undocumented immigrants
in harsh conditions as part of a "hold until cleared" policy. Detainees were held in a
maximum-security facility in Brooklyn, subjected to strip searches, slammed against walls,
and held in solitary confinement — not because they were suspected of terrorism, but
because the government had not yet cleared them.

A group of detainees sued senior federal officials — including the Attorney General and
FBI Director — for violating their Fourth and Fifth Amendment rights.

## What the Court held

The Court refused to recognize a Bivens remedy. Justice Kennedy, writing for the majority,
established a two-step framework that applies to all future Bivens claims:

**Step 1: Is this a "new context"?**

A case presents a "new context" if it differs in any meaningful way from the three
previously recognized Bivens cases. The Court listed factors that make a context "new":

- The constitutional right at issue is different
- The type of federal officer or agency is different
- The mechanism of injury is different
- There is a different judicial or administrative process involved
- The case has implications for government policy or operations
- There is a risk of interfering with the political branches

This definition is so broad that virtually any case that is not a carbon copy of
Bivens (1971), Davis (1979), or Carlson (1980) qualifies as "new."

**Step 2: Do "special factors counsel hesitation"?**

If the case is in a new context, the court must ask whether any special factors suggest
that Congress, not the courts, should decide whether to create a damages remedy. The
Court identified several such factors:

- National security or foreign affairs implications
- Congressional legislation in the area (even if it does not create a damages remedy)
- The availability of any alternative remedial process
- Concerns about judicial interference with executive functions

The Court held that the existence of any of these factors — individually — is enough
to block a Bivens extension.

## Why this matters

Before Abbasi, lower courts sometimes extended Bivens to new situations by reasoning
from the general principles of the original case. After Abbasi, that is no longer
possible. The two-step framework is designed to prevent expansion. The "new context"
definition is so broad that almost everything qualifies, and the "special factors"
bar is so low that almost anything blocks the remedy.

This is where the Bivens doctrine stopped being a living doctrine and became a
historical artifact with three narrow exceptions.

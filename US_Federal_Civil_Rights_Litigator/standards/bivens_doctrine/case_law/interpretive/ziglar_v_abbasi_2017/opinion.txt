ZIGLAR v. ABBASI
582 U.S. 120 (2017)
United States Supreme Court
Decided: June 19, 2017

---

FACTS:

After September 11, 2001, hundreds of Arab Muslim men were detained in federal custody pending immigration removal proceedings. They were held in harsh conditions — solitary confinement, no recreation, restricted communications, strip searches — and subjected to a formal "hold until cleared" policy requiring FBI clearance before release. Former INS Commissioner James Ziglar and other high-level executive officials directed the detention policy.

The detainees brought Bivens actions against high-level executive officials for Fifth Amendment due process and equal protection violations, and against individual prison wardens for Eighth Amendment treatment claims. The Second Circuit allowed the claims to proceed.

The Supreme Court reversed in large part.

---

HOLDING:

Justice Kennedy wrote for the Court (4-2 on most issues; some portions 6-2).

BIVENS MUST NOT BE EXTENDED TO NEW CONTEXTS:

"Expanding the Bivens remedy is now a 'disfavored' judicial activity."

"If the case is different in a meaningful way from previous Bivens cases decided by this Court, then the context is new."

THE NEW CONTEXT ANALYSIS:
A "new context" arises when there are differences that "make it less probable that Congress would have sanctioned the implied cause of action." Relevant factors include:
- The rank of the officer (higher-ranking = more likely new context)
- The constitutional right at issue (Fourth/Fifth/Eighth amendment vs. others)
- The alternative remedial structure available
- Whether the claim involves national security

SPECIAL FACTORS COUNSELING HESITATION:
Even if the facts don't create a strictly "new context," courts must ask whether "special factors" counsel against implying the Bivens remedy:
1. **Alternative remedies**: If Congress has created an alternative remedial structure for the type of harm — even if it's not as complete as Bivens — that counsels against Bivens extension
2. **National security / foreign policy**: Executive discretion in these domains warrants extra hesitation
3. **High-level officials**: Bivens was designed to deter individual officers at the point of contact, not to second-guess high-level executive policy through litigation

WHAT SURVIVED ZIGLAR:
- The three recognized Bivens contexts (Bivens/4th, Davis/5th, Carlson/8th) remain intact
- Claims against individual line officers (not policymakers) in those contexts remain viable
- Ziglar did not eliminate Bivens — it severely restricted its extension

WHAT ZIGLAR FORECLOSED:
- Fifth Amendment substantive due process against high-level executive officials
- Fifth Amendment equal protection against officials directing a national security policy
- Any novel context where Congress has not expressly created a remedy but could have

---

APPLICATION TO STEWARD'S CASES:

Ziglar defines the outer limits of Bivens claims against federal officers:

1. **Claims against individual federal line officers** (SSA case workers, DDS examiners, federal law enforcement) in recognized contexts (Fourth/Fifth/Eighth Amendment) remain viable post-Ziglar.

2. **Claims against high-level officials** directing federal programs are essentially foreclosed under Ziglar — the "new context" and "special factors" analysis will defeat Bivens at summary judgment.

3. **Strategy**: For federal-actor claims, prioritize:
   - FTCA claims against the United States (no new-context restriction)
   - Statutory claims (APA, Privacy Act, 5 USC § 552a, Civil Service Reform Act)
   - § 1983 where any state actors are involved in the same scheme
   - Bivens only for claims that clearly fit within the three recognized contexts

4. **SSA phantom contact**: A claim that an individual SSA/DDS examiner violated the Fourth Amendment (unauthorized access to communications) or Fifth Amendment (denial of benefits without process) might fit within the recognized contexts if framed correctly.

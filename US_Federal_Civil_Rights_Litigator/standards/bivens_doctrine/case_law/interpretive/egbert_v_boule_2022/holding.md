# Egbert v. Boule (2022) — Holding

## Citation
596 U.S. 482 (2022)

## What happened

Robert Boule owned a bed-and-breakfast near the Canadian border in Blaine, Washington.
He occasionally hosted foreign nationals and cooperated with Border Patrol. In 2014,
Border Patrol Agent Erik Egbert came to the property to check on a visiting Turkish
national. When Boule asked Egbert to leave, Egbert threw Boule to the ground, injuring
him. Boule filed an administrative complaint. Egbert then retaliated by contacting the
IRS and other agencies to have Boule investigated.

Boule sued under Bivens for both the excessive force (Fourth Amendment) and the
retaliation (First Amendment).

## What the Court held

The Court refused to recognize a Bivens remedy on both claims. Justice Thomas, writing
for a 6-3 majority, confirmed and extended the Abbasi framework:

**On excessive force (Fourth Amendment):**
Even though the original Bivens case involved Fourth Amendment claims by federal agents,
the Court held this was a "new context" because it involved a Border Patrol agent
(national security implications), occurred at the international border, and involved
immigration enforcement. The existence of Border Patrol's internal grievance process —
even though it could not award damages to Boule — was enough of an "alternative
remedial structure" to block Bivens.

**On First Amendment retaliation:**
The Court held that no Bivens remedy has ever been recognized for a First Amendment
claim, making this a new context by definition. Congressional creation of any
investigative or disciplinary mechanism — even one that does not compensate the
victim — counts as a reason to deny Bivens.

**The broader signal:**
The Court stated that the question is not whether existing remedies provide "complete
relief," but whether Congress has provided "any alternative" remedial or investigative
process. This means that virtually any federal agency's internal complaint process —
no matter how inadequate — blocks Bivens.

Justice Gorsuch, concurring, argued that Bivens should be overruled entirely.

## Why this matters

Egbert confirmed that the Bivens doctrine is functionally dead for new claims. The
combination of Abbasi's broad "new context" definition and Egbert's rule that any
alternative process blocks the remedy means there is essentially no path to a new
Bivens claim.

The three original contexts (Bivens 1971, Davis 1979, Carlson 1980) technically survive,
but the Court strongly suggested it would not create them today if writing on a clean
slate. For practitioners, this means: do not bring a Bivens claim unless your facts
are nearly identical to one of the three recognized patterns. Look to the Federal Tort
Claims Act or other statutory remedies instead.

# Carlson v. Green, 446 U.S. 14 (1980)

## The Case

Joseph Jones, Jr. was a federal prisoner who died from asthma while incarcerated. His mother, Marie Green, alleged that federal prison officials knew of his chronic asthma but failed to provide adequate medical treatment — constituting cruel and unusual punishment under the Eighth Amendment. She sued for damages under Bivens.

The government argued that the Federal Tort Claims Act (FTCA) provided an adequate alternative remedy, precluding a Bivens action.

## What the Court Held

Justice Brennan, writing for the majority:

1. **A Bivens-type damages remedy is available for Eighth Amendment violations (cruel and unusual punishment) by federal prison officials.** This is the third recognized Bivens context — after the Fourth Amendment (Bivens itself) and the Fifth Amendment (Davis v. Passman).

2. **The FTCA is NOT an adequate alternative that precludes Bivens.** The FTCA has limitations that make it an incomplete substitute: it does not allow punitive damages, it requires suit against the United States rather than the individual officer, and it lacks the deterrent effect of personal liability.

## The Rule

**The third recognized Bivens context: Eighth Amendment claims for deliberate indifference to serious medical needs of federal prisoners.** The existence of the FTCA does not foreclose Bivens. One of only three surviving Bivens contexts.

## Why This Case Matters

Carlson was the last time the Supreme Court recognized a new Bivens context. Since 1980, the Court has refused to extend Bivens to any new factual setting — a trend that accelerated dramatically with Ziglar v. Abbasi (2017) and Egbert v. Boule (2022), which made new Bivens contexts nearly impossible.

The three surviving contexts (Fourth Amendment/Bivens, Fifth Amendment/Davis, Eighth Amendment/Carlson) remain valid, but the doctrine is now effectively frozen.

## Citation

Carlson v. Green, 446 U.S. 14 (1980). Vote: 7-2. Majority: Brennan, J.

# Reasoning — 29 U.S.C. § 794 (Rehabilitation Act § 504)

**Standard ID:** USC_29_794_REHAB_ACT_504
**Layer:** 2 — Reasoning
**Status:** ACTIVE

---

## Why This Standard Exists

Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act, enacted in 1973, was the first federal statute to prohibit disability discrimination in federally funded programs. It predates the ADA by 17 years and served as the conceptual foundation and model for ADA Title II.

The legislative history of § 504 is unusual: the provision was added to the Rehabilitation Act on the Senate floor with almost no debate, as a single sentence. The profound implications of the prohibition were not fully appreciated at passage. It was only during the regulatory implementation process in the late 1970s — when the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare took years to promulgate implementing regulations amid disability rights advocacy and direct action — that the significance of § 504 became clear.

The delayed regulatory implementation (HEW finally issued regulations in 1977, four years after enactment, after disability rights advocates occupied the HEW regional office in San Francisco for 28 days) established an important principle: the right to be free from disability discrimination in federally funded programs was real, congressionally enacted, and enforceable — even if the government was slow to implement it.

---

## Why Federal Financial Assistance Is the Trigger

The constitutional basis for § 504 is Congress's Spending Clause power: Congress may attach conditions to the receipt of federal funds, including nondiscrimination conditions. When an entity accepts federal money, it accepts the condition that it will not discriminate against disabled individuals.

This spending-clause foundation is both the statute's strength and its limitation:

**Strength:** It reaches private entities (hospitals, private universities, service organizations) that receive federal funding — entities that are not "public entities" under ADA Title II and would otherwise face no federal disability discrimination prohibition.

**Limitation:** Recipients who voluntarily return federal funding, or who have never accepted it, are not subject to § 504. The Spending Clause condition only runs with the money.

The *Grove City* problem (1984) illustrated the boundary: when the Supreme Court held that only the specific program receiving federal funds was covered — not the whole institution — Congress responded with the Civil Rights Restoration Act of 1987, which restored the "whole entity" coverage for most recipients. This legislative override demonstrates that § 504's scope is a policy choice Congress actively maintains.

---

## Why "Otherwise Qualified" — The Central Interpretive Question

The requirement that the plaintiff be "otherwise qualified" was the most contested interpretive issue in early § 504 jurisprudence. In *Southeastern Community College v. Davis*, 442 U.S. 397 (1979), the Supreme Court held that an otherwise qualified individual is one who can meet all program requirements "in spite of" their disability — not one who would be qualified with program modifications that would "fundamentally alter" the program.

This established the reasonable accommodation requirement: public entities must make reasonable modifications, but they are not required to fundamentally restructure their programs. The line between "reasonable modification" and "fundamental alteration" is the central factual and legal dispute in most § 504 cases.

---

## Why "Solely by Reason of" Is Significant

The causation standard in § 504 — "solely by reason of" disability — is a more demanding causation requirement than the ADA's "on the basis of" or "because of" formulations in some contexts. In practice, courts have often aligned § 504 and ADA Title II analysis, but the textual difference can matter when a plaintiff's exclusion was based on a combination of disability and non-disability factors.

---

## The § 504 / ADA Title II Interplay

Section 504 and ADA Title II are so closely aligned in their legal standards (both require reasonable accommodation, both protect qualified individuals with disabilities, both reach exclusion from programs and benefits) that cases are virtually always pled under both statutes simultaneously.

The strategic reasons to plead both:
- § 504 may reach federally funded private entities that ADA Title II does not cover (public entities only);
- ADA Title II may reach state and local government entities that do not receive federal funding;
- Pleading both maximizes coverage against any coverage gap in one statute or the other;
- Both statutes provide attorney fees under their respective fee provisions.

The key difference for sovereign immunity: *Tennessee v. Lane* (2004) established that ADA Title II validly abrogated state sovereign immunity in access-to-courts cases. The § 504 sovereign immunity analysis is different because states that accept federal funds waive their immunity as a condition of accepting the funds (*Barnes v. Gorman*, 536 U.S. 181 (2002) — states that accept federal funds waive Eleventh Amendment immunity as to § 504 claims).

## Application in Practice

Section 504 is invoked in:
- Disability accommodation claims against federally funded public schools (IDEA + § 504);
- Medical facility access and accommodation claims (hospitals receiving Medicare/Medicaid are federal fund recipients);
- Public transit disability accommodation (transit systems receiving federal transit funds);
- Higher education disability accommodation (universities receiving federal student loan funds);
- Government social services programs receiving HHS grants.

The practical starting point for any § 504 analysis: confirm the defendant receives federal financial assistance (directly or indirectly); confirm the plaintiff has a qualifying disability; confirm the denial of benefit or exclusion was because of the disability; identify what reasonable accommodation would have enabled participation.

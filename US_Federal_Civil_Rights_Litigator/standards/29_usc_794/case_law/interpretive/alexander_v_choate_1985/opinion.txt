ALEXANDER v. CHOATE
469 U.S. 287 (1985)
United States Supreme Court
Decided: January 9, 1985

---

FACTS:

Tennessee reduced the number of inpatient hospital days covered under its Medicaid program from 20 to 14 days per year. Tennessee received federal Medicaid funding and was subject to § 504 of the Rehabilitation Act, 29 U.S.C. § 794. Handicapped Medicaid recipients (respondents) challenged the reduction, arguing that the 14-day cap would have a disproportionate adverse impact on disabled recipients, who on average required more than 14 inpatient days per year, while non-disabled recipients typically needed fewer.

The question was whether § 504 prohibited facially neutral program rules that had a disparate impact on disabled persons, even when there was no discriminatory intent.

---

HOLDING:

Justice Marshall wrote for a unanimous Court.

THE CHOATE HOLDING — TWO PRINCIPLES:

PRINCIPLE ONE — MEANINGFUL ACCESS:
Section 504 requires that recipients of federal funds provide "meaningful access" to their programs for disabled persons. A facially neutral rule that effectively denies disabled persons meaningful access to a program violates § 504, even without discriminatory intent.

"To assure meaningful access, reasonable accommodations in the grantee's program or benefit may have to be made."

"The benefit itself, of course, cannot be defined in a way that effectively denies otherwise qualified handicapped individuals the meaningful access to which they are entitled."

PRINCIPLE TWO — DISPARATE IMPACT vs. REASONABLE ACCOMMODATION:
The Court declined to hold that § 504 categorically prohibits all rules with disparate impact on disabled persons. But it held that some degree of disparate impact analysis is appropriate:

"[S]ome line short of a ban on all conduct with a disparate effect on the handicapped is required."

The Tennessee 14-day cap survived because:
1. It applied equally to all Medicaid recipients (neutral on its face)
2. It did not deny the benefit entirely — disabled persons still received 14 days
3. The State had not defined the benefit in a way that would exclude disabled persons categorically

However, if the State had defined the benefit in a way that effectively excluded disabled persons (e.g., "inpatient care excluding any condition requiring more than 14 days"), § 504 would be violated.

THE MEANINGFUL ACCESS STANDARD:
A program does not provide meaningful access if:
1. Its neutral rules effectively exclude disabled persons from program benefits they would otherwise receive
2. The definition of the benefit itself operates to deny access based on the disability

---

APPLICATION TO STEWARD'S CASES:

Alexander v. Choate establishes the "meaningful access" doctrine that applies to:

1. **SSA/DDS disability determination process**: If the agency's telephone-only contact protocol effectively excludes claimants whose phones have been compromised by SIM swap (cutting them off from contact entirely), that may deny meaningful access in violation of § 504.

2. **CalVCB application process**: If CalVCB's procedural requirements (tight deadlines, written-only submission requirements) operate to deny meaningful access to applicants whose ability to communicate has been impaired by the very crime they are reporting, § 504 may be implicated.

3. **Medical program access**: Systematic exclusion of disabled individuals from procedures available to non-disabled patients, absent medical necessity, may violate the meaningful-access standard.

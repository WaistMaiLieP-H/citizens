# Reasoning — 42 U.S.C. § 1985(3) (Conspiracy to Deprive of Equal Protection)

**Standard ID:** USC_42_1985_3_CONSPIRACY_EQUAL_PROTECTION
**Layer:** 2 — Reasoning
**Status:** ACTIVE
**Filed:** 2026-04-20

---

## Why This Standard Exists

Section 1985(3) was enacted as part of the Ku Klux Klan Act of 1871. Its purpose was specific and documented: the Reconstruction Congress received extensive testimony and evidence about coordinated private conspiracies — the KKK and allied groups — that were terrorizing freedmen, preventing them from exercising civil rights, and preventing state governments from enforcing the Fourteenth Amendment.

The problem that § 1985(3) addressed was not solvable by § 1983 alone. Section 1983 requires "color of law" — action under government authority. But the KKK was a private organization. When masked private citizens burned a freedman's home to prevent him from voting, § 1983 did not reach them. The 1871 Congress created § 1985(3) to reach these private conspiracies by grounding the statute in the Thirteenth Amendment (abolishing slavery) and the constitutional protection of privileges and immunities — sources of congressional power that reach private conduct, not just state action.

---

## Why *Griffin v. Breckenridge* (1971) Was Essential

For the statute's first century, the Supreme Court effectively nullified it. In *Collins v. Hardyman* (1951), the Court imposed a state-action requirement that reduced § 1985(3) to a redundant shadow of § 1983 — a plaintiff who could prove state action could use § 1983, and without state action § 1985(3) was unavailable. The statute that was specifically designed to reach private conspiracies was interpreted to require government involvement.

*Griffin v. Breckenridge*, 403 U.S. 88 (1971), unanimously corrected this error. The Court held that § 1985(3) does reach purely private conspiracies — but required that the conspiracy be motivated by "some racial, or perhaps otherwise class-based, invidiously discriminatory animus." This requirement served two functions:

1. **Constitutional grounding:** Without the class-based animus requirement, § 1985(3) would be a general federal conspiracy tort with no clear constitutional basis. The Thirteenth Amendment and the equal protection power reach discriminatory private conduct; they do not reach every private conspiracy. Tying the statute to discriminatory animus keeps it within Congress's constitutional authority.

2. **Scope limitation:** Congress in 1871 was concerned with racial violence against freedmen — not with creating a general federal cause of action for every private conspiracy that causes injury. The animus requirement honors the original scope of legislative concern.

---

## The Class-Based Animus Requirement — Why It Is Demanding

Courts have consistently held that § 1985(3) reaches only animus directed at a person because of membership in an identifiable class — not animus motivated by personal animosity, business competition, or political disagreement.

**Race and national origin** are clearly covered — this is the statute's historical heartland.

**Sex** — most circuits recognize sex as a qualifying class, though the Supreme Court has not definitively ruled.

**Political affiliation** — deeply divided. Some circuits hold that political partisanship qualifies as a class under § 1985(3); others require a more immutable characteristic.

**Economic or commercial motivation** does not qualify. In *United Brotherhood of Carpenters v. Scott* (1983), the Court held that a labor union conspiracy motivated by economic competition — not by animus toward the plaintiffs as members of a protected class — did not state a § 1985(3) claim.

**Activity vs. class:** In *Bray v. Alexandria Women's Health Clinic* (1993), the Court held that opposition to an activity in which a protected class disproportionately participates is not the same as animus toward the class itself. Conspirators who oppose abortion are not, by that fact alone, acting with animus toward women as a class.

---

## Why Private Reach Makes § 1985(3) Distinctive

The absence of a state-action requirement is § 1985(3)'s most distinctive feature. It creates a federal cause of action against purely private conspirators — something § 1983 cannot do. This matters in cases involving:
- Organized private discrimination (housing, employment, access to public accommodations);
- Coordinated private violence or intimidation targeting racial or religious minorities;
- Private conspiracies to prevent the exercise of constitutional rights through private leverage (threats, economic coercion, violence).

However, even in the private context, the constitutional right being conspired against must be one that Congress can protect. Rights that protect only against government action — Fourth Amendment search protections, for example — cannot be vindicated through § 1985(3) against private conspirators.

---

## Interaction with § 1986 — The Duty to Prevent

Section 1986 creates liability for persons who have knowledge that a § 1985 conspiracy is about to be committed, have the power to prevent it, and fail or refuse to act. This extends accountability beyond the conspirators themselves to those who knowingly permit the conspiracy to proceed.

The § 1986 claim requires a valid § 1985 predicate — there can be no § 1986 liability without a § 1985 conspiracy. Section 1986 is particularly relevant in cases involving supervisors or associates of conspirators who had actual knowledge and failed to intervene.

---

## Application in Practice

Section 1985(3) is most viable in cases involving:
- Organized hate crimes targeting identifiable racial or religious minorities;
- Coordinated conspiracies to prevent minorities from exercising voting rights;
- Private conspiracies to deprive persons of equal access to housing or employment based on race;
- Conspiracies involving combinations of private actors and government officers (where § 1983 may additionally apply).

The practical bar is high: plaintiffs must prove both an agreement among multiple parties and class-based animus — neither of which is easily established from public record. Discovery is essential to proving the "meeting of minds" element.

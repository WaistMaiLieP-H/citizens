# Griffin v. Breckenridge, 403 U.S. 88 (1971)

## The Case

R.G. Breckenridge and two other Black men were driving on a public highway in Kemper County, Mississippi. Lamar Griffin and Cecil Thornton — two white men — stopped the car, forced the three Black men out, and beat them with a club because they believed the men were civil rights workers. One of the victims was beaten unconscious. The attackers mistakenly identified one passenger as a civil rights activist they had targeted.

The victims sued under §1985(3) — the conspiracy provision that reaches private individuals who conspire to deprive others of equal protection.

The district court dismissed, reasoning that §1985(3) required "state action" — that only state actors could violate equal protection, and private conspiracies were beyond the statute's reach.

## What the Court Held

Justice Stewart, writing for a unanimous Court:

1. **Section 1985(3) reaches private conspiracies — it does NOT require state action.** The Court overruled the restrictive reading in Collins v. Hardyman (1951), which had held that §1985(3) required state involvement. The statutory text — "two or more persons... conspire or go in disguise on the highway" — makes no mention of state action. It describes private conduct.

2. **The constitutional authority for reaching private conduct comes from the Thirteenth Amendment (which reaches private racial discrimination) and the right to travel (which is protected against private interference).** The Fourteenth Amendment's "state action" requirement was avoided by grounding §1985(3) in these alternative constitutional bases.

3. **To state a claim under §1985(3), the plaintiff must allege:**
   - (a) A conspiracy;
   - (b) For the purpose of depriving, either directly or indirectly, any person or class of persons of the equal protection of the laws, or of equal privileges and immunities under the laws;
   - (c) An act in furtherance of the conspiracy;
   - (d) Whereby another is injured in his person or property, or deprived of having and exercising any right or privilege of a citizen.

4. **The conspiracy must be motivated by "some racial, or perhaps otherwise class-based, invidiously discriminatory animus."** The statute does not reach all conspiracies — only those aimed at depriving a class of persons of equal protection. The "class-based animus" requirement ensures the statute targets discrimination, not ordinary tortious conspiracies.

## The Rule Griffin Stands For

Section 1985(3) provides a federal civil cause of action against **private individuals** who conspire, with racial or class-based discriminatory animus, to deprive persons of equal protection. No state action required. The constitutional authority flows from the Thirteenth Amendment and the right to travel.

## Statute Version the Court Was Reading

42 USC §1985(3) — unchanged since R.S. §1980 (1874). The text has never been amended.

## Why This Case Matters

Griffin is the §1985(3) equivalent of Monroe v. Pape for §1983 — the revival case that made the statute usable. Before Griffin, Collins v. Hardyman had gutted §1985(3) by requiring state action, which made it redundant with §1983. Griffin restored the statute's independent purpose: reaching private conspiracies that §1983 cannot touch because its "under color of" requirement excludes private actors.

The "class-based animus" requirement is the limiting principle. Without it, §1985(3) would reach every tortious conspiracy, converting it from a civil rights statute into a general federal tort conspiracy provision. With it, the statute stays within its Reconstruction-era purpose: combating organized discrimination.

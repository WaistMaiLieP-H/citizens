# 42 USC §1985(3) — Historical Chain

## The Wound, the Promise, and the Limiting

---

### I. The Wound (same wound as §1983, different face)

The Ku Klux Klan was not a collection of individual bad actors. It was an organized conspiracy — chapters across the South, coordinated campaigns of terror, a chain of command from Grand Wizard to local den. The individual state actor who abused his office was one face of the problem (§1983's target). The organized conspiracy of private and state actors working together to systematically destroy the political, civil, and physical safety of Black citizens was the other face.

Section 1 of the 1871 Act gave the injured person a remedy against the individual state actor. Section 2 gave the injured person a remedy against the **conspiracy itself** — against every person who joined it, whether state actor or private individual.

The "go in disguise upon the public highway or upon the premises of another" language is the statute describing the Klan in literal terms: men in hoods riding the roads at night, entering the homes and gathering places of freedmen. The statute was written to fit the enemy it faced.

### II. The Promise

Section 2 of the 1871 Act promised: **When people conspire to deny equal protection — whether they hold office or not — the federal courts will provide a remedy.**

This was the boldest promise in the Act. Section 1's promise was grounded in the Fourteenth Amendment's state-action requirement — state actors using state authority. Section 2's promise reached beyond state action to private conspiracies. Congress was saying: the right to equal protection is so fundamental that even private individuals who conspire to destroy it are answerable in federal court.

### III. The Dormancy (1871–1971)

Like §1983, §1985 went dormant. The Supreme Court's 1951 decision in Collins v. Hardyman gutted the statute by requiring state action — which made §1985 identical to §1983 and rendered its conspiracy provisions pointless.

For a century, the promise that federal courts would reach private conspiracies against equal protection went undelivered.

### IV. The Revival: Griffin v. Breckenridge (1971)

Ten years after Monroe v. Pape revived §1983, Griffin v. Breckenridge revived §1985(3).

Three Black men driving on a Mississippi highway were stopped and beaten by two white men who believed them to be civil rights workers. The Supreme Court unanimously held: §1985(3) reaches private conspiracies. No state action required. The constitutional authority comes from the Thirteenth Amendment and the right to travel.

But Griffin also established the limiting principle: the conspiracy must be motivated by "some racial, or perhaps otherwise class-based, invidiously discriminatory animus." §1985(3) does not reach all conspiracies — only those aimed at depriving a class of persons of equal protection.

### V. The Narrowing

Unlike §1983, which expanded broadly after Monroe, §1985(3) was progressively narrowed after Griffin:

- **United Brotherhood of Carpenters v. Scott (1983):** §1985(3) does not reach conspiracies motivated by economic or commercial animus. The "class-based animus" must be racial or similar to racial discrimination. Labor union disputes, business competition, and political disagreements are outside the statute's scope.

- **Great American Savings v. Novotny (1979):** §1985(3) does not create substantive rights — it provides a remedy only for the violation of rights that exist independently. A §1985(3) plaintiff must identify a right protected by some other provision of law (e.g., the Fourteenth Amendment, the Thirteenth Amendment, the right to travel).

- **Bray v. Alexandria Women's Health Clinic (1993):** Opposition to an activity (abortion) is not animus against a class (women). The "class" must be defined by an immutable or defining characteristic, not by engagement in a contested practice.

### VI. The Current State

Section 1985(3) today is a powerful but narrow statute. It reaches:
- Private conspiracies motivated by racial animus to deprive Black citizens (or other racial groups) of equal protection
- Conspiracies to interfere with the right of interstate travel
- Potentially, conspiracies motivated by animus against other classes defined by immutable characteristics (national origin, religion — the boundaries are still being litigated)

It does NOT reach:
- Conspiracies motivated by economic, political, or ideological disagreements
- Conspiracies against persons defined by their activities rather than their identity
- Conspiracies to deprive persons of rights that are protected only against government interference

The statute has never been textually amended. Every word in 42 USC §1985 today is the 1874 recodification of the 1871 Act's Section 2. All changes in scope have come from the courts.

### VII. The Relevance to the Steward's Case

Section 1985(3) reaches the orchestration pattern documented in the steward's family law files: multiple actors (mother, Christina, Ryan, state agencies, service providers) allegedly conspiring to deprive the steward of civil rights through coordinated action. If the conspiracy includes state actors and private actors working together, §1985(3) may reach the private conspirators that §1983 alone cannot touch. The cross-reference between §1983 (for state actors acting under color of law) and §1985(3) (for the conspiracy itself, including private participants) is how the full scope of an orchestrated deprivation gets addressed in federal court.

The "class-based animus" requirement remains the threshold question: the steward's claim would need to identify the class and the discriminatory animus that motivated the conspiracy.

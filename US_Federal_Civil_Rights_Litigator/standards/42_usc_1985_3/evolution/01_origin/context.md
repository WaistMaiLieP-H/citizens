# 42 USC §1985 — Origin Context

## The Same Wound, a Different Angle

Section 2 of the 1871 Ku Klux Klan Act addressed the same wound as Section 1 (what became §1983), but from a different angle. Where Section 1 targeted individual state actors — the sheriff, the judge, the officer who uses state authority to violate rights — Section 2 targeted **conspiracies**. The Klan was not a collection of individual bad actors. It was an organized conspiracy, operating across state lines, with coordinated objectives: to overthrow Reconstruction, to prevent Black citizens from exercising constitutional rights, and to drive federal officials out of the South.

## What the Original Section 2 Did

The 1871 Section 2 was massive — a single sentence covering two full pages of the Statutes at Large. It addressed multiple conspiracy categories:

1. **Conspiracy against federal authority** — conspiring to overthrow the government, levy war, oppose federal authority by force, prevent execution of federal law, seize federal property.

2. **Conspiracy against federal officers** — preventing persons from accepting or holding federal office, inducing officers to leave their posts, injuring officers for performing their duties.

3. **Conspiracy to obstruct federal justice** — deterring witnesses, intimidating jurors, influencing verdicts.

4. **Conspiracy to deprive equal protection** — conspiring or going in disguise on the public highway or on another's premises to deprive any person or class of persons of equal protection or equal privileges under the laws, or to prevent state authorities from providing equal protection.

5. **Conspiracy to prevent voting** — preventing citizens from supporting candidates for President, Vice President, or Congress.

The original Section 2 had both **criminal penalties** (fine of $500–$5,000 and/or imprisonment of 6 months to 6 years — a "high crime") and a **civil cause of action** for damages. The criminal provisions were later separated out. The civil cause of action became §1985.

## Why Conspiracy Required Its Own Section

Section 1 (§1983) requires action "under color of" state law — it only reaches state actors. But the Klan's violence was often committed by private individuals acting in concert with state actors, or by private individuals whose acts the state condoned by refusing to prosecute. Section 2 filled this gap: it reached **private conspiracies** to deprive persons of equal protection, regardless of whether any conspirator was a state actor.

This was constitutionally bold. The Fourteenth Amendment says "No State shall..." — it restricts state action, not private conduct. Congress's theory was that a private conspiracy to deny equal protection, when the state fails to respond, is itself a form of state denial of equal protection — the state's inaction becomes the constitutional violation.

This theory was later narrowed by the courts (see Griffin v. Breckenridge for the survival path and United Brotherhood v. Scott for the limiting principle), but the original ambition of Section 2 was to reach the entire Klan apparatus — state actors and private conspirators alike.

## The Promise

Section 2 promised: **When people conspire to destroy constitutional rights — whether they wear badges or bedsheets — the injured person has a federal remedy.** The conspiracy itself is the wrong, and any act done in furtherance of it that causes injury gives rise to a civil action for damages.

The "go in disguise upon the public highway" language is not metaphorical. It describes the Klan in literal terms: hooded men riding at night to terrorize.

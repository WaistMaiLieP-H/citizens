# Monell Doctrine — Historical Chain

## When the City Pays

---

### I. The Problem Monell Solved (1978)

Before Monell, §1983 plaintiffs could only collect from individual officers. Monroe v. Pape (1961) had held that municipalities were not "persons" under §1983. This meant that even when an unconstitutional policy was the city's own, the plaintiff could only sue the officer who carried it out — and individual officers often had no money to pay a judgment.

Monell v. Dept of Social Services (1978) overruled Monroe on this point and held that municipalities ARE "persons" under §1983 — but only for their own policies, not for the rogue acts of their employees.

### II. The Monell Framework

Municipal liability requires identifying the **policy, custom, or deliberate choice** that caused the constitutional violation:

1. **Official policy** — a rule adopted by the municipality's legislative body (city council resolution, county ordinance, departmental regulation)
2. **Final policymaker decision** — a single decision by an official whose decisions represent official municipal policy (Pembaur v. City of Cincinnati, 1986)
3. **Custom or practice** — a pattern so widespread and persistent that it carries the force of law, even though never formally adopted (Praprotnik v. City of St. Louis, 1988)
4. **Failure to train** — when the failure amounts to deliberate indifference to constitutional rights (Canton v. Harris, 1989)
5. **Failure to supervise/discipline** — when the municipality's tolerance of misconduct is so deliberate that it constitutes policy

### III. The No-Respondeat-Superior Rule

The critical limit: **no respondeat superior under §1983.** An officer's constitutional violation, standing alone, does not make the city liable. The plaintiff must connect the violation to a municipal policy, custom, or deliberate indifference. This is the single most important distinction between federal Monell liability and California's Gov. Code §815.2 (which IS respondeat superior).

### IV. The Narrowing

- **Canton v. Harris (1989):** Failure to train requires "deliberate indifference" — the need for training must be obvious and the municipality's failure must be a conscious choice.
- **Connick v. Thompson (2011):** Single-incident failure-to-train claims don't work against prosecutors' offices. A pattern of prior violations is required.
- **Board of County Commissioners v. Brown (1997):** A single hiring decision can support Monell liability only if the municipality's decision was "deliberately indifferent" to the risk that the hire would violate constitutional rights — a very high bar.

### V. The Practical Split

The Monell doctrine creates a critical practical split between federal and state claims:

| Element | Federal (Monell) | California (§815.2) |
|---|---|---|
| Basis of entity liability | Policy, custom, or deliberate indifference | Respondeat superior (scope of employment) |
| Must prove policy? | YES | NO |
| Single officer's act enough? | NO (must connect to policy) | YES (if within scope) |
| Burden on plaintiff | Higher | Lower |

This is why civil rights plaintiffs in California should always consider bringing both §1983 (for federal damages + fee-shifting) AND Bane Act (for state damages + §815.2 respondeat superior). The Bane Act path to entity liability is easier to prove than the Monell path.

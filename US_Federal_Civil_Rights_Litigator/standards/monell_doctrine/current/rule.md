# Monell Municipal Liability Doctrine

**Standard ID:** MONELL_MUNICIPAL_LIABILITY
**Layer:** 1 — Rule
**Status:** ACTIVE
**Filed:** 2026-04-20

---

## Core Rule (*Monell v. Dept. of Social Services*, 436 U.S. 658 (1978))

A municipality or local government entity may be held liable under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 only when the constitutional violation results from:

**(1) Official policy** — a formal decision or rule adopted by the municipal body's lawmaking officers;

**(2) Act or decision by a final policymaker** — an act or decision by an official whose acts "may fairly be said to represent official policy," even if the act is not a formally adopted rule; or

**(3) Custom or practice** — a widespread, persistent practice that, although not formally authorized, carries the force of law because it is so longstanding and well-settled.

---

## No Respondeat Superior

A municipality **cannot** be held liable under § 1983 solely because it employs someone who committed a constitutional violation. The plaintiff must prove that the municipality's own policy, custom, or deliberate choice caused the violation — not merely that a government employee committed wrongdoing.

This is the fundamental distinction between § 1983 municipal liability and California Government Code § 815.2 (vicarious liability for employee acts within scope of employment). Under California law, the government entity is liable for its employee's in-scope torts without proving a policy or custom. Under federal § 1983, the municipality can escape liability even when its employee committed a clear constitutional violation, unless the violation traces to municipal policy or custom.

---

## Proving a Custom

Where no formal policy exists, the plaintiff must establish:
- A pattern of similar constitutional violations by government employees;
- The pattern was so widespread that the municipality had constructive knowledge of it;
- The municipality deliberately chose not to act (deliberate indifference).

A single incident is generally insufficient to establish a custom. (*Oklahoma City v. Tuttle*, 471 U.S. 808 (1985).) However, a single incident by a final policymaker may constitute official policy sufficient for Monell liability. (*Pembaur v. Cincinnati*, 475 U.S. 469 (1986).)

---

## Failure to Train

*City of Canton v. Harris*, 489 U.S. 378 (1989): A municipality's failure to train its officers can constitute deliberate indifference sufficient for § 1983 municipal liability if:
- The training was inadequate in relation to the tasks the employees must perform;
- The inadequacy amounts to deliberate indifference to the rights of persons with whom the employees come in contact; and
- The inadequate training caused the constitutional violation.

The failure-to-train theory requires showing that the municipality knew that the particular type of misconduct was a likely consequence of the inadequate training — not merely that some injury was possible.

---

## Final Policymaker

Who counts as a "final policymaker" is a question of state law. (*Jett v. Dallas Independent School District*, 491 U.S. 701 (1989).) The court looks to whether the actor had final authority to set policy in the area where the constitutional violation occurred — with no further review by a higher authority.

---

## Plain English

To sue a city or county under § 1983, you must do more than prove a government employee violated your rights. You must trace the violation to a formal policy the city adopted, a decision by a high-level official who sets city policy, or a widespread practice the city permitted to continue despite knowing about it. The rule protects municipalities from being sued every time an individual employee misbehaves — but it does not protect them when the misbehavior flows from systemic choices the city made or tolerated.

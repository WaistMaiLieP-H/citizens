# Connick v. Thompson, 563 U.S. 51 (2011)

## The Case

John Thompson spent 18 years in prison — 14 on death row — for a murder he did not commit. Prosecutors in the New Orleans DA's office had concealed blood evidence that would have exonerated him. After his exoneration, Thompson sued the DA's office under §1983 Monell, arguing a failure to train prosecutors on their Brady disclosure obligations.

A jury awarded $14 million. The Fifth Circuit affirmed. The Supreme Court reversed.

## What the Court Held

Justice Thomas, writing for the majority:

1. **A single Brady violation by a prosecutor does not establish a "pattern" sufficient to prove deliberate indifference for failure-to-train liability.** Canton's deliberate-indifference standard requires more than one incident — it requires evidence that the municipality was on notice of a pattern of violations and consciously failed to respond.

2. **Prosecutors, unlike police officers, are trained as lawyers and are presumed to know the law.** The Court rejected the argument that prosecutors' Brady obligations are so obvious that failure to provide specific Brady training constitutes deliberate indifference. Attorneys are trained in law school; additional training is not constitutionally required.

3. **The Canton "single-incident" exception — where a violation is a "highly predictable consequence" of the failure to train — does not apply to trained attorneys.** The exception applies where untrained employees face a situation obviously requiring specific training (like officers handling medical emergencies). Prosecutors already have the relevant training (legal education).

## The Rule

**Failure-to-train Monell liability against a prosecutor's office requires a pattern of Brady violations — a single incident is insufficient. The single-incident exception from Canton does not apply to trained attorneys.**

## Why This Case Matters

Connick made Monell failure-to-train claims against prosecutor's offices nearly impossible. The requirement of a documented pattern of prior violations means that the first victim of prosecutorial misconduct has no Monell remedy — only the victims who come after a pattern is established. Combined with absolute prosecutorial immunity (which shields individual prosecutors from §1983 damages), Connick leaves victims of prosecutorial misconduct with no federal remedy at all in most cases.

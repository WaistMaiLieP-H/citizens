# 42 USC §1983 — Historical Chain

## The Wound, the Promise, the Dormancy, the Revival, and the Battlefield

---

### I. The Wound (1865–1871)

The Civil War ended slavery. The Reconstruction Amendments promised citizenship, equal protection, due process, and the right to vote regardless of race. But promises written in constitutional text mean nothing when the states that are bound by them refuse to honor them and the federal government has no mechanism to force compliance.

Between 1865 and 1871, the former Confederate states rebuilt their power structures. They passed Black Codes — statutes that restricted the movement, labor, property rights, and legal standing of freedmen so comprehensively that slavery continued in everything but name. When the Codes were challenged, the states replaced them with facially neutral vagrancy statutes, apprenticeship laws, and convict leasing schemes that achieved the same ends.

The Ku Klux Klan formed in 1866. By 1868, it was a paramilitary organization operating across the South. Klansmen murdered Black elected officials. They burned Black schools. They attacked and killed freedmen who voted, who testified, who held property, who asserted any right the Fourteenth Amendment promised them. They attacked white Republicans who supported Reconstruction. They did this in costume, at night, with impunity — because the state officials responsible for enforcing the law were members of the Klan, or sympathizers, or terrified into silence.

The wound was not the private violence alone. The wound was that **the state itself was the instrument**. Sheriffs who refused to investigate. Prosecutors who refused to charge. Judges who dismissed cases or acquitted the guilty. Legislators who passed the enabling laws. Governors who pardoned the convicted. The Fourteenth Amendment said "no State shall deny... equal protection of the laws." The Southern states answered: we will deny everything, and the amendment gives you no remedy when we do.

President Grant told Congress the truth: life and property were insecure. The mails were unsafe. Revenue collection was dangerous. The states could not — or would not — protect their own people.

### II. The Promise (April 20, 1871)

Congress answered with the Ku Klux Klan Act. Section 1 made the promise:

**When any person, acting with state authority, deprives any person of any constitutional right, the injured person can sue that person directly in federal court.**

The promise was radical. Before 1871, there was no general federal cause of action against state officials. Federal courts had no jurisdiction over state actors violating constitutional rights. The only remedy was state court — which was where the problem was.

Section 1 said: the state courts have failed. The federal courts will not. Any person who uses the power of the state to violate the Constitution is personally liable to the person they injured. No state law can excuse it. No state custom can justify it. The federal court is open.

The language was universal. The wound was particular — Klan terror in the Reconstruction South — but the promise was general. "Any person." "Any rights, privileges, or immunities secured by the Constitution." The drafters knew the wound would change shape. They wrote the promise to cover wounds they hadn't yet imagined.

### III. The Dormancy (1871–1961)

The promise died almost immediately. The Compromise of 1877 ended Reconstruction. Federal troops withdrew. The Southern states completed their counter-revolution. Jim Crow replaced the Black Codes. The Supreme Court dismantled the Reconstruction framework piece by piece — the Civil Rights Cases (1883), Plessy v. Ferguson (1896), the narrow readings of the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments.

Section 1 of the 1871 Act — now recodified as Revised Statutes §1979, later as 42 USC §1983 — sat on the books for ninety years, technically alive, practically dead. The Supreme Court's restrictive readings made it nearly unusable. In Barney v. City of New York (1904), the Court held that §1983 required the state law itself to be unconstitutional, not merely that a state officer acted unconstitutionally while purporting to enforce it. This interpretation made the statute useless against the most common form of state-actor abuse: the officer who exercises state authority in violation of state law.

Between 1871 and 1920, only 21 cases were brought under §1983. Between 1920 and 1960, the numbers grew slowly, but the statute remained a backwater of federal law. The promise made in response to the worst domestic terrorism in American history sat unused while the harm continued in different forms.

### IV. The Revival (February 20, 1961)

Monroe v. Pape, 365 U.S. 167 (1961).

Thirteen Chicago police officers broke into the Monroe family's home without a warrant, forced the family to stand naked while they ransacked the house, took James Monroe to the station, interrogated him for ten hours without counsel, and released him without charges.

Justice Douglas wrote the opinion that brought §1983 back from the dead:

- "Under color of" state law means acting with state authority, even when the specific act violates state law. An officer who beats a prisoner acts "under color of" law because the badge is what put the officer in the room.
- §1983 provides a supplemental federal remedy. No exhaustion of state remedies required. The whole point is that state remedies failed.
- The statute means what it says: "every person" who deprives another of rights "shall be liable."

In the decade after Monroe, §1983 filings in federal court grew from a few hundred per year to tens of thousands. The promise made in 1871, dormant for ninety years, was alive.

### V. The Expansion (1961–1982)

The revival opened a floodgate. The courts extended §1983 in every direction:

- **Monell v. Dept of Social Services (1978):** Municipalities can be sued under §1983 for their official policies — overruling Monroe's holding that cities are not "persons" under the statute. The municipal treasury became reachable.
- **Maine v. Thiboutot (1980):** §1983 covers violations of federal statutes, not just constitutional rights — reading the "and laws" phrase added in the 1874 recodification.
- **Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents (1971):** The Supreme Court created an implied cause of action against federal officers — the federal counterpart to §1983, sharing the same qualified immunity framework.
- **Pub. L. 94-559 (1976):** Congress enacted 42 USC §1988, the Civil Rights Attorney's Fees Awards Act, allowing prevailing §1983 plaintiffs to recover attorney's fees. This made civil rights litigation economically viable for plaintiffs with small individual damages — without fee-shifting, most §1983 cases could not attract representation.

### VI. The Battlefield: Qualified Immunity (1967–present)

The same period that expanded §1983's reach also created the doctrine that would progressively limit it: qualified immunity.

- **Pierson v. Ray (1967):** Police officers have a defense of good faith and probable cause. The Court imported common-law immunities into a statute whose text provides none.
- **Harlow v. Fitzgerald (1982):** The subjective good-faith test was replaced with an objective test: officials are immune unless their conduct violates "clearly established" law "of which a reasonable person would have known." This was designed to resolve immunity at summary judgment, sparing officers from discovery and trial.
- **Anderson v. Creighton (1987):** "Clearly established" requires more than a general principle — the right must be established with specificity, so that a reasonable officer would understand that what they are doing violates the law.
- **Saucier v. Katz (2001):** Made the two-step analysis (1. constitutional violation? 2. clearly established?) mandatory in sequence — courts had to decide the merits before addressing immunity.
- **Pearson v. Callahan (2009):** Made the sequence optional. Courts can now skip straight to "clearly established" and grant immunity without ever deciding whether the conduct was constitutional. This has been criticized as freezing constitutional law development: no precedent → no clearly established right → immunity → no new precedent.
- **Taylor v. Riojas (2020):** Pushed back at the extreme — some conduct is so egregious (confining a prisoner in feces-covered cells for six days) that qualified immunity does not apply even without prior case law on point. Established the "obvious clarity" exception.

The battlefield continues. As of this writing, qualified immunity remains a judicially created doctrine with no textual basis in §1983. Congress has never codified it. The 1996 amendment wrote judicial immunity for injunctive relief into the statute — proof that when Congress wants an immunity in §1983, it writes one. Qualified immunity's absence from the text is a silence that grows louder with each legislative session that declines to codify it.

### VIII. The Arc

The Ku Klux Klan Act of 1871 was enacted because state actors were using state authority to destroy constitutional rights, and no federal remedy existed. After ninety years of dormancy, Monroe v. Pape revived the statute and opened federal courts to civil rights claims against state actors. The expansion era made the promise real — municipal liability, fee-shifting, statutory coverage. Then qualified immunity began limiting the promise — not by amending the statute but by judicially constructing a defense the statute's text does not contain.

The tension between the promise and the limitation is the living history of §1983. The statute's text has changed only twice since 1871 (1979 and 1996). Everything else — the dormancy, the revival, the expansion, the immunity — happened in the courts, interpreting words that Congress wrote in response to Klan terror during Reconstruction.

The wound is still there. The promise is still there. The argument over whether the promise is being kept is the argument over qualified immunity. And that argument is the argument over what §1983 means — which is the argument over whether the federal government will enforce the Fourteenth Amendment against state actors who violate it, or whether state actors will be effectively immune from accountability for constitutional violations.

That is the standard this Citizen carries. Not just the current text. The wound. The promise. The dormancy. The revival. The battlefield. All of it.

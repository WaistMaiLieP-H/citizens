# Pulliam v. Allen, 466 U.S. 522 (1984)

## The Case

Gladys Pulliam was a magistrate in Bland County, Virginia. She had a practice of incarcerating people who could not post bond for nonjailable offenses — misdemeanors that carried no jail time under Virginia law. Richmond Allen and Jesse Nicholson were among those jailed. They sued Pulliam under §1983, seeking injunctive relief and attorney's fees under §1988.

Pulliam claimed absolute judicial immunity barred both the injunctive relief and the fee award.

## What the Court Held

Justice Blackmun, writing for the majority:

1. **Absolute judicial immunity does not bar injunctive relief under §1983.** The common-law immunity that Pierson v. Ray imported into §1983 was an immunity from damages, not from all relief. Injunctive relief is prospective — it tells the judge to stop the unconstitutional conduct going forward. It does not impose personal financial liability for past acts.

2. **Attorney's fees under §1988 may be awarded against a judge who is the target of a successful §1983 injunctive relief action.** Since injunctive relief is available, the fee provision follows.

## The Rule Pulliam Stands For

**Judges are not immune from injunctive relief under §1983.** Their absolute immunity protects them from damages liability only. A court can order a judge to stop violating the Constitution.

## What Happened Next: The 1996 Legislative Override

Congress effectively overruled Pulliam twelve years later. The Federal Courts Improvement Act of 1996 (Pub. L. 104-317, §309(c)) added the judicial immunity exception clause to §1983:

> "except that in any action brought against a judicial officer for an act or omission taken in such officer's judicial capacity, injunctive relief shall not be granted unless a declaratory decree was violated or declaratory relief was unavailable."

This is the only time Congress has written an immunity into the text of §1983. Every other immunity — qualified immunity for executive officials, absolute immunity for prosecutors, absolute immunity for legislators — exists only in case law. The 1996 amendment made judicial injunctive immunity statutory, superseding Pulliam's holding.

## Why This Case Still Matters

Pulliam matters even though it was legislatively overridden, for two reasons:

1. **The override itself is evidence.** When Congress disagreed with the Court's reading of §1983, Congress amended the statute. Congress has never amended §1983 to codify qualified immunity for executive officials. The contrast between legislative action (judicial immunity override) and legislative inaction (qualified immunity left uncodified) is argued as evidence that qualified immunity lacks congressional endorsement.

2. **The override is not total.** The 1996 amendment still permits injunctive relief against judges in two circumstances: (a) when a declaratory decree was violated, and (b) when declaratory relief was unavailable. Pulliam's reasoning still governs in those two carve-out situations.

## Statute Version the Court Was Reading

42 USC §1983 (post-1979 D.C. amendment, pre-1996 judicial immunity amendment). Pulliam was reading the statute WITHOUT the judicial immunity exception — that clause was enacted in response to this case.

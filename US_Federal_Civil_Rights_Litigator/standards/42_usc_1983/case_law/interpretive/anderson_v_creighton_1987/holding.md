# Anderson v. Creighton, 483 U.S. 635 (1987)

## The Case

FBI Agent Russell Anderson led a warrantless search of the Creighton family's home in Vadnais Heights, Minnesota, looking for a suspected bank robber. The Creightons — Sarisse and Robert, with their children present — sued Anderson under Bivens and §1983 for Fourth Amendment violations.

Anderson claimed qualified immunity. The Eighth Circuit denied it, reasoning that the Fourth Amendment right to be free from warrantless searches was clearly established.

## What the Court Held

Justice Scalia, writing for the majority:

1. **The "clearly established" right must be defined at a specific level of factual particularity, not at a high level of generality.** It is not enough to say "the right to be free from unreasonable searches is clearly established." The question is whether it was clearly established that **this particular type of search, under these circumstances**, was unreasonable.

2. **The contours of the right must be sufficiently clear that a reasonable official would understand that what he is doing violates that right.** The test is not whether the abstract right exists — it almost always does — but whether the specific application of the right to the facts at hand was beyond debate at the time of the conduct.

## The Rule Anderson Stands For

**Qualified immunity's "clearly established" prong requires a prior case addressing the specific factual context, not just the general constitutional principle.** A right can be clearly established in the abstract (warrantless searches violate the Fourth Amendment) while remaining uncertain in its specific application (does this particular set of exigent circumstances justify a warrantless entry?). The officer gets immunity unless the specific application was clearly established.

## Why This Case Matters

Anderson is where qualified immunity became truly powerful as a defense. After Harlow replaced the subjective good-faith test with the objective "clearly established" test, the question became: how specifically does the right need to be established? Anderson answered: very specifically. This created what critics call the "clearly established" ratchet — each new variation of misconduct can be distinguished from prior precedent, leaving the officer immune.

Combined with Pearson v. Callahan's holding that courts can skip the merits (Step 1) and go straight to "clearly established" (Step 2), Anderson's specificity requirement means courts can grant immunity for novel fact patterns without ever creating the precedent that would make the conduct clearly unlawful for the next officer who does it.

## Statute Version the Court Was Reading

42 USC §1983 (post-1979, pre-1996). The case technically involved a Bivens claim against a federal officer, but the Court applied the same qualified immunity standard that governs §1983.

MONELL v. DEPARTMENT OF SOCIAL SERVICES OF CITY OF NEW YORK
436 U.S. 658 (1978)
United States Supreme Court
Decided: June 6, 1978

---

FACTS:

Female employees of the Department of Social Services and the Board of Education of the City of New York challenged an official city policy that compelled pregnant employees to take unpaid leave before such leave was medically required. The plaintiffs sought backpay for periods of forced leave and a declaratory judgment that the policy was unlawful.

The central question: could local governmental units — the Department of Social Services, the Board of Education, and ultimately the City of New York — be sued under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 as "persons"? Monroe v. Pape (1961) had held municipalities were not § 1983 "persons." The Court now revisited that holding.

---

HOLDING:

Justice Brennan wrote for the Court (7-2 on the core holding).

MUNICIPALITIES ARE "PERSONS" UNDER § 1983:
"We overrule Monroe v. Pape, insofar as it holds that local governments are wholly immune from suit under § 1983."

"[L]ocal governing bodies ... can be sued directly under § 1983 for monetary, declaratory, or injunctive relief where, as here, the action that is alleged to be unconstitutional implements or executes a policy statement, ordinance, regulation, or decision officially adopted and promulgated by that body's officers."

THE MONELL MUNICIPAL LIABILITY STANDARD — THREE-PART RULE:

A municipality is liable under § 1983 only when:
1. **Official Policy or Custom**: The constitutional deprivation was caused by an official policy, custom, or practice of the municipality;
2. **Moving Force**: The official policy or custom was the "moving force" behind the constitutional violation; and
3. **Not Respondeat Superior**: A municipality cannot be held liable solely because it employs a tortfeasor (respondeat superior does not apply).

"[A] municipality cannot be held liable solely because it employs a tortfeasor — or, in other words, a municipality cannot be held liable under § 1983 on a respondeat superior theory."

WHAT COUNTS AS "POLICY OR CUSTOM":
- A formal written policy adopted by the governing body
- A decision by an official with final policymaking authority
- A widespread practice that, though not authorized by written law, is "so permanent and well-settled" as to constitute a custom or usage with the force of law
- Deliberate indifference to a known pattern of constitutional violations (failure to train)

---

WHAT MONELL DID NOT RESOLVE (resolved in later cases):
1. What conduct constitutes "official policy" vs. individual employee misconduct — see Pembaur v. City of Cincinnati (1986)
2. "Failure to train" as a policy — see City of Canton v. Harris (1989)
3. "Deliberate indifference" as the standard for failure-to-train — see City of Canton
4. Single-incident liability — see Bd. of County Commissioners v. Brown (1997)

---

APPLICATION TO STEWARD'S CASES:

Monell municipal liability is essential for the § 1983 federal complaint against the Antioch Police Department and Contra Costa County:

1. **APD policy/custom**: If APD officers systematically dispatched to false domestic violence calls without independent investigation, that may constitute a custom "so well-settled" as to constitute Monell liability.
2. **Failure to train**: If APD had no adequate training on verifying caller credibility before dispatching officers to arrest a resident, deliberate indifference to that training gap is actionable under City of Canton.
3. **County social services**: DFCS/social worker policies that enabled coordinated abuse of child protective reporting to manipulate custody proceedings may constitute official county custom.
4. **Contra Costa County**: As a "person" under Monell, the County is a proper § 1983 defendant when official policy or custom caused the constitutional violations.

The key Monell allegation: the constitutional violations were not aberrant individual acts — they reflected a pattern of official conduct so well-settled that it constitutes custom with the force of law.

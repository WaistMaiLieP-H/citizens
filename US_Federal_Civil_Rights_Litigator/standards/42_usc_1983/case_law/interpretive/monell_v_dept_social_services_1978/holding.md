# Monell v. Dept of Social Services, 436 U.S. 658 (1978)

## The Case

Female employees of the New York City Department of Social Services and the Board of Education challenged a policy that required pregnant employees to take unpaid leaves of absence before medical necessity required it. They sued the City, the Department, and the Board under §1983, arguing the mandatory leave policy violated due process.

The lower courts dismissed the municipal defendants based on Monroe v. Pape's holding that municipalities are not "persons" under §1983.

## What the Court Held

Justice Brennan, writing for the majority, overruled Monroe v. Pape on the municipal liability question:

1. **Municipalities and other local government bodies ARE "persons" under §1983.** The Court re-examined the legislative history of the 1871 Act — specifically the defeat of the Sherman Amendment (which would have imposed strict vicarious liability on municipalities for acts of private persons) — and concluded that Monroe had misread the rejection of the Sherman Amendment as a rejection of all municipal liability. In fact, Congress rejected vicarious liability for private violence but did not reject liability for the municipality's own unconstitutional policies.

2. **Municipal liability requires an "official policy or custom."** A municipality cannot be held liable under §1983 solely because it employs someone who committed a constitutional violation (no respondeat superior). The violation must result from the execution of a government's policy or custom — whether made by its lawmakers, by those whose edicts or acts may fairly be said to represent official policy, or by a custom so widespread as to carry the force of law.

3. **The Sherman Amendment's defeat does not immunize municipalities — it only bars vicarious liability for acts the municipality did not cause.** The operative distinction is between municipal policy (liable) and employee misconduct (not liable unless it reflects policy).

## The Rule Monell Stands For

A municipality can be sued under §1983 when the alleged unconstitutional action implements or executes a policy officially adopted by that municipality's officers, or a governmental custom even though not formally approved through official decision-making channels. But a municipality cannot be held liable solely because it employs a tortfeasor — there is no respondeat superior under §1983.

## Statute Version the Court Was Reading

42 USC §1983 (pre-1979 amendment). Same text as Monroe read — the unchanged 1874 recodification.

## Why This Case Matters

Monell opened the door to suing cities, counties, school districts, and other local government bodies under §1983 — but only for their own policies, not for the rogue acts of their employees. This created the "Monell claim" framework: to sue a municipality, you must identify a policy, custom, or practice that caused the constitutional violation.

The practical consequences were enormous. Before Monell, suing a police officer for misconduct meant collecting from the officer personally — a judgment against an individual officer is often uncollectible. After Monell, plaintiffs could reach the municipal treasury if they could show the misconduct reflected official policy. This made §1983 a viable enforcement mechanism rather than a symbolic one.

# Pearson v. Callahan, 555 U.S. 223 (2009)

## The Case

Afton Callahan was arrested in a drug sting. A confidential informant wearing a wire entered Callahan's home, bought drugs, and gave a signal. Officers entered the home without a warrant under the "consent-once-removed" doctrine — the theory that the informant's valid entry and observation of criminal activity authorized police to enter without independent consent or a warrant.

Callahan sued the officers under §1983 for Fourth Amendment violations. The officers claimed qualified immunity.

## What the Court Held

Justice Alito, writing for a unanimous Court:

1. **The two-step qualified immunity sequence established in Saucier v. Katz (2001) is no longer mandatory.** Saucier had required courts to address the constitutional merits first (Step 1: was there a constitutional violation?) before turning to whether the right was clearly established (Step 2). Pearson overruled Saucier's mandatory sequencing.

2. **Courts may address either step first, or may skip Step 1 and resolve the case on Step 2 alone.** If the right was not clearly established at the time, the court can grant qualified immunity without deciding whether the conduct was actually unconstitutional.

## The Rule Pearson Stands For

The qualified immunity analysis has two steps, but courts can take them in either order and can resolve the case on either step alone:
- **Step 1:** Did the officer's conduct violate a constitutional right?
- **Step 2:** Was the right clearly established at the time of the conduct?

Courts may skip straight to Step 2 if it is easier to resolve.

## Why This Case Matters

The practical effect of Pearson is that courts can dismiss §1983 cases on qualified immunity grounds without ever saying whether the officer's conduct was constitutional. This has been criticized as freezing constitutional law development: if courts never reach Step 1, they never create the precedent that would make the right "clearly established" for future cases. The result is a cycle where officers avoid liability because no prior case has held similar conduct unconstitutional, and no prior case exists because courts keep skipping Step 1.

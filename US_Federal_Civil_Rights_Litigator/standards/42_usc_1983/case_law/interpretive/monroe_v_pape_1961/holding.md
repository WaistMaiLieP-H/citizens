# Monroe v. Pape, 365 U.S. 167 (1961)

## The Case

On the morning of October 29, 1958, thirteen Chicago police officers broke into the home of James Monroe and his family. Without a warrant, without probable cause, without announcing their authority. They routed the family from bed at gunpoint. They forced them to stand naked in the living room while the officers ransacked the house. They took James Monroe to the police station. They held him for ten hours. They interrogated him about a murder. They did not bring him before a magistrate. They did not let him call a lawyer. They did not let him call his family. They released him without charges.

The Monroes sued the thirteen officers and the City of Chicago under 42 USC §1983.

## What the Court Held

Justice Douglas, writing for the majority, held:

1. **"Under color of" state law means acting with the authority of state law, even when the specific act violates state law.** The officers' conduct violated Illinois law — no warrant, no probable cause, no magistrate appearance. But they were still acting "under color of" state law because they exercised power they possessed only by virtue of their positions as state officers. An officer who beats a prisoner acts "under color of" law even though no state law authorizes the beating — because the officer could not have committed the beating without the authority of his office.

2. **§1983 provides a supplemental federal remedy; it does not require exhaustion of state remedies.** The Monroes did not have to sue in state court first. The whole purpose of §1983 was to provide a federal forum when the state itself was the source of the deprivation — requiring victims to exhaust state remedies first would defeat the statute's purpose.

3. **Municipalities are NOT "persons" under §1983 and cannot be sued.** The Court dismissed the claim against the City of Chicago, holding that Congress did not intend §1983 to impose liability on municipal corporations. (This holding was overruled seventeen years later by Monell v. Dept of Social Services, 1978.)

## The Rule Monroe Stands For

**Section 1983 is alive.** After ninety years of dormancy, the statute means what its text says: any person who uses state authority to violate constitutional rights is liable to the injured person in federal court. The plaintiff does not need to show that the state authorized the violation — only that the defendant used state authority to commit it. And the plaintiff goes straight to federal court.

## Statute Version the Court Was Reading

R.S. §1979 as codified at 42 USC §1983 (pre-1979 amendment). The text at this time did NOT include the D.C. provisions (added 1979) or the judicial immunity clause (added 1996). The Court was reading the unchanged 1874 recodification.

## Why This Case Matters to the Evolution Chain

Monroe is not just an interpretive case — it is the doctrinal event that brought §1983 back from the dead. Between 1871 and 1961, §1983 was functionally inert. The restrictive interpretation in cases like Barney v. City of New York (1904) — requiring the state statute itself, not just the officer's conduct, to be unconstitutional — made §1983 nearly impossible to invoke. Monroe overruled that restrictive reading and opened the door to modern civil rights litigation.

The modern §1983 — the one that generates tens of thousands of cases per year, that is the primary vehicle for police misconduct claims, that is the mechanism through which the Fourth, Eighth, and Fourteenth Amendments are enforced against state actors in federal court — begins with Monroe.

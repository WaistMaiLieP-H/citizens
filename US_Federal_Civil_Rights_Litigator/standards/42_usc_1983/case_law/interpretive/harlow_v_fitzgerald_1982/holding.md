# Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800 (1982)

## The Case

A. Ernest Fitzgerald was a management analyst at the Department of the Air Force who testified before Congress about cost overruns in the C-5A transport aircraft program. He was fired. He sued several White House aides, including Bryce Harlow and Alexander Butterfield, alleging they conspired to have him terminated in retaliation for his congressional testimony — a First Amendment violation.

The case raised the question of what standard of immunity applied to senior executive officials.

## What the Court Held

Justice Powell, writing for the majority, transformed qualified immunity from a subjective test (Pierson's "good faith and probable cause") into an **objective test**:

1. **Government officials performing discretionary functions are shielded from liability for civil damages insofar as their conduct does not violate clearly established statutory or constitutional rights of which a reasonable person would have known.**

2. **The subjective element of qualified immunity — the officer's actual good faith — is eliminated.** Pierson had required inquiry into the officer's subjective state of mind, which created problems: it defeated the purpose of immunity as a defense from suit (not just a defense from liability) because subjective good faith could not be determined without discovery and trial. The objective test allows the immunity question to be resolved on summary judgment, sparing the officer from the burdens of discovery and trial.

3. **The test is: was the right "clearly established" at the time of the conduct?** If a reasonable officer could have believed the conduct was lawful in light of clearly established law, the officer is immune — regardless of the officer's actual beliefs or motives.

## The Rule Harlow Stands For

**Qualified immunity protects government officials from §1983 damages liability unless their conduct violates clearly established law.** The test is objective — what a reasonable official would have known — not subjective. The qualified immunity inquiry is: (1) did the official's conduct violate a constitutional right? (2) was that right clearly established at the time?

## Statute Version the Court Was Reading

42 USC §1983 (post-1979 D.C. amendment, pre-1996 judicial immunity amendment). However, the 1979 amendment was irrelevant to the case — it involved federal executive officials, not state or D.C. actors. (The case technically arose under Bivens, not §1983, but the Court held that the qualified immunity standard is the same for both.)

## Why This Case Matters

Harlow is the most consequential qualified immunity case ever decided. It established the modern framework that governs every §1983 damages case against an individual defendant. The "clearly established law" test determines whether an officer can be held personally liable, and it has been interpreted with increasing strictness — requiring not just a general principle but a precedent with closely analogous facts (see Anderson v. Creighton, Ashcroft v. al-Kidd).

The transformation from subjective to objective was a policy choice: the Court decided that protecting officials from the burdens of litigation was more important than inquiring into whether officials actually acted in good faith. This choice has been criticized as converting qualified immunity from a shield for good-faith officers into a shield for all officers except those who violate rights that have been established by prior cases with nearly identical facts.

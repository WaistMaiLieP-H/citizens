# Pierson v. Ray, 386 U.S. 547 (1967)

## The Case

Fifteen white and Black clergy members (Episcopal priests, including the Rev. Robert Pierson) traveled to Jackson, Mississippi in 1961 as part of the Freedom Rides to test the desegregation of interstate bus terminals. They entered the whites-only waiting room at the Jackson bus terminal and were arrested by police officers, including Captain Ray, for violating Mississippi's breach-of-the-peace statute. They were convicted in municipal court. The convictions were later reversed.

The clergy sued the officers under §1983 for false arrest — the arrests were made under a statute that was unconstitutional (the Supreme Court had already held in Garner v. Louisiana (1961) and Thomas v. Mississippi (1965) that breach-of-the-peace statutes could not constitutionally be applied to peaceful sit-ins and Freedom Riders).

## What the Court Held

Chief Justice Warren, writing for the majority:

1. **The common-law defense of good faith and probable cause is available to police officers sued under §1983.** The Court held that Congress, in enacting §1983, did not intend to abolish the common-law immunities that existed in 1871. At common law, a police officer was not liable for an arrest made in good faith and with probable cause, even if the person arrested was later found innocent or the statute under which the arrest was made was later held unconstitutional.

2. **Judges have absolute immunity from §1983 damages liability for acts performed in their judicial capacity.** The municipal judge who convicted the clergy was absolutely immune from damages. This absolute judicial immunity, like the officer's qualified good-faith defense, was grounded in common-law immunities that predated the 1871 Act.

## The Rule Pierson Stands For

Police officers and other executive officials sued under §1983 have a defense of **qualified immunity** — they are not liable if they acted in good faith and with probable cause (as understood at common law). Judges have **absolute immunity** from damages for judicial acts.

This was the seed of the qualified immunity doctrine. In 1967, the defense was still tied to the officer's subjective good faith — what the officer actually believed. The transformation into an objective test would come in Harlow v. Fitzgerald (1982).

## Statute Version the Court Was Reading

42 USC §1983 (pre-1979 amendment). Same 1874 recodification text.

## Why This Case Matters

Pierson is where the qualified immunity doctrine was born. The text of §1983 says nothing about immunity — it says "every person" who deprives another of rights "shall be liable." The Court read immunity into the statute by importing common-law defenses that existed in 1871. This interpretive move — reading unwritten immunities into a statute whose text provides none — is the foundational premise that qualified immunity critics challenge. If Congress intended to preserve common-law immunities, the argument goes, it would have written them into the statute. The 1996 amendment writing judicial immunity into the text (for injunctive relief only) arguably confirms this — when Congress wants an immunity in §1983, it writes one.

# Taylor v. Riojas, 141 S. Ct. 52 (2020)

## The Case

Trent Taylor was a prisoner in the Texas Department of Criminal Justice. For six days, correctional officers confined him in two cells that were, by any human measure, uninhabitable:

**Cell 1:** The walls were covered with "ichassive amounts" of feces — human feces smeared on the walls, the floor, the ceiling, and the water faucet. The feces were so thick that Taylor could not find a clean place to sit or stand. He could not eat without risk of contamination. He was held in this cell for four days.

**Cell 2:** Taylor was moved to a cell that was "frigidly cold" and flooded with raw sewage on the floor. He had no bunk — he was forced to sleep on the sewage-covered floor. He was naked because his clothes had been taken. He was held in this cell for two days.

Taylor sued the officers under §1983, alleging Eighth Amendment violations (cruel and unusual punishment). The Fifth Circuit granted qualified immunity because Taylor could not identify a prior case with sufficiently similar facts.

## What the Court Held

Per curiam (unsigned opinion of the Court), the Supreme Court reversed:

1. **No reasonable correctional officer could have concluded that it was constitutionally permissible to house an inmate in conditions that involved feces covering the walls for four days and raw sewage on the floor for two days.** The Court did not need a prior case with identical facts because the constitutional violation was so obvious.

2. **The "clearly established" prong does not require a prior case directly on point when the unconstitutionality of the officer's conduct is "beyond debate."** The standard is not mechanical: courts do not need factual near-identities in precedent when the conduct is so egregious that no reasonable officer could have thought it was constitutional.

## The Rule Taylor Stands For

**Qualified immunity does not apply when the violation is so obvious that no reasonable officer could have believed the conduct was lawful, even without a prior case involving materially similar facts.** This is sometimes called the "obvious clarity" or "obvious case" exception to the specificity requirement.

## Why This Case Matters

Taylor is the Court's most significant recent pushback against the increasing specificity requirements of qualified immunity. The Fifth Circuit had required Taylor to find a prior case involving feces-covered cells to defeat immunity — implying that each new variety of abuse requires its own prior precedent before an officer can be held liable. The Supreme Court rejected that approach: some conduct is so far beyond any reasonable understanding of the Constitution that precedent is unnecessary.

Taylor does not overhaul qualified immunity. It carves out an exception at the extreme. But it establishes that the doctrine has a floor — there is conduct so egregious that "clearly established" is satisfied by the constitutional text alone, without case-law specificity. The Eighth Amendment's prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment means what it says, and an officer who confines a human being in human waste for six days cannot claim they didn't know it was unconstitutional.

## Statute Version the Court Was Reading

42 USC §1983 (current text, post-1996). The text was not at issue; the case turned entirely on the qualified immunity doctrine.

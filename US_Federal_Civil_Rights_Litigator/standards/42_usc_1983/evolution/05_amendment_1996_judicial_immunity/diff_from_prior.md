# §1983 Evolution: 1979 Text → Pub. L. 104-317 (1996)

## What Changed

One clause added — the judicial immunity exception:

- **Added after "for redress":** ", except that in any action brought against a judicial officer for an act or omission taken in such officer's judicial capacity, injunctive relief shall not be granted unless a declaratory decree was violated or declaratory relief was unavailable."

## What This Means

Before 1996, judicial officers sued under §1983 were protected by absolute judicial immunity from **damages** (established by the Supreme Court in Pierson v. Ray, 1967; refined in Stump v. Sparkman, 1978), but could still be subject to **injunctive relief** — a court order directing the judge to stop the unconstitutional conduct.

The 1996 amendment restricted injunctive relief against judges to two situations:
1. The judge violated an existing declaratory decree (i.e., a court had already declared the judge's conduct unconstitutional, and the judge kept doing it).
2. Declaratory relief was unavailable (i.e., the plaintiff couldn't get a declaration in the first place, so injunctive relief was the only remaining remedy).

## Why This Was Enacted

The Federal Courts Improvement Act of 1996 (Pub. L. 104-317) was a package of federal court reforms. Section 309(c) addressed a concern raised by the federal judiciary: that §1983 injunctive relief actions were being used to collaterally attack judicial decisions through the device of suing the judge rather than appealing the ruling. The amendment channeled litigants toward declaratory relief (which says what the law is) before injunctive relief (which orders a person to act or stop acting).

## Doctrinal Significance

This is the only provision in the text of §1983 itself that limits the remedy. All other immunity doctrines — qualified immunity, absolute prosecutorial immunity, absolute legislative immunity — are judicially created. They exist in case law, not in the statutory text. The 1996 amendment is therefore unique: it is the one time Congress chose to write an immunity into §1983 rather than leaving it to the courts.

This matters because it implies that the other immunities are not legislatively mandated — they are judicial constructions that Congress could override. Congress chose to codify judicial immunity for injunctive relief; it has never chosen to codify qualified immunity. That silence is argued by qualified immunity critics as evidence that Congress does not endorse the doctrine.

## Current Text After This Amendment

This is the current text. No amendments have been enacted since 1996.

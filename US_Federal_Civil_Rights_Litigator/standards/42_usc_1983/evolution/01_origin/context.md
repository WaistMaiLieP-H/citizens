# 42 USC §1983 — Origin Context

## The Wound

By 1871, the promise of Reconstruction was being murdered in the streets. The Thirteenth Amendment (1865) had abolished slavery. The Fourteenth Amendment (1868) had guaranteed equal protection and due process. The Fifteenth Amendment (1870) had guaranteed the right to vote regardless of race. But the amendments were paper without enforcement, and the former Confederate states knew it.

The Ku Klux Klan and similar organizations — the Knights of the White Camelia, the White League, the Red Shirts — were conducting a systematic campaign of terror against Black citizens and white Republicans across the South. They murdered elected officials. They burned schoolhouses. They whipped, tortured, and killed freedmen who voted, who testified in court, who held office, who owned property. They did this openly, and the state governments — controlled by former Confederates who had regained power through exactly this violence — refused to prosecute them.

The wound was not merely that private actors committed violence. The wound was that **state actors were complicit** — sheriffs who refused to investigate, judges who dismissed cases, legislators who passed Black Codes and vagrancy statutes designed to re-enslave, governors who pardoned convicted Klansmen. The state itself had become the instrument of the deprivation. The Fourteenth Amendment's promise of equal protection was being nullified not by constitutional deficiency but by state-level refusal to enforce it.

## The Congressional Response

President Ulysses S. Grant sent a message to Congress on March 23, 1871, describing conditions in the South:

> "A condition of affairs now exists in some States of the Union rendering life and property insecure and the carrying of the mails and the collection of the revenue dangerous. The proof that such a condition of affairs exists in some localities is now before the Senate."

The 42nd Congress responded with three Enforcement Acts. The third — the Act of April 20, 1871, known as the Ku Klux Klan Act — was the most sweeping.

**Section 1** (what became §1983) created a **federal civil cause of action** against any person who, acting under color of state law, deprived any person of constitutional rights. This was revolutionary: it meant that when the state itself was the instrument of deprivation, the injured person could bypass the state courts entirely and sue in federal court.

**Section 2** (what became §1985) addressed conspiracies to deprive persons of equal protection.

**Sections 3 and 4** gave the President power to suspend habeas corpus and use military force to suppress insurrection (these provisions were temporary and expired).

## Why Section 1 Was Necessary

Before the 1871 Act, a person whose constitutional rights were violated by a state actor had no federal remedy. Federal courts had no jurisdiction over state officials acting under state authority. The victim's only recourse was the state court system — the same system whose officials had committed the violation. This was not an oversight; it was the structure of federalism as understood before the Civil War.

The Fourteenth Amendment changed the constitutional relationship between the federal government and the states, but it did not create an enforcement mechanism. Section 1 of the 1871 Act was that mechanism. It said: when a state actor violates the Constitution, the injured person can sue that actor directly in federal court, regardless of what state law says — "any such law, statute, ordinance, regulation, custom, or usage of the State to the contrary notwithstanding."

## Key Features of the Original Text

1. **"Under color of any law, statute, ordinance, regulation, custom, or usage of any State"** — Note the word "law" appears in the 1871 text but was dropped in the 1874 Revised Statutes recodification. The original text also says "of any State" only — no Territory, no District of Columbia.

2. **"Any person within the jurisdiction of the United States"** — The 1871 text protects "any person," not "any citizen." This was deliberate — the Fourteenth Amendment's Equal Protection Clause applies to "any person within [the state's] jurisdiction," not just citizens. The 1874 recodification changed this to "any citizen of the United States or other person within the jurisdiction thereof" — expanding the explicit scope while adding the "citizen" category.

3. **"Secured by the Constitution of the United States"** — The original protects only constitutional rights. The phrase "and laws" was added later, extending §1983 to cover rights secured by federal statutes as well.

4. **"Any such law... of the State to the contrary notwithstanding"** — This "notwithstanding clause" was the operational heart of Section 1. It meant that state law could not authorize or excuse the deprivation. This clause was dropped in later codifications as legally redundant (the Supremacy Clause already provides this), but its presence in the original shows the drafters' intent: they wanted no ambiguity about federal supremacy over state complicity.

5. **Venue in "the several district or circuit courts of the United States"** — The original statute specified federal court jurisdiction directly. This procedural language was later separated out into the jurisdictional statute at 28 USC §1343.

6. **Cross-reference to the Civil Rights Act of 1866** — The original Section 1 explicitly incorporated the procedural framework of the 1866 Act (14 Stat. 27), which had been enacted under the Thirteenth Amendment. This linkage tied the 1871 Act's Fourteenth Amendment enforcement to the existing Thirteenth Amendment enforcement machinery.

## The Promise

Section 1 of the 1871 Act made a promise: **when the state itself becomes the instrument of injustice, the federal courts will provide a remedy.** This promise was made in response to a specific historical catastrophe — the systematic terrorization of Black citizens and Republican officeholders by state-complicit violence during Reconstruction. But the promise was written in universal terms: "any person" deprived of "any rights, privileges, or immunities secured by the Constitution."

The universality was the design. The wound was particular — Klan terror and state complicity in the post-Civil War South. But the promise was general — whenever any state actor violates any constitutional right of any person, that person has a federal remedy.

This promise then went dormant for ninety years.

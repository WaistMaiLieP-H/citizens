# §1983 Evolution: 1871 → 1874 Recodification

## What happened

In 1874, Congress enacted the Revised Statutes of the United States — the first official codification of all general and permanent federal statutes. The 1871 KKK Act §1 was codified as **Revised Statutes §1979**, under Title XXIV (Civil Rights).

This was not an "amendment" — Congress was not changing the law. It was reorganizing the entire body of federal statutory law from a scattered pile of session laws into a single organized code. But the recodification process itself introduced textual changes to §1, some of which had lasting doctrinal consequences.

## Textual changes (1871 §1 → R.S. §1979)

| Feature | 1871 Original | 1874 R.S. §1979 | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| "under color of" | "any law, statute, ordinance, regulation, custom, or usage" | "any statute, ordinance, regulation, custom, or usage" | **Dropped "law."** "Statute" was treated as encompassing "law" — functionally no doctrinal change. |
| Geographic scope | "of any State" | "of any State or Territory" | **Added Territory.** Extended coverage to US territories. |
| Protected persons | "any person within the jurisdiction of the United States" | "any citizen of the United States or other person within the jurisdiction thereof" | **Added "citizen" category.** Changed "jurisdiction of the United States" to "jurisdiction thereof" (i.e., jurisdiction of the State/Territory). Scope appears equivalent but phrasing is more precise. |
| Rights protected | "secured by the Constitution of the United States" | "secured by the Constitution and laws" | **Added "and laws."** This is the single most consequential change. The original 1871 text protected only constitutional rights. The 1874 recodification extended §1983 to cover rights secured by federal statutes. This expansion opened §1983 to the vast body of federal statutory rights (ADA, Title IX, Medicaid, etc.) — an expansion that became transformative in the late 20th century. |
| Notwithstanding clause | "any such law... of the State to the contrary notwithstanding" | (removed) | **Dropped.** Legally redundant with the Supremacy Clause, but its removal erased an explicit textual declaration of intent. |
| Venue specification | "the several district or circuit courts of the United States" with full procedural cross-reference to 1866 Act | (removed — venue handled separately) | **Separated out.** Jurisdiction was moved to what eventually became 28 USC §1343. |
| Remedies | "action at law, suit in equity, or other proper proceeding for redress" | (retained identically) | No change. |

## The "and laws" expansion

The addition of "and laws" to "secured by the Constitution **and laws**" is the single most important textual change in §1983's history. It happened not as a deliberate policy choice by Congress, but as a byproduct of the codification process. Whether the codifiers intended to expand the statute's reach or considered "and laws" to be implicit in the original is debated. What matters for the corpus is: **the operative positive law from 1874 forward says "and laws."**

This two-word addition eventually enabled:
- **Maine v. Thiboutot** (1980) — SCOTUS held that "and laws" means ALL federal laws, not just civil rights laws
- The explosion of §1983 litigation to enforce Medicaid, education, welfare, and other statutory rights against state officials
- The entire body of "§1983 statutory claims" that makes up a substantial fraction of modern federal civil rights practice

## Source

Revised Statutes of the United States, 1st Edition (1875), Title XXIV, §1979.

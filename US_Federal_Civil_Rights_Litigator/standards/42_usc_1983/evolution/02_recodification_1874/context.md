# Revised Statutes §1979 (1874) — Context

## What Happened

In 1874, Congress authorized and published the Revised Statutes of the United States — the first official codification of all permanent general federal statutes. The project consolidated the scattered statutory fabric of the republic, which up to that point existed only as individual acts in the Statutes at Large, into an organized topical code.

Section 1 of the 1871 Ku Klux Klan Act was recodified as Revised Statutes §1979, under Title XXIV (Civil Rights). The recodification was directed to be non-substantive — same legal force as the original acts — but the codifiers made textual choices that quietly altered the statute's reach (see diff_from_prior.md).

## The Dormancy Begins

The timing of the recodification coincided with the collapse of Reconstruction. The Compromise of 1877 withdrew federal troops from the South. The former Confederate states systematically dismantled the political and civil rights gains of the Reconstruction era. Federal enforcement of the civil rights statutes effectively ceased.

R.S. §1979 sat on the books, technically in force, practically dead. For ninety years it was invoked in only a handful of cases, and the Supreme Court's interpretation in cases like Barney v. City of New York (1904) — which held that §1983 required the state statute itself to be unconstitutional, not merely that a state officer acted unconstitutionally under it — made the statute nearly impossible to use.

The statute waited. The promise made in 1871, in response to the blood of Reconstruction, sat dormant in the Revised Statutes from 1874 to 1961.

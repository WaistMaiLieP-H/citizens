# Monroe v. Pape (1961) — The Revival

## Citation
365 U.S. 167 (1961). Decided February 20, 1961. Opinion by Justice Douglas.

## What happened in the case

On the morning of October 29, 1958, thirteen Chicago police officers broke into the home of James Monroe, a Black man, and his family. They did not have a warrant. They routed the family from bed, made them stand naked in the living room while the officers ransacked the home. They then took James Monroe to the police station, held him for ten hours on "open" charges, interrogated him about a murder, and refused to allow him to call his family or an attorney. He was then released without charges.

The officers acted under no warrant, no court order, no statute authorizing what they did. Under Illinois law, their conduct was illegal — a series of torts and crimes. But Monroe did not sue in Illinois state court. He sued in federal court under R.S. §1979 (42 USC §1983), claiming that the officers had deprived him and his family of their Fourth Amendment rights (unreasonable search and seizure) and Fourteenth Amendment rights (due process).

## The question

Did the officers act "under color of" state law within the meaning of §1983? The lower courts said no — the officers' conduct was not authorized by Illinois law, so they were not acting "under color of" Illinois law. They were simply breaking the law, like any private criminal.

## The holding

The Supreme Court reversed, 8-1. Justice Douglas held that "under color of" state law means **misuse of power possessed by virtue of state authority** — not just action formally authorized by the state. An officer who uses his badge, his authority, his position to violate rights is acting "under color of" state law even if the state has not authorized and would not authorize the specific violative act.

Douglas traced the phrase back to the 1871 congressional debates and showed that the 42nd Congress meant to reach precisely this situation: state officers who abuse their authority. If "under color of" required formal state authorization, the statute would be useless — states do not pass laws authorizing constitutional violations. The whole point of §1 was to provide a remedy against officers who use the power of their office to violate rights, regardless of whether the state approves.

## Why this was revolutionary

Before Monroe, §1983 was functionally dead. Between 1871 and 1920, only 21 cases were filed under it. The narrow reading of "under color of" — requiring formal state authorization — meant the statute applied only to the narrow case where a state legislature had passed a law that facially required a constitutional violation. That almost never happens.

Monroe opened the door. After 1961, any state or local officer who violated constitutional rights while exercising the authority of office could be sued under §1983 in federal court. The statute went from near-dormancy to the most-litigated provision in federal civil rights law.

The numbers tell the story:
- 1960: fewer than 300 §1983 cases filed in federal court
- 1970: approximately 4,000
- 1980: approximately 14,000
- 2000: approximately 40,000+

## The one thing Monroe got wrong

Monroe also held that municipalities were **not** "persons" subject to suit under §1983. This was overruled by Monell v. Department of Social Services (1978), which held that municipalities ARE persons under §1983 but can be held liable only for constitutional violations caused by official policy or custom — not respondeat superior.

# 42 USC 1985 — Subsection-by-Subsection Analysis

## Origin

All three subsections of 1985 come from Section 2 of the Civil Rights Act of 1871, commonly called the Ku Klux Klan Act. Congress passed it in response to organized violence against Black citizens and their allies in the Reconstruction South — night-riding Klan groups that beat, murdered, and terrorized people to prevent them from voting, holding office, testifying in court, or living as free citizens.

The statute has never been amended. The text is the same today as when it was recodified in the Revised Statutes of 1874.

---

## Section 1985(1) — Conspiracies Against Federal Officeholders

### What It Covers

If two or more people conspire to prevent someone from accepting or holding any federal office, or to prevent any federal officer from carrying out their duties, every member of that conspiracy is liable for damages to anyone harmed by the conspiracy.

### Plain Language

This subsection says: you cannot organize a group to stop people from serving in federal government or to stop federal officers from doing their jobs. If you do, anyone your conspiracy hurts can sue every conspirator for damages.

### Who It Protects

- People who have been appointed or elected to any federal office (not state or local)
- People who want to accept a federal position but are blocked by a conspiracy
- Federal officers trying to discharge their official duties

### What the Plaintiff Must Show

1. Two or more people formed a conspiracy
2. The purpose was to prevent someone from holding federal office or to prevent a federal officer from performing duties
3. The plaintiff was injured as a result

### Class-Based Animus Requirement

**Not required.** The text of 1985(1) does not contain equal protection language. The wrong is interference with federal governance, not discrimination against a class. Any conspiracy targeting a federal officeholder — regardless of motive — falls within 1985(1).

### Practical Reality

Section 1985(1) is rarely litigated. Most threats against federal officeholders are prosecuted criminally (under 18 USC 372 and related statutes). The few civil cases that cite 1985(1) typically involve organized efforts to prevent someone from carrying out specific federal functions — for example, conspiracies to prevent federal revenue officers from collecting taxes during Reconstruction, or organized interference with federal election officials.

The subsection remains available but is overshadowed by both criminal statutes and the much more frequently invoked 1985(3).

## Section 1985(2) — Witness Intimidation and Obstruction of Justice

### Structure: Two Separate Clauses

Section 1985(2) is split into two clauses that serve different purposes and have different legal requirements. The Supreme Court confirmed this split in Kush v. Rutledge (1983).

### First Clause: Protecting Federal Court Proceedings

**What it covers:** Conspiracies to —
- Deter any party or witness from attending any federal court
- Injure any party or witness because they attended or testified in federal court
- Injure any federal juror because of their verdict or because of a case they heard

**Plain language:** If someone cooperates with a federal investigation, testifies in federal court, serves on a federal jury, or even just shows up to a federal proceeding — and a group of people conspires to punish them for it or scare them out of doing it — every member of that conspiracy is liable.

**Class-based animus: NOT required.** The first clause protects the federal judicial system itself. The wrong is the interference with federal proceedings, regardless of why the conspirators did it. A conspiracy to fire someone for cooperating with a federal Medicare fraud investigation (Haddle v. Garrison) is actionable even though the motive is retaliation for being a snitch, not racial hatred.

**Key case — Haddle v. Garrison, 525 U.S. 121 (1998):** An at-will employee was fired in retaliation for cooperating with a federal grand jury investigation. The Eleventh Circuit said he had no claim because, as an at-will employee, he had no "right" to his job and therefore was not "injured in his person or property." The Supreme Court unanimously reversed. Being fired is an injury. Period. At common law, you could always sue someone who maliciously caused your employer to fire you. The at-will nature of the employment does not erase the harm.

### Second Clause: Protecting State Court Equal Protection

**What it covers:** Conspiracies to obstruct the due course of justice in any state or territory with the intent to deny any citizen the equal protection of the laws.

**Plain language:** If a group conspires to rig or obstruct a state court proceeding specifically to deny someone equal treatment because of their race, class, or other protected characteristic, every conspirator is liable.

**Class-based animus: REQUIRED.** Because this clause specifically targets denial of "equal protection of the laws," the plaintiff must show the conspirators were motivated by discriminatory hatred toward a particular class of people. This is the same requirement that applies to 1985(3) under Griffin v. Breckenridge.

**Key case — Kush v. Rutledge, 460 U.S. 719 (1983):** A personal injury plaintiff alleged that the defendants conspired to intimidate her witnesses and manufacture false evidence in her state court case. The Supreme Court unanimously held that her claim fell under the second clause of 1985(2), because the alleged obstruction targeted a state court proceeding. Since she alleged no class-based discriminatory motive — just ordinary litigation dirty tricks — her claim failed. The Court made clear that if the same conduct had targeted a federal proceeding, the first clause would have applied and no class-based animus would have been needed.

### The Kush Line

The practical takeaway from Kush is a bright line:

| Federal proceedings | State proceedings |
|---|---|
| First clause applies | Second clause applies |
| No class-based animus required | Class-based animus required |
| Protects the federal judicial system | Protects equal protection in state courts |
| Motive is irrelevant — the act is the wrong | Discriminatory motive is an element |

## Section 1985(3) — Conspiracies to Deprive Equal Protection

### What It Covers

Conspiracies to deprive any person or class of persons of the equal protection of the laws, or of equal privileges and immunities under the laws. Also covers conspiracies to prevent state authorities from giving equal protection.

### Plain Language

If a group conspires to deny someone equal treatment under the law — whether by direct violence, organized intimidation, or any other concerted action — every conspirator is liable. This reaches private actors, not just government officials.

### Class-Based Animus Requirement

**Required.** Since Griffin v. Breckenridge (1971), every 1985(3) claim must show the conspiracy was motivated by "some racial, or perhaps otherwise class-based, invidiously discriminatory animus." Without this, the statute would become a federal general conspiracy tort — which is not what Congress intended.

### Detailed Treatment

Section 1985(3) has its own deep analysis at `../42_usc_1985_3/` with full case law treatment of Griffin v. Breckenridge (1971), Bray v. Alexandria Women's Health Clinic (1993), United Brotherhood of Carpenters v. Scott (1983), and Great American Federal Savings v. Novotny (1979).

## Comparison: What Requires Class-Based Animus and What Does Not

| Subsection | Requires class-based animus? | Why? |
|---|---|---|
| 1985(1) — federal officeholders | No | Protects federal governance, not equal protection |
| 1985(2), first clause — federal proceedings | No | Protects federal judicial integrity, not equal protection |
| 1985(2), second clause — state court obstruction | Yes | Explicitly targets denial of "equal protection" |
| 1985(3) — general equal protection conspiracy | Yes | Explicitly targets deprivation of "equal protection" |

The pattern is straightforward: when the statutory text is about protecting federal institutions (officeholders, courts), no discriminatory motive is needed because the wrong is the interference itself. When the text is about equal protection, discriminatory motive is required because "equal protection" only means something when someone is being treated unequally because of who they are.

## Elements Summary

### Section 1985(1)
1. A conspiracy of two or more persons
2. Purpose: prevent someone from holding federal office OR prevent a federal officer from discharging duties
3. Injury to the plaintiff resulting from the conspiracy

### Section 1985(2), First Clause
1. A conspiracy of two or more persons
2. Purpose: deter a party/witness from attending federal court, or injure a party/witness for testifying, or injure a federal juror for their service
3. Injury to the plaintiff in person or property (includes loss of at-will employment per Haddle)

### Section 1985(2), Second Clause
1. A conspiracy of two or more persons
2. Purpose: obstruct justice in a state court
3. With intent to deny equal protection of the laws
4. Class-based discriminatory animus motivating the conspiracy
5. Injury to the plaintiff in person or property

### Section 1985(3)
1. A conspiracy of two or more persons
2. Purpose: deprive a person or class of equal protection or equal privileges and immunities
3. Class-based discriminatory animus ("some racial, or perhaps otherwise class-based, invidiously discriminatory animus")
4. An act in furtherance of the conspiracy
5. Injury to the plaintiff in person or property, or deprivation of a right or privilege

## The Statute's Architecture

Reading all three subsections together, 1985 creates a layered system:

- **1985(1)** guards the ability of the federal government to function by protecting its officers.
- **1985(2)** guards the ability of the courts to function — federal courts absolutely (first clause), and state courts when equal protection is at stake (second clause).
- **1985(3)** guards the ability of citizens to live with equal protection — the broadest provision, reaching any private conspiracy motivated by discriminatory animus.

Each successive subsection casts a wider net. Subsection (1) protects a narrow class (federal officeholders). Subsection (2) protects anyone involved in court proceedings. Subsection (3) protects anyone targeted by a discriminatory conspiracy.

The common thread is the Reconstruction purpose: organized groups using conspiracy to destroy the ability of people — especially Black citizens and their allies — to participate in government, access courts, and live under equal protection of law. Congress was fighting the Klan, and it built a statute with three angles of attack: protect the officials, protect the courts, protect the people.

# Kush v. Rutledge, 460 U.S. 719 (1983)

## The Case

Yvonne Rutledge filed a personal injury lawsuit in Illinois state court after a car accident. She alleged that the defendants — including Kush — conspired to prevent her witnesses from testifying and to manufacture false evidence against her. She brought a federal claim under 42 USC 1985(2), arguing the defendants conspired to obstruct justice and intimidate witnesses.

The question the Supreme Court took up: does the first clause of 1985(2) — which protects witnesses in federal court proceedings — require the plaintiff to show that the conspirators acted out of hatred toward a particular racial or other class? Or is the conspiracy itself enough?

## What the Court Held

Justice Stevens, writing for a unanimous Court:

1. **Section 1985(2) contains two distinct clauses, and they have different requirements.** The first clause covers conspiracies to deter witnesses from attending federal court, to injure witnesses for having testified in federal court, or to injure federal jurors. The second clause covers conspiracies to obstruct justice in state courts with the purpose of denying equal protection.

2. **The first clause (federal proceedings) does NOT require class-based animus.** It protects the integrity of the federal judicial system. A conspiracy to intimidate a federal witness or obstruct federal proceedings is actionable regardless of whether the conspirators were motivated by racial hatred or any other class-based discrimination. The wrong is the interference with federal courts, not discrimination against a class.

3. **The second clause (state court obstruction) DOES require class-based animus.** Because the second clause is specifically about denying "equal protection of the laws" or "equal privileges and immunities," the plaintiff must show the conspiracy was motivated by discriminatory hatred toward a particular class — the same requirement the Court established for 1985(3) in Griffin v. Breckenridge.

4. **Rutledge's claim failed because her allegations involved a state court proceeding, not a federal proceeding.** Her case fell under the second clause, which requires class-based animus, and she alleged no such motivation. She claimed only a garden-variety conspiracy to obstruct her personal injury case — not discrimination against any class.

## The Rule Kush Stands For

The two clauses of 1985(2) have different thresholds. If someone conspires to intimidate witnesses or obstruct proceedings in federal court, they can be sued under 1985(2) without any showing of racial or class-based discrimination. But if the conspiracy targets state court proceedings, the plaintiff must prove the conspirators were motivated by hatred toward a particular class of people — just like a 1985(3) claim.

## Why This Case Matters

Kush draws a bright line through the middle of 1985(2). Before Kush, lower courts were confused about whether all 1985 claims required class-based animus after Griffin. The answer is no — but only for the first clause of 1985(2), which protects federal proceedings regardless of motive.

This matters practically because witness intimidation and evidence tampering happen for all sorts of reasons that have nothing to do with race or class. A corrupt defendant who threatens a federal witness to hide financial fraud can be sued under 1985(2)'s first clause even though the motive is greed, not bigotry. But the same conduct aimed at a state court proceeding requires proof of discriminatory class-based purpose.

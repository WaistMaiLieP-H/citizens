# Haddle v. Garrison, 525 U.S. 121 (1998)

## The Case

Michael A. Haddle worked as an at-will employee at a healthcare company. A federal grand jury was investigating Medicare fraud at the company. Haddle cooperated with the investigation. In retaliation, his employers — Garrison and others — conspired to have him fired. They succeeded. Haddle lost his job because he was helping the federal government investigate his employer's fraud.

Haddle sued under 42 USC 1985(2), alleging a conspiracy to intimidate a witness in a federal proceeding. The Eleventh Circuit dismissed his claim, ruling that because Haddle was an at-will employee — meaning he could be fired at any time for any reason — his termination did not count as being "injured in his person or property" as the statute requires. The appeals court reasoned that since Haddle had no legal entitlement to keep his job, losing it was not a legally recognizable injury.

## What the Court Held

Chief Justice Rehnquist, writing for a unanimous Court:

1. **Firing an at-will employee in retaliation for cooperating with a federal investigation IS an injury to "person or property" under 1985(2).** The Eleventh Circuit was wrong to treat the at-will nature of the employment as disqualifying. The statute says "injured in his person or property" — and being fired causes real, concrete harm regardless of whether the employment was at-will or under contract.

2. **The common law of torts has always recognized harm from third-party interference with at-will employment.** At common law, a plaintiff could sue someone who maliciously caused their employer to fire them — even if the employment was at-will. This was the tort of "intentional interference with business relations." The Court looked at what "injured in his person or property" meant when the statute was enacted in 1871, and at common law, this kind of harm was compensable.

3. **The Eleventh Circuit confused "legal entitlement" with "legally recognizable injury."** You do not need a contractual right to your job in order to be injured when a conspiracy causes you to lose it. The injury is the loss itself — the lost wages, the disruption, the harm — not the prior legal status of the employment relationship.

## The Rule Haddle Stands For

A person who is fired from an at-will job in retaliation for cooperating with a federal investigation or testifying before a federal tribunal has been "injured in his person or property" and has standing to sue under 1985(2). The at-will nature of employment does not eliminate the injury — it just means the employer could have fired the person for other reasons. The question is whether the actual reason was a retaliatory conspiracy, not whether the plaintiff had an enforceable right to keep the job.

## Why This Case Matters

Before Haddle, some courts were dismissing 1985(2) claims by at-will employees on the theory that you cannot be "injured" by losing something you had no right to keep. This created an absurd loophole: the more vulnerable the employee (at-will, no contract, no union protection), the less protection the federal anti-conspiracy statute gave them. Haddle closed that loophole.

This is especially important because most employees in the United States are at-will. If at-will employees could not bring 1985(2) claims, the statute would protect very few of the people most likely to face retaliation for cooperating with federal investigations — people without the bargaining power to negotiate employment contracts with termination protections.

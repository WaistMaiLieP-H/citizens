# Reasoning — 42 U.S.C. § 1985 (Conspiracy to Interfere with Civil Rights — Full Statute)

**Standard ID:** USC_42_1985_CONSPIRACY_CIVIL_RIGHTS
**Layer:** 2 — Reasoning
**Status:** ACTIVE
**Filed:** 2026-04-20

---

## Why This Standard Exists — The Reconstruction Context

Section 1985 was enacted as § 2 of the Ku Klux Klan Act of 1871. The 42nd Congress was responding to a documented emergency: private conspiracies of former Confederates and their allies were systematically destroying the gains of Reconstruction. Freedmen who voted were killed. Federal and state officers who attempted to enforce civil rights laws were intimidated, threatened, and assaulted. Witnesses and jurors in federal court proceedings were threatened into silence.

The three subsections of § 1985 address three distinct categories of this organized resistance:

**§ 1985(1)** was directed at conspiracies to prevent federal officers from performing their duties — specifically, U.S. marshals and federal officials attempting to enforce Reconstruction laws who were being threatened and driven out of jurisdictions by organized violence.

**§ 1985(2)** was directed at conspiracies to corrupt federal judicial proceedings through intimidation of parties, witnesses, and jurors. Documented KKK activity included preventing freedmen from testifying and threatening jurors who voted to convict in civil rights cases.

**§ 1985(3)** — the broadest and most litigated subsection — was directed at the KKK's core activity: coordinated private conspiracies to deprive freedmen of equal protection of the laws by violence, intimidation, and threats.

---

## Why Three Separate Subsections

The three subsections are not redundant — they address distinct threats requiring distinct structural elements:

**§ 1985(1)** protects federal government function itself. No class-based animus is required; the target is federal officers regardless of the conspirators' motivations. A conspiracy to drive a federal marshal out of a jurisdiction is prohibited regardless of whether it is racially motivated.

**§ 1985(2)** has two clauses: (a) conspiracies to obstruct federal court proceedings through intimidation, and (b) conspiracies to obstruct state court proceedings with intent to deny equal protection. The first clause (federal courts) does not require class-based animus — obstructing federal courts is prohibited regardless of motivation. The second clause (state courts) requires intent to deny equal protection, which courts have interpreted to require class-based animus consistent with *Griffin v. Breckenridge*.

**§ 1985(3)** requires class-based animus explicitly — the conspiracy must be for the purpose of depriving a person or class of equal protection. This is the subsection that requires the *Griffin* elements.

---

## Why § 1985 Operates Differently from § 1983

Section 1983 requires "color of law" — state action. Section 1985 reaches purely private conspiracies. This distinction traces to different constitutional powers:

- § 1983 is grounded in the Fourteenth Amendment's prohibition on state deprivations of due process and equal protection.
- § 1985 is grounded in a combination of the Thirteenth Amendment (abolishing slavery and authorizing Congress to enforce the prohibition against private parties) and the Fourteenth Amendment privileges and immunities clause.

The difference matters practically: a racially motivated private group that conspires to prevent minorities from exercising civil rights can be reached by § 1985(3) without any government actor present. Under § 1983, the same group would be unreachable.

---

## Why *Griffin* Required Class-Based Animus

When the Supreme Court held in *Griffin v. Breckenridge* (1971) that § 1985(3) reaches private conspiracies, it could not simply hold that any private conspiracy causing injury to a person's rights is actionable under federal law. That would create a general federal tort for private conspiracies with no clear constitutional basis — Congress's Fourteenth Amendment power reaches state action; its Thirteenth Amendment power reaches badges and incidents of slavery, but not every private wrong.

The class-based animus requirement was the Court's solution: by requiring that the conspiracy be motivated by discriminatory hostility toward a class, the statute remains within Congress's constitutional power to reach private discrimination. The animus requirement is not merely a narrowing gloss — it is what makes the statute constitutionally valid.

---

## Relationship to § 1986 — Accessory Liability

Section 1986 creates a separate cause of action for persons who have knowledge of an impending § 1985 conspiracy, have the power to prevent it, and fail to act. This extends the Reconstruction-era accountability framework beyond direct conspirators to knowing bystanders with the ability to intervene. Section 1986 claims require a valid § 1985 predicate and cannot survive if the underlying § 1985 claim fails.

---

## Application in Practice

Section 1985 is most effective when used with its subsections understood separately:

- § 1985(1): interference with federal officers' duties (no animus required);
- § 1985(2), first clause: obstruction of federal judicial proceedings through intimidation (no animus required);
- § 1985(2), second clause: obstruction of state court equal protection enforcement (animus required);
- § 1985(3): private conspiracies to deprive of equal protection (animus required; *Griffin* elements apply).

In complex civil rights cases involving both private parties and government actors, § 1985 and § 1983 are frequently pled together — § 1983 for the government actors' conduct, § 1985(3) for any private conspiracy that underlies or accompanies the government misconduct.

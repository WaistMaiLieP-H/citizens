# The Arc of Federal Civil Rights Litigation

This is the story of how the federal government promised equality, abandoned that promise for ninety years, and then slowly — incompletely — tried to keep it.

---

## 1865–1871: The Promise

The Civil War ended slavery. The Thirteenth Amendment (1865) abolished it. The Fourteenth Amendment (1868) said no state could deny any person equal protection or due process. The Fifteenth Amendment (1870) said the right to vote could not be denied on account of race.

Southern states refused to honor any of it. The Ku Klux Klan ran campaigns of terror — murder, arson, intimidation — to prevent Black citizens from exercising the rights the Constitution now guaranteed. State governments either participated or looked the other way.

## 1871: The Remedy

Congress responded with the Civil Rights Act of 1871, also called the Ku Klux Klan Act. It created three provisions that still govern civil rights litigation today:

- **Section 1 (now 42 U.S.C. §1983):** Any person who, under color of state law, deprives someone of a constitutional right can be sued for damages. This is the foundation of modern civil rights litigation.
- **Section 2 (now 42 U.S.C. §1985):** Conspiracy to deprive someone of civil rights — two or more people acting together.
- **Section 3 (now 42 U.S.C. §1986):** Neglect to prevent a conspiracy — if you knew about a §1985 conspiracy and had the power to stop it but didn't.

The text of §1983 is remarkably simple: "Every person" who acts "under color of" state law to deprive someone of constitutional rights "shall be liable to the party injured." That simplicity is why it has survived 155 years.

## 1874: Recodification

The 1871 Act was recodified as Revised Statutes §§1979 and 1980. The substance did not change. The numbering is why older cases cite "Rev. Stat. §1979" instead of "42 U.S.C. §1983."

## 1877–1961: Ninety Years of Dormancy

The Compromise of 1877 settled the disputed presidential election by withdrawing federal troops from the South. Without enforcement, the Reconstruction Amendments became words on paper.

What followed:
- Jim Crow laws imposed racial segregation across the South
- The Supreme Court narrowed the civil rights statutes almost to nothing
- §1983 was effectively dead — courts required proof that the defendant acted under an actual state statute, not just misused state power
- Plessy v. Ferguson (1896) blessed "separate but equal"
- For ninety years, the federal civil rights remedy Congress created in 1871 went almost entirely unused

## 1961: Revival

**Monroe v. Pape** (1961) brought §1983 back from the dead. Chicago police officers broke into a family's home without a warrant, forced them to stand naked, ransacked the house, and detained the father for ten hours without charges. The officers were acting as police — using state authority — even though nothing they did was authorized by state law.

The Supreme Court held that "under color of" state law means misuse of power possessed by virtue of state authority. The officers carried badges and guns because the state gave them badges and guns. That they abused that power did not make them private citizens.

This single decision transformed §1983 from a dead letter into the primary vehicle for holding state actors accountable for constitutional violations.

## 1964–1976: The Legislative Expansion

Congress built on the revived framework:

- **Civil Rights Act of 1964:** Prohibited discrimination in public accommodations, employment, and federally funded programs. Title VI (federal funding) and Title VII (employment) created new enforcement mechanisms beyond §1983.
- **Voting Rights Act of 1965:** Federal enforcement of the Fifteenth Amendment — preclearance requirements, federal registrars, the tools that Reconstruction had promised but the Compromise of 1877 took away.
- **42 U.S.C. §1988 (1976):** Fee-shifting. The prevailing plaintiff in a §1983 action can recover attorney's fees. This is what makes civil rights litigation economically viable. Without it, most victims could never afford to bring suit.

## 1971: Two Expansions

**Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents** (1971): The Supreme Court created an implied cause of action against federal officers who violate constitutional rights. §1983 only reaches state actors. Bivens filled the gap — if a federal agent violates your Fourth Amendment rights, you can sue them directly under the Constitution.

**Griffin v. Breckenridge** (1971): Revived 42 U.S.C. §1985(3) — the conspiracy provision. Purely private conspiracies motivated by racial animus could be actionable. The statute was no longer limited to state-actor conspiracies.

## 1978: Municipal Liability

**Monell v. Department of Social Services** (1978): Cities, counties, and other local governments can be sued under §1983 — but only when the constitutional violation results from an official policy, custom, or practice. A single officer's misconduct does not create municipal liability unless it reflects something systemic.

This is both an expansion and a limitation. Expansion: municipalities are "persons" under §1983. Limitation: you cannot hold a city liable just because it employs the officer who violated your rights. You must prove the city itself — through its policies or customs — caused the violation.

## 1982–Present: The Qualified Immunity Battlefield

**Harlow v. Fitzgerald** (1982): The Supreme Court replaced the subjective qualified immunity test (did the officer act in good faith?) with an objective test: was the right "clearly established" at the time? If no prior case with sufficiently similar facts held the conduct unconstitutional, the officer is immune from damages.

This created the modern qualified immunity doctrine. In practice, it means:

- Officers can violate the Constitution and face no personal liability if no prior case addressed their specific conduct
- Courts often resolve qualified immunity without ever deciding whether the conduct was unconstitutional, which means the right never becomes "clearly established"
- The doctrine has been called a one-way ratchet: each grant of immunity makes future immunity more likely because the law remains unsettled

**Taylor v. Riojas** (2020): The Supreme Court pushed back, holding that some constitutional violations are so obvious that no prior case is needed. Confining an inmate in a cell covered in feces for six days violates the Eighth Amendment with "obvious clarity." But this remains the exception, not the rule.

## 1990: Disability Rights

**Americans with Disabilities Act** (1990): Title II extended the civil rights framework to disability. State and local governments cannot discriminate on the basis of disability in their programs, services, or activities. This intersects with §1983 — disability-based violations by state actors can be challenged under both the ADA and the Constitution.

## 2017–2022: Bivens Dies

**Ziglar v. Abbasi** (2017) and **Egbert v. Boule** (2022): The Supreme Court effectively killed Bivens for any new context. The 1971 implied remedy against federal officers now applies only to the specific facts of the cases already decided — Fourth Amendment unreasonable search (Bivens), Fifth Amendment gender discrimination (Davis), and Eighth Amendment inadequate medical care (Carlson). For any "new context," courts must defer to Congress.

Federal officers who violate constitutional rights in ways that do not precisely match existing Bivens cases are now functionally immune from damages suits unless Congress creates a statutory remedy. Congress has not.

## Present: Where the Fight Is

The text of §1983 has not changed since it was enacted in 1871 and recodified in 1874 (with minor amendments in 1996). The statutes are not the battlefield.

The battlefield is doctrine:
- **Qualified immunity** shields individual officers from accountability unless a prior case with nearly identical facts established the right
- **Monell** shields municipalities unless the violation resulted from official policy or custom
- **Bivens** is functionally dead for new contexts against federal officers
- **Fee-shifting under §1988** remains the economic engine that makes litigation possible

The promise of 1871 — that any person who uses state power to violate constitutional rights shall be liable — is still the law. The question, 155 years later, is whether courts will let it mean what it says.

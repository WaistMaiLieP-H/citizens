# Historical Chain — Field Act (1933 Ch. 59)
## Cal. Education Code §§ 17280 et seq. — origin: Stats. 1933, Ch. 59

---

## The wound

On March 10, 1933, the Newport-Inglewood Fault ruptured at 5:54 PM PST. Magnitude 6.4. The earthquake struck after most California schools had dismissed for the day.

The California Geological Survey documented 70 schools destroyed and approximately 120 schools damaged in the affected region — roughly 75 percent of schools in the impacted area. When investigators walked the wreckage, the conclusion was stark: school buildings had failed structurally in ways that would have been catastrophic if children had been present. Walls collapsed. Roofs fell. Buildings designed to hold hundreds of students had pancaked under lateral seismic forces that proper engineering could have resisted.

The total death toll across the earthquake was 120. Long Beach lost 52 people. Compton lost 17. Approximately two-thirds of deaths occurred when people fled buildings and were struck by falling exterior debris — a pattern that pointed directly to unreinforced construction and the absence of any requirement to design for horizontal forces.

The earthquake struck at 5:54 PM. Schools in the area were empty or nearly empty. Had the fault moved at 9 AM on a school day, the casualty count among children would almost certainly have been catastrophic. California's Legislature understood this when it convened its emergency session. The near-miss was not reassuring; it was the warning.

---

## The legislative response

Assemblyman C. Don Field introduced AB 2342 on March 23, 1933 — 13 days after the earthquake. It passed both houses by April 7 and was approved by Governor James "Sunny Jim" Rolph on April 10, 1933 — 31 days from the triggering event.

Chapter 59, Statutes of California 1933, was an urgency act. Section 9 declared it necessary for "the immediate preservation of the public peace, health, and safety," citing the earthquake series, the loss of life and property, the seriously damaged school buildings, and the need for replacement buildings to be properly constructed.

The Act vested authority in the State Division of Architecture to supervise the construction of all public school buildings. No contract for school construction could be let without prior Division approval of the plans. No public money could be paid for non-conforming work. Violations — including false statements in the required verified reports — were felonies.

Field's original intent was broader legislation covering most building types. He narrowed it to public schools to secure passage and enforceability. The Legislature followed with the Riley Act (Ch. 601, May 26, 1933) six weeks later to cover all buildings statewide.

---

## The current state

Since 1933, according to the Division of the State Architect — the Field Act's modern administering agency — there has never been a major structural failure at a public California K–12 school or community college. That outcome is the measure of the Act's effect.

The Field Act is now codified across three articles of the California Education Code: Article 3 (§§ 17280–17317, K–12 buildings), Article 6 (§§ 17365 et seq., additional K–12 requirements), and Article 7 (§§ 81130–81147, community colleges). The modern short-title provision at Education Code § 17281 names all three articles together as "the Field Act." Community colleges were added to the definition of "school building" by the first Field Act amendment, 1935 Ch. 284, which also raised the trigger threshold from $1,000 to $4,000.

The modern regulatory implementation is California Code of Regulations Title 24 (administered by the Division of the State Architect, now part of the California Department of General Services). The DSA reviews and approves all plans for new public school construction and reconstruction statewide — the same supervisory role that the 1933 Act vested in the Division of Architecture.

## Steward relevance

The Field Act is the corpus's primary example of a standard where the historical loss is precisely documented, the legislative response is primary-source-verified, and the current-state outcome is itself verified by the administering agency. It is the proof-of-concept for the five-layer bar. The structural engineer who signs school building drawings in California today is the professional embodiment of what Assembly Member Field demanded in 31 days in 1933.

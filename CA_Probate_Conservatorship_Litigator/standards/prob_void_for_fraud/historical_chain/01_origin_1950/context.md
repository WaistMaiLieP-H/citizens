# Historical Chain — Void for Fraud Doctrine in Conservatorship (1950)

## Equity Origins — Fraud on the Court

The doctrine that a court order obtained by fraud has no legal force traces to English equity jurisprudence from the 17th century. Chancery courts refused to enforce their own orders when those orders were procured through fraud, on the ground that equity will not enforce unconscionable conduct.

American courts adopted this doctrine broadly in the 19th and early 20th centuries. The California courts applied it specifically to probate and conservatorship/guardianship proceedings in a series of cases through the first half of the 20th century.

## Freese v. Bragg (1950) 98 Cal.App.2d 478

Freese v. Bragg is the California authority establishing that a conservatorship/guardianship obtained by fraud — including misrepresentations to the court in the petition — is void or voidable. The Court of Appeal held that courts of equity will not allow their processes to be used to accomplish fraud, and a judgment procured by fraud is subject to being set aside.

The case stands for the proposition that the existence of a court order does not insulate the order from collateral attack when the order was itself the product of fraud on the court.

## The Civil Code Fraud Definitions (CIV §§1572, 1573)

California Civil Code §§1572 and 1573 provide the substantive definitions of actual fraud and constructive fraud that are applied in conservatorship void-for-fraud analysis. These sections have been part of California law since 1872 (the original Civil Code codification). They provide the statutory grounding for fraud claims that do not require proof of subjective intent (constructive fraud under §1573) — critical in cases where the fraud was accomplished through a system of actors who may have had different levels of knowledge and intent.

## Current State

The void-for-fraud doctrine in conservatorship proceedings is recognized California law. It provides the basis for collateral attacks on conservatorship orders, even long after the original proceeding, when fraud is established. The SOL for such attacks runs from discovery (not from the original fraud) under the discovery rule — making the doctrine accessible even to conservatees who were controlled for decades without knowing the basis of their situation.

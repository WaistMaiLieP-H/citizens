Freese v. Bragg
98 Cal.App.2d 478 (1950)
California Court of Appeal

[Note: Full opinion text from training knowledge — verify at Google Scholar or Westlaw]

Plaintiffs challenged a conservatorship (then called guardianship under prior law)
that had been obtained through material misrepresentation to the court. The conservator
had presented false evidence to establish the proposed conservatee's incapacity.

HELD: A guardianship/conservatorship obtained through fraud on the court is void ab
initio — not merely voidable. A judgment obtained through fraud has no legal effect.
A collateral attack on the order — raising fraud as a defense or basis for relief in
any subsequent proceeding — is available without time limit.

The court distinguished between:
- Errors of fact or law in a conservatorship proceeding → voidable, corrected by appeal
- Fraud in the procurement of the court's order → void ab initio, subject to collateral attack

A void order can be attacked in any court, at any time, in any proceeding where it is
relevant. The statute of limitations does not protect a void judgment.

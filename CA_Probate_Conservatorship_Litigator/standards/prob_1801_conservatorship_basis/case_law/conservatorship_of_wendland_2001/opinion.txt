Conservatorship of Wendland
26 Cal.4th 519 (2001)
California Supreme Court

JUSTICE WERDEGAR delivered the opinion of the Court.

Robert Wendland suffered severe brain injury in a 1993 automobile accident. He was left
in a minimally conscious state — not in a persistent vegetative state — and could
communicate minimally. His wife, Rose Wendland, was appointed conservator of his person.

Rose sought court authorization to remove Robert's feeding tube. Robert's mother and
sister opposed. The question: what standard governs a conservator's decision to withdraw
life-sustaining treatment from a conservatee who is conscious but lacks decision-making
capacity?

HELD: A conservator of the person may not withhold life-sustaining treatment from a
conscious conservatee who has not clearly expressed a desire to refuse that treatment,
absent clear and convincing evidence that the conservatee would have wanted the
treatment withdrawn.

The Court rejected the substituted judgment standard as the sole basis for decision.
Where the conservatee has not clearly and convincingly expressed their own wishes, the
conservator must act in the conservatee's best interests — not the conservatee's
assumed or inferred preferences.

Key holdings:
1. A conservator's power over medical decisions is not unlimited — it is constrained by
   the conservatee's known wishes, by the standard of care, and by the requirement that
   decisions serve the conservatee's best interests.

2. The standard of clear and convincing evidence applies to the conservatee's purported
   wishes — uncorroborated family accounts of what the conservatee "would have wanted"
   are insufficient.

3. A conservatee who has regained even minimal consciousness retains dignity interests
   that the conservator must respect — the conservatorship does not eliminate the
   person's fundamental right to make their own choices to the extent they are able.

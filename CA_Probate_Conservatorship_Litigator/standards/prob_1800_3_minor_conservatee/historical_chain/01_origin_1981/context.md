# § 1800.3 Origin: The Least Restrictive Alternative as a Constitutional Requirement

§1800.3 was enacted as part of the 1981 Prob. Code reform. The least restrictive
alternative requirement (§1800.3(b)) codified the constitutional principle that
deprivation of liberty must be accomplished through the minimum necessary restriction.

The California Supreme Court and the U.S. Supreme Court both recognized in the 1970s-80s
that civil commitment to psychiatric facilities and to conservatorships involves a
deprivation of liberty protected by the Due Process Clause. The least restrictive
alternative doctrine — developed in mental health law — holds that the state cannot
impose the most restrictive available option when less restrictive means are adequate.

§1800.3(b)'s express finding requirement ensures the least restrictive alternative
analysis actually appears in the court record. Without an express finding, there is
no evidence the court performed the analysis at all.

The minor conservatee limitation (§1800.3(a)(2)) reflects the Legislature's recognition
that minors present special concerns: parental authority already exists for unmarried
minors; conservatorship of an unmarried minor's person is an extreme measure reserved
for situations where parental authority has failed (dissolved marriage context) and
adult-level legal management of the minor's affairs is required.

For an unmarried minor, the appropriate legal framework is guardianship (Prob. Code
§1500 et seq.) — not conservatorship. Guardianship for a minor is the established
mechanism; conservatorship of a minor's person requires the marital status condition
because conservatorship is designed for adults who have lost capacity, not for minors
who have not yet reached it.

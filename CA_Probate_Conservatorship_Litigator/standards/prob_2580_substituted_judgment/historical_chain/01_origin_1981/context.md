# Historical Chain — § 2580 Substituted Judgment (1981)

## The Substituted Judgment Doctrine

The substituted judgment doctrine predates California's 1981 Probate Code revision. It originated in English chancery court decisions of the 19th century and was adopted by American courts as a constitutional alternative to "best interests" decision-making for incapacitated persons.

The core distinction:
- **Best interests:** What does the court think is good for this person?
- **Substituted judgment:** What would this person have chosen for themselves?

The substituted judgment standard was adopted because "best interests" was recognized as too easily captured by the decision-maker's own preferences or values rather than the conservatee's.

## California 1981 Codification

California's 1981 Probate Code revision codified the substituted judgment doctrine in §2580 with a specific enumeration of the acts subject to court authorization. The enumeration was designed to ensure that the highest-stakes decisions — those most likely to permanently alter the conservatee's estate structure — required judicial review.

The legislative concern: without §2580, a conservator with broad estate management powers could argue that making gifts to family members, surrendering insurance policies, or changing pension beneficiaries all fell within "ordinary" management authority. Section 2580 draws a bright line: these acts require a petition and a court order.

## Current State

§2580 is actively used in California conservatorship proceedings. Real property sales, trust creation from conservatorship estates, and pension elections by conservators on behalf of conservatees all typically require §2580 petitions. When such actions occur WITHOUT a §2580 order, the legal consequence is that the act was outside the conservator's authority — potentially void and subject to recovery by the conservatee's estate.

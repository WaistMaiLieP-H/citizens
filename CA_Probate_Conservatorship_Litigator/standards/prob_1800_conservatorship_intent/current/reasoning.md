# Reasoning — Why § 1800 Exists

## The Pre-Reform Conservatorship

Before the 1981 Probate Code reform that created the modern California conservatorship framework, California used a combination of the Probate Code guardianship provisions and the LPS Act (1967) for psychiatric holds. The conservatorship system was widely documented as:
- Easily obtained by family members who simply wanted to control a relative
- Rarely reviewed — conservatorships lasted indefinitely without any court re-examination
- Used to strip rights without any ongoing accountability — once appointed, the conservator controlled everything
- Invisible to the conservatee in many cases — persons conserved who did not know proceedings had occurred

The 1981 reforms created the modern framework: mandatory investigator pre-appointment report (§1826), mandatory annual review (§1851), clear and convincing evidence standard (§1801(e)), and the legislative intent section (§1800) to constrain how the conservatorship could be used.

## §1800 as a Constraint on Conservator Power

§1800 is not procedural. It is substantive. Every act a conservator takes must be measured against these eight mandates. A conservator who:
- Uses conservatorship authority to deny the conservatee access to chosen counsel (§1800(a) violation)
- Never assesses whether the conservatee could live independently (§1800(d) violation)
- Never conducts or facilitates periodic review that considers the conservatee's expressed wishes (§1800(e) violation)
- Systematically prevents the conservatee from making or communicating their own choices (§1800(h) violation)

...is breaching the conservatorship's statutory mandate with each such act. These are not technical violations — they are the exact conduct §1800 was enacted to prohibit.

## The 2014 Addition of §1800(h)

The 2014 amendment adding §1800(h) — the retained choice mandate — followed the national Britney Spears-era (and earlier disability rights) advocacy that documented how conservatorships were being used to silence conservatees. The Legislature's explicit addition of §1800(h) confirms that the problem was well-documented and required statutory correction. Its presence in the code means courts can no longer treat the conservatee's silencing as within the conservator's discretion.

# § 13955 Origin — The Wound and the Promise (1965)

## The wound

Before 1965 California law, a crime victim who could not sue and collect from the perpetrator had no public remedy for medical bills, lost wages, funeral costs, or psychological treatment. The state's interest in crime was confined to prosecution — an offense against the state, not a wrong against the victim. Victim advocacy, as a profession and practice, did not exist. Rape crisis centers, domestic violence shelters, and crime victim advocates were all 1970s or later developments.

The practical effect: victims of serious crimes (rape, assault, homicide of a family member) carried catastrophic uncompensated costs. Medical bankruptcies were common. Widows of homicide victims lost income with no replacement. Families of victims paid funeral expenses out of pocket. Mental health treatment was privately funded or not obtained at all. Meanwhile, the state paid to prosecute the perpetrator.

The wound was **systematic state indifference to victim harm**: the legal system processed the crime as an offense against sovereign order, and the actual injured human being was treated as a witness, not a victim with claims.

## The promise

In 1965, California became the first US state to enact a crime victim compensation program. The original program was administered by the State Board of Control (which at the time handled most state claims) under former Gov. Code §§ 13959-13974. The original eligibility rule (former § 13960 or thereabouts) was substantially narrower than current § 13955:

- The crime had to occur in California (no out-of-state-resident-victim coverage)
- "Direct victim" only — derivative victim coverage was added later
- Physical injury only in most categories — emotional-injury-only coverage was a later expansion
- Shorter timeliness rules
- Lower compensation caps

The promise was: **the state will help pay for the pecuniary consequences of crime when the victim cannot otherwise recover**. The program was means-tested originally (applicants had to show financial hardship); later amendments removed the means test.

## Expansion: 1965 → 2002 → current

Over the following decades, the program expanded:

- **1970s:** Added derivative victim category (family members of homicide victims). Added mental health counseling as a compensable expense.
- **1982:** California Constitution Article I § 28 ("Victims' Bill of Rights") enacted by Prop 8. First constitutional recognition of victim rights.
- **1984:** Federal Victims of Crime Act (VOCA), 42 USC § 10601, authorized federal matching funds. California and every other state created VOCA-qualifying programs.
- **1999:** Major expansion of § 13955 (then former § 13960) to add emotional-injury-only coverage for enumerated crimes, broaden derivative victim categories, and loosen the police-report rule for domestic violence applicants.
- **2002:** Stats. 2002, ch. 1141 recodified the program. Former §§ 13959-13974 became §§ 13950-13969. Current § 13955 was enacted with the seven-element structure in force today.
- **2008:** Marsy's Law (Prop 9) significantly expanded Cal. Const. Art. I § 28, creating a robust constitutional overlay on victim rights that affects how § 13955 must be interpreted.
- **Post-2008:** Multiple amendments expanding derivative victim categories, adjusting caps for inflation, adding human trafficking and military sexual assault categories.

## The promise today

Section 13955 still delivers on the core 1965 promise: a California resident injured by crime can seek public compensation for pecuniary losses. The seven-element structure is more complex than the 1965 original but generally more inclusive — derivative victims are covered, emotional-injury-only claims for enumerated crimes are covered, residency rules are more flexible.

But the promise is now conditional on Fund solvency (§ 13950(b)), on the absence of § 13956 denial grounds, and on compliance with § 13959 procedural rules. When any of these conditions are imperfectly administered by CalVCB staff, the appeal mechanism in § 13959 is the path to reclaiming the promise.

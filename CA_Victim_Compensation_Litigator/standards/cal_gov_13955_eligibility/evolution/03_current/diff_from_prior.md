# § 13955 — Diff from 1965 origin to 2026 current

## Structural changes

- **1965 (former §§ 13959-13974):** Narrow program. Victim-only coverage. Means-tested. California crime only. Physical injury only. Shorter timelines. Lower caps.
- **2002 (current § 13955):** Seven-element structure. Victim + derivative victim + funeral/cleanup claimants. No means test. California crime OR California resident victim of any crime. Physical OR emotional injury (in enumerated-crime category). Longer timelines. Higher caps.

## Major substantive expansions since 1965

1. **Derivative victim category** (subd. (a)(2), (c)) — family members, household members, primary caretakers
2. **Non-California-crime coverage for California residents** (subd. (b)(2))
3. **Emotional-injury-only coverage for enumerated crimes** (subd. (f)(3))
4. **Child witness of DV presumption** (subd. (f)(1) last sentence)
5. **Service dog injury** (subd. (f)(4))
6. **Minor nonconsensual image distribution** (subd. (f)(5))
7. **Military sexual assault** (cross-referenced via § 13956(b)(5))
8. **Human trafficking** (subd. (f)(3) cross-reference to PC § 236.1, and § 13956(b)(4))

## What has NOT changed

- The conjunctive "all of the following requirements are met" language
- The seven-element basic structure (since 2002)
- The "except as provided in Section 13956" opening clause (carving out denial grounds as affirmative defense)
- The direct-result causation requirement
- The "pecuniary loss within the scope of § 13957-13957.7" requirement

## Practical effect

A modern § 13955 application benefits from dozens of expansions that did not exist in 1965. But the core logic is the same: the applicant must prove each conjunctive element, and the Board can deny even a qualifying application on § 13956 grounds. The appeal brief template is stable regardless of the particular expansion being invoked.

# Gov. Code § 13955 — CalVCB Eligibility Requirements

**Popular name:** CalVCB seven-element eligibility test
**Location:** Government Code, Title 2, Division 3, Part 4, Chapter 5 (Crime Victim Compensation), Article 5

## What § 13955 does

Section 13955 lists the conjunctive requirements that an applicant must meet to be eligible for CalVCB compensation. The section uses "all of the following requirements are met" language — every element must be satisfied. A failure on any one element is a sufficient ground for denial.

The seven elements:

1. **Claimant status** (subd. (a)) — victim, derivative victim, or funeral/cleanup reimbursement claimant
2. **Jurisdictional hook** (subd. (b)) — California crime OR California resident victim of any crime
3. **Derivative victim qualifications** (subd. (c)) — if applicable
4. **Timeliness** (subd. (d)) — § 13953 deadlines
5. **Direct-result causation** (subd. (e)) — injury/death was a direct result of a crime (with the motor vehicle exceptions)
6. **Qualifying injury** (subd. (f)) — physical, emotional + threat, enumerated-crime emotional, service dog, minor sexual image distribution
7. **Pecuniary loss within § 13957 scope** (subd. (g))

## The "direct result of a crime" element (e)(1)

This is the battlefield in many contested applications. "Direct result" is interpreted through CalVCB regulations (2 CCR § 649.12) and administrative practice. The statutory text does not define "direct result," and the Board has some discretion. Appellants generally argue for a broad interpretation (any injury that would not have occurred but for the crime); Boards sometimes narrow it to exclude remote consequences.

For the Hartmann application, the "direct result" element turns on whether the June 16, 2023 events qualify as a "crime" for § 13955(e) purposes. If criminal charges were filed in 04-23-01959, that supports the element. If not, the applicant must show the crime element independently. The Board is not bound by prosecutorial charging decisions.

## The qualifying-injury element (f)

Subdivision (f) requires a specific type of injury, listed in five alternative categories. For most applications, (f)(1) physical injury and (f)(3) emotional injury from an enumerated crime are the operative categories. The enumerated PC sections in (f)(3) include the violent felony list at PC § 667.5(c), which covers most serious crimes.

The Board "may presume" physical injury for a child who witnessed domestic violence — this is a rare statutory presumption operating in favor of the applicant. It is worth flagging when it applies.

## Interaction with § 13956

Section 13955 opens with "Except as provided in Section 13956" — meaning that even if an applicant satisfies all seven § 13955 elements, a § 13956 denial ground can still defeat the claim. § 13956 functions as an affirmative defense the Board can assert. An appellant must address BOTH: prove § 13955 eligibility AND negate any § 13956 denial grounds.

## Appeal framework

When a CalVCB denial is appealed under § 13959:

1. The applicant bears the burden of establishing the § 13955 elements by a preponderance of the evidence (§ 13959(c)).
2. The Board may rely on any relevant evidence (§ 13959(e)) — formal evidence rules do not apply.
3. The hearing officer applies the law in effect on the application date (§ 13959(d)).

Each § 13955 element must be separately addressed in the appeal brief. Each element that the Board rejected or failed to consider is a separate ground for reversal.

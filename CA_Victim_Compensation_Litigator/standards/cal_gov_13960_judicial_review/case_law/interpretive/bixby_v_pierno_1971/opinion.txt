BIXBY v. PIERNO
4 Cal.3d 130 (1971)
California Supreme Court
Decided: February 1971

---

FACTS:

Bixby was a licensee who challenged an administrative agency's revocation of his license via a petition for writ of mandate under CCP § 1094.5. The question was which standard of judicial review applied: the more deferential "abuse of discretion/substantial evidence" standard, or the more searching "independent judgment" standard.

---

HOLDING:

CCP § 1094.5 — THE BIXBY TWO-TRACK STANDARD OF REVIEW:

1. **The standard of judicial review depends on whether the administrative decision substantially affects a "fundamental vested right."** This is the Bixby rule — the controlling framework for California administrative law judicial review.

2. **If the decision substantially affects a fundamental vested right, independent judgment review applies.** The superior court independently re-weighs the evidence and exercises its own judgment as to what the facts and the law require. The court does not merely check whether substantial evidence supports the agency's findings.

3. **If the decision does not substantially affect a fundamental vested right, substantial evidence/abuse of discretion review applies.** The court asks only whether the agency's findings are supported by substantial evidence in the light of the whole record.

4. **"Fundamental vested right" is a fact-sensitive inquiry.** Factors include: whether the right is a traditional common-law right; whether the right has a constitutional dimension; whether the right is highly personal (liberty, property, livelihood); whether deprivation causes substantial harm.

5. **License revocation triggers independent judgment.** The loss of the right to practice a profession — a livelihood — is a fundamental vested right. By extension, the Bixby framework applies to any administrative decision affecting comparably personal and fundamental interests.

---

APPLICATION OF BIXBY TO CALVCB § 13960 WRIT PETITIONS:

Every § 13960 CalVCB writ petition must address Bixby: does the denial of victim compensation substantially affect a "fundamental vested right"?

**The argument for independent judgment in CalVCB cases:**
- Victim compensation is a statutory entitlement, not a gratuity — once eligibility is established, the applicant has a right to the award
- The Marsy's Law constitutional guarantees (Cal. Const. Art. I § 28) elevate victim rights to constitutional status — deprivation of those rights may constitute deprivation of a fundamental constitutional right
- Denial of compensation to a violent crime victim affects bodily integrity (by denying reimbursement for medical treatment), financial survival, and other highly personal interests

**Fallback — de novo review of legal questions regardless of standard:**
Even if the court applies the deferential substantial evidence standard to factual findings, the Board's legal conclusions (interpretation of § 13955, § 13956, § 13959, Marsy's Law) are reviewed DE NOVO. Where the Board applied the wrong legal standard, the court corrects the legal error without deference.

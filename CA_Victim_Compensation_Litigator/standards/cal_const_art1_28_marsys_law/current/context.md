# Cal. Const. Art. I § 28 — Marsy's Law (Context)

**Popular name:** Victims' Bill of Rights (1982); Marsy's Law (2008)
**Location:** California Constitution, Article I (Declaration of Rights), Section 28
**Enactment:** Proposition 8 (1982); substantially expanded by Proposition 9 (2008)

## What § 28 does

Article I § 28 establishes enforceable constitutional rights for crime victims in California. The section has two layers:

1. **The 1982 Victims' Bill of Rights (Prop 8)** — enacted the original victim rights framework, including the "truth-in-evidence" rule (§ 28(f)(2)), the restitution right, and a foundational commitment to victim participation in the criminal justice process.

2. **Marsy's Law (Prop 9, 2008)** — substantially expanded § 28(b) to enumerate 17 specific rights and strengthened enforcement mechanisms. "Marsy" is Marsy Nicholas, a UC Santa Barbara student murdered in 1983; her family funded the Prop 9 campaign after experiencing what they described as mistreatment by the criminal justice system.

The 17 enumerated rights in § 28(b) are the most important operational provisions. They cover:

- **Fairness and dignity** (§ 28(b)(1))
- **Protection from defendant** (§ 28(b)(2), (3))
- **Privacy of records and communications** (§ 28(b)(4))
- **Refusal of defense discovery** (§ 28(b)(5))
- **Information and participation rights** (§ 28(b)(6)-(12), (15), (16))
- **Restitution** (§ 28(b)(13))
- **Property return** (§ 28(b)(14))
- **Right to be informed of rights** (§ 28(b)(17))

## How § 28 interacts with CalVCB

CalVCB is a compensation program, not a criminal prosecution. But § 28's rights apply to "the criminal or juvenile justice process" broadly, and case law has extended at least some of the rights to related administrative processes. The overlap with CalVCB is strongest in these areas:

1. **§ 28(b)(1) — fairness, respect, dignity.** This right applies throughout the victim's engagement with the criminal justice system, including the CalVCB process. A CalVCB denial that fails to treat the applicant with fairness and dignity — for example, mechanically applying denial grounds without considering trauma circumstances — is arguably a violation.

2. **§ 28(b)(13) — restitution.** The constitutional right to restitution is directly relevant to CalVCB compensation, which is sometimes characterized as a form of publicly-funded restitution when the perpetrator cannot pay.

3. **§ 28(b)(1) privacy / § 28(b)(4) confidential information.** If CalVCB discloses an applicant's confidential records without authorization, that is a § 28(b)(4) violation.

4. **§ 28(b)(17) — right to be informed of rights.** If CalVCB failed to inform the applicant of their Marsy's Law rights — as required by the Marsy's Law Card distribution provisions — the failure is an independent appeal ground.

## Enforcement mechanisms

Subdivision (c) provides that the rights in § 28(b) are enforceable by the victim, the victim's lawful representative, and the prosecuting attorney in trial or appellate courts. This is a constitutional enforcement clause — the rights are not merely aspirational.

For CalVCB purposes, enforcement can take several forms:

1. **Direct assertion at the § 13959 hearing** — argue that CalVCB's handling violated specific § 28(b) rights.
2. **Assertion at reconsideration under § 13959(i)** — argue the initial decision violated Marsy's Law.
3. **CCP § 1094.5 writ of mandate** — argue the final CalVCB decision violates Marsy's Law as a matter of constitutional error.
4. **Independent civil action** — § 28(c) may support a direct civil action for declaratory or injunctive relief. Case law on this is limited.

## The Marsy's Law Card

Following Prop 9, California adopted a "Marsy's Law Card" — a written statement of the 17 rights that is distributed to crime victims at specific points in the criminal justice process. The Card is a constitutional-level compliance document. Failure to provide the Card is a failure to inform the victim of their rights under § 28(b)(17).

For A25-10117946, the relevant question is: **was the applicant provided the Marsy's Law Card or equivalent notification at the CalVCB application stage?** If not, that is a constitutional violation and an independent appeal ground.

## Relevance to A25-10117946

The Marsy's Law overlay gives the Hartmann appeal a **constitutional dimension** beyond the statutory and regulatory arguments:

1. **Fairness and dignity (§ 28(b)(1))** — frame the mechanical denial as a dignity violation
2. **Right to be informed (§ 28(b)(17))** — argue CalVCB failed to inform the applicant of their rights
3. **Right to restitution (§ 28(b)(13))** — frame the denial of compensation as a failure of the constitutional restitution commitment
4. **Privacy and confidentiality (§ 28(b)(4))** — if confidential records were mishandled
5. **Victim advocacy failures** — the "advocacy" concept in the 13 FAIL audit findings maps onto the broader § 28(b)(1) dignity framework

Marsy's Law is a high-value ground for reversal because:
- It is constitutional, so it survives any statutory interpretation dispute
- It is enforceable under § 28(c) explicitly
- It gives the appeal a rhetorical frame (a crime victim denied their constitutional rights) that is politically and practically compelling
- It is harder for the Board to rebut than statutory arguments because the rights are foundational

## Limitations

Marsy's Law has important limits:

- Many of the rights (§ 28(b)(2)-(12), (15)-(16)) are keyed to the criminal prosecution context, not to the civil compensation context. Their applicability to CalVCB proceedings is an argument the applicant must make, not a settled doctrine.
- Case law enforcing Marsy's Law in the administrative compensation context is thin.
- The California Supreme Court has sometimes construed § 28 narrowly when it conflicts with the accused's federal constitutional rights. This is less of an issue in CalVCB proceedings (which do not directly involve the accused) but the narrow-construction tradition is relevant.

Despite these limits, the rhetorical and doctrinal value of Marsy's Law in the appeal is significant. Every CalVCB appeal should include a Marsy's Law argument alongside the statutory arguments.

# Historical Chain — Cal. Const. Art. I § 28 (Victims' Bill of Rights / Marsy's Law)

## The wound

Before 1982, California crime victims had no constitutional status in the criminal justice process. They were treated as witnesses — the prosecution's witnesses, specifically, and only to the extent the prosecutor chose to involve them. Victims had no right to information about the case, no right to be present at hearings, no right to be heard on bail, plea, sentencing, or parole. Their property was held indefinitely as "evidence." Their privacy was routinely violated by defense discovery. Their safety was a secondary concern to the accused's constitutional rights under the federal and state constitutions.

The wound was **victim invisibility in the criminal justice process**: a person injured by crime had extensive obligations (appear as witness, testify, face cross-examination, relive the trauma) but no enforceable rights.

## The promise (1982)

Proposition 8, the "Victims' Bill of Rights," was placed on the 1982 California ballot by initiative. It amended the California Constitution in multiple respects, including adding § 28 to Article I. The original § 28 was narrower than today's — it focused primarily on:

- Truth-in-evidence rule (§ 28(f)(2)) — previously-excluded evidence about defendants' prior convictions could be admitted
- Restitution right for victims
- Foundational commitment to victim participation

Prop 8 passed with a strong majority and was implemented over the following years. The California Supreme Court construed some provisions narrowly (particularly the truth-in-evidence rule), which limited Prop 8's practical effect.

## The expansion (2008)

Proposition 9, "Marsy's Law," was placed on the 2008 ballot by initiative. It was funded largely by Dr. Henry Nicholas, whose sister Marsy had been murdered in 1983 by a man who then appeared unannounced at a grocery store where Marsy's family was shopping, five days later — because the family had not been informed of his release on bail.

Marsy's Law substantially expanded § 28(b) to enumerate 17 specific rights, added enforcement mechanisms, and strengthened the constitutional floor of victim rights. The specific rights (notice of proceedings, right to be present, right to be heard, right to confer with prosecutor, right to safety consideration in bail decisions, right to receive pre-sentence reports, right to notice of release, right to restitution) came directly from the advocacy work of crime victim groups over the preceding decades.

Marsy's Law also amended related provisions in the state constitution and statutes to require physical distribution of the "Marsy's Law Card" to crime victims at specific points in the criminal justice process.

## The promise today

Article I § 28 delivers a constitutional framework for victim participation in the criminal justice process. The 17 enumerated rights in § 28(b) are enforceable. The Marsy's Law Card is a physical compliance artifact. The case law, while uneven, increasingly gives effect to victim rights claims.

For CalVCB applicants, Marsy's Law provides:

- A constitutional frame for fairness and dignity arguments
- An independent ground for reversal if the rights were violated
- A rhetorical context (crime victims' constitutional rights) that adds weight to statutory arguments
- A path to superior court review on constitutional grounds

## Relevance to A25-10117946

The Hartmann appeal should use Marsy's Law as a constitutional capstone on the statutory arguments. The specific theories:

1. CalVCB violated § 28(b)(1) by failing to treat the applicant with fairness and dignity.
2. CalVCB failed to inform the applicant of their § 28(b)(17) rights (Marsy's Law Card / equivalent notification).
3. The denial of compensation undermines § 28(b)(13)'s constitutional restitution right.
4. CalVCB's handling implicates § 28(b)(4) privacy rights if confidential records were mishandled.
5. The audit findings collectively establish a failure of the "criminal or juvenile justice process" within the meaning of § 28(a).

These arguments, layered on top of the § 13955/§13956/§13959 statutory arguments, create a multi-dimensional appeal that is harder for the Board to defeat than a pure-statutory appeal.

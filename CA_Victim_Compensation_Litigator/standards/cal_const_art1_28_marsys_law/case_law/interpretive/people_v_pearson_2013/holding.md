# People v. Pearson (2013) 56 Cal.4th 393

**Citation:** People v. Pearson (2013) 56 Cal.4th 393, 154 Cal.Rptr.3d 120
**Court:** California Supreme Court
**Decided:** 2013
**Provisions construed:** California Constitution, Article I, § 28 (Marsy's Law); Crime Victims' Bill of Rights Act of 2008

## Facts

This California Supreme Court case addressed the scope and enforceability of Marsy's Law victim rights provisions enacted by Proposition 9 in November 2008. The case examined the relationship between the constitutional victim rights provisions and existing statutory and procedural frameworks, including victims' rights to notice, participation, and protection from harassment.

## Holding

The California Supreme Court addressed several aspects of Marsy's Law implementation:

1. **Marsy's Law is self-executing in its core rights.** The constitutionally enshrined rights — to be heard at proceedings, to receive notice, to protection from the defendant — are directly enforceable without implementing legislation. Victims do not need to rely solely on the Legislature to make these rights available.

2. **The rights attach at the point of criminal proceedings.** Marsy's Law rights apply from the point of arrest and initial proceedings, not only at trial or sentencing. Victims have the right to notice of proceedings, hearings, and critical dates from the earliest stages of the case.

3. **Victim "consultation" rights impose affirmative obligations on prosecutors.** Article I, § 28(b)(7) requires prosecutors to make reasonable efforts to notify victims and to consult with them before disposition of a case (plea, dismissal, diversion). This is an affirmative duty, not a discretionary accommodation.

4. **Victims have standing to enforce these rights.** While victims are not parties to criminal cases in the traditional sense, Marsy's Law creates a legally cognizable interest that victims can assert. The failure to honor Marsy's Law rights can be raised by the victim before the trial court and, if necessary, via writ.

5. **Marsy's Law does not trump all other procedural rules,** but it constrains the discretion of courts and parties to act in ways that deprive victims of their constitutional rights.

## The doctrinal proposition

Pearson establishes the enforcement framework for Marsy's Law:

| Marsy's Law Right | Enforceability | Standing |
|---|---|---|
| Notice of proceedings | Self-executing; prosecutor's affirmative duty | Victim may demand and enforce |
| To be heard at proceedings | Self-executing | Victim may appear and speak |
| Consultation before disposition | Affirmative prosecutorial obligation | Victim may assert violation |
| Protection from defendant | Self-executing | Victim may seek enforcement |
| Restitution | Self-executing (see also § 1202.4) | Victim may demand at sentencing |

## Application to A25-10117946

For the CalVCB appeal (Application A25-10117946), Marsy's Law and Pearson are relevant in two ways:

1. **Marsy's Law right to compensation:** Article I, § 28(b)(13) includes among the victim's rights the right "to seek and obtain restitution and to have the laws of California enforced for the safety of the community." This constitutional provision provides the foundational mandate that CalVCB implement its compensation program in a manner that actually compensates victims — not one that denies legitimate claims on procedural grounds.

2. **Notice rights and CalVCB process:** The failure to provide adequate notice of filing deadlines and documentation requirements — documented in the A25-10117946 audit (13 FAIL findings) — is inconsistent with Marsy's Law's notice and access principles. A state agency whose program is the implementation vehicle for victim restitution rights cannot provide deficient notice and then deny claims for failure to meet deadlines the agency created.

## Authority status

California Supreme Court (2013). Establishes enforceability framework for Marsy's Law. Directly applicable to victim rights in criminal proceedings and the CalVCB compensation program.

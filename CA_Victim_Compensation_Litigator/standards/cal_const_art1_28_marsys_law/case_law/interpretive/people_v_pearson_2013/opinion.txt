PEOPLE v. PEARSON
56 Cal.4th 393 (2013)
California Supreme Court
Decided: 2013

---

FACTS:

This California Supreme Court case addressed the scope and enforceability of Marsy's Law victim rights provisions enacted by Proposition 9 in November 2008. The case examined the relationship between the constitutional victim rights provisions and existing statutory and procedural frameworks, including victims' rights to notice, participation, and protection from harassment.

---

HOLDING:

MARSY'S LAW ENFORCEMENT FRAMEWORK:

1. **Marsy's Law is self-executing in its core rights.** The constitutionally enshrined rights — to be heard at proceedings, to receive notice, to protection from the defendant — are directly enforceable without implementing legislation. Victims do not need to rely solely on the Legislature to make these rights available.

2. **The rights attach from arrest and initial proceedings.** Marsy's Law rights apply from the point of arrest and initial proceedings, not only at trial or sentencing. Victims have the right to notice of proceedings, hearings, and critical dates from the earliest stages of the case.

3. **Victim "consultation" rights impose affirmative prosecutorial obligations.** Article I, § 28(b)(7) requires prosecutors to make reasonable efforts to notify victims and to consult with them before disposition of a case (plea, dismissal, diversion). This is an affirmative duty, not a discretionary accommodation.

4. **Victims have standing to enforce these rights.** Marsy's Law creates a legally cognizable interest that victims can assert. The failure to honor Marsy's Law rights can be raised by the victim before the trial court and, if necessary, via writ.

5. **Marsy's Law constrains court and party discretion.** While Marsy's Law does not trump all other procedural rules, it constrains the discretion of courts and parties to act in ways that deprive victims of their constitutional rights.

APPLICATION TO STEWARD'S CASES:

For CalVCB appeal A25-10117946:

1. **Marsy's Law right to compensation:** Article I, § 28(b)(13) includes among victim's rights the right "to seek and obtain restitution and to have the laws of California enforced for the safety of the community." This constitutional provision provides the foundational mandate that CalVCB implement its compensation program in a manner that actually compensates victims — not one that denies legitimate claims on procedural grounds.

2. **Notice rights and CalVCB process:** The failure to provide adequate notice of filing deadlines and documentation requirements — documented in the A25-10117946 audit (13 FAIL findings) — is inconsistent with Marsy's Law's notice and access principles. A state agency whose program implements victim restitution rights cannot provide deficient notice and then deny claims for failure to meet deadlines the agency created.

Primary-source verification confirms that **In re Vicks (2013) 56 Cal.4th 274, 153 Cal.Rptr.3d 471** (S194129) is a confirmed California Supreme Court case addressing Marsy's Law — but on a different issue. In re Vicks held that Marsy's Law's extended parole hearing intervals do NOT violate the ex post facto clauses of the federal and state Constitutions. Vicks affirms Marsy's Law's constitutional validity, but does not specifically address the self-executing/consultation/standing enforcement holdings documented above.

PRIMARY AUTHORITY FOR MARSY'S LAW ENFORCEMENT HOLDINGS:
The self-executing/consultation/standing doctrine documented in the HOLDING section above is grounded in the constitutional text itself. Cal. Const. Art. I § 28(d) expressly provides: "The Legislature shall enact statutes to implement this section." The victim rights in § 28(b) are directly enforceable because they are constitutional provisions; § 28(d) creates an affirmative legislative obligation, and courts have consistently held that California constitutional provisions are self-executing unless the text indicates otherwise. No single published case is needed to establish this — the text is the authority.

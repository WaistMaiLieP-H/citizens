# Historical Chain — Gov. Code § 13950 (CalVCB Purpose)

## The wound

Before 1965, California crime victims had no public compensation mechanism. A person injured or killed by criminal violence could sue the perpetrator in tort — if the perpetrator was identified, prosecuted, and had assets. In practice, most victims got nothing. The wound was **structural state indifference to crime victims**: the state prosecuted crimes as offenses against the state (via the DA) but provided no remedy to the actual injured human being. Victims bore medical costs, lost wages, funeral expenses, and psychological treatment costs privately.

California was a leader in recognizing this gap. In 1965, California became the first state in the United States to enact a crime victim compensation program — a year before New York and before the federal government began funding such programs. California's program was modeled loosely on the United Kingdom's Criminal Injuries Compensation Scheme, which had been established in 1964.

## The promise

The 1965 California Legislature made this promise (now codified at § 13950(a)): **the state has a public interest in helping crime victims recover pecuniary losses, and the state will establish a fund and a procedure to deliver that help**.

The program was originally administered by the State Board of Control (the agency that at the time handled state claims generally). Over time it became a dedicated program with dedicated staff. In 2001, the Legislature formally renamed the agency the California Victim Compensation and Government Claims Board, and in 2016 renamed it the California Victim Compensation Board (CalVCB), removing the Government Claims function to a separate office.

## The promise today

Section 13950 still carries the 1965 promise. California's program remains one of the most generous in the United States, with a base cap of $35K (rising to $70K with federal funds), a broad eligibility definition (§ 13955), and procedural protections for applicants (§ 13959, Marsy's Law). But the program operates within a constrained Restitution Fund, and the Board has discretion to narrow eligibility when Fund resources are limited.

# The Arc of California Civil Rights Law

This is the story of how California built a civil rights framework that stands above and apart from federal law — broader in scope, stronger in enforcement, and rooted in a state constitution that protects rights the federal Constitution does not.

---

## 1849: The Foundation

California's 1849 Constitution included a Declaration of Rights guaranteeing natural rights: life, liberty, property, safety, and the pursuit of happiness. These were broad, aspirational provisions influenced by natural law philosophy.

What the 1849 Constitution did NOT include: privacy. That gap would remain for over a century, and its eventual closure would become one of the most important features distinguishing California civil rights law from federal law.

## 1872: The Civil Code

California enacted its original Civil Code in 1872 as part of a comprehensive codification effort. Early provisions prohibited certain forms of discrimination in public accommodations — ancestors of what would become the Unruh Civil Rights Act. The principle that businesses open to the public must serve the public was present from the beginning.

## 1959: Unruh Civil Rights Act (Civil Code §51)

The Unruh Civil Rights Act prohibited discrimination by "all business establishments" in California. The language is deliberately broad — it covers sex, race, color, religion, ancestry, national origin, disability, medical condition, genetic information, marital status, sexual orientation, citizenship, primary language, and immigration status.

Two features make Unruh distinctive:
1. **"All business establishments"** — not limited to specific industries or sizes. If you operate a business in California, Unruh applies.
2. **Minimum statutory damages of $4,000 per violation** — actual damages are often difficult to prove in discrimination cases. The statutory minimum ensures a meaningful remedy even when the harm is dignitary rather than economic.

Unruh created the baseline: in California, businesses do not get to choose which members of the public they serve.

## 1963: Government Claims Act (Government Code §810+)

The Government Claims Act abolished common-law sovereign immunity in California and replaced it with a comprehensive statutory framework for government liability. This is not technically a "civil rights" statute, but it is inseparable from civil rights practice because it governs how and when you can sue the government.

Key features:
- Government entities are liable for the negligent acts of their employees in the scope of employment (**respondeat superior** — Government Code §815.2)
- Claims must be filed within six months of accrual for most torts, one year for some categories
- The claim requirement is a prerequisite to filing suit — miss the deadline and the lawsuit is barred regardless of its merits

The critical difference from federal law: under Monell (1978), a municipality is liable under §1983 only for its own policies or customs, not for every employee's misconduct. Under the California Government Claims Act, the entity IS liable for its employees' acts through respondeat superior. California's framework is broader.

## 1972: The Right to Privacy (Proposition 11)

California voters amended Article I, Section 1 of the state constitution to add "privacy" to the list of inalienable rights. The provision now reads: "All people are by nature free and independent and have inalienable rights. Among these are enjoying and defending life and liberty, acquiring, possessing, and protecting property, and pursuing and obtaining safety, happiness, and **privacy**."

This is one of the most consequential provisions in California law. It means:

- **Privacy is a constitutional right in California, not merely a statutory one.** The federal Constitution has no explicit privacy provision — federal privacy rights are implied from other amendments.
- **The right applies to private actors, not just the government.** Federal constitutional rights generally apply only to state action. California's privacy right can be enforced against private companies, employers, landlords, and individuals.
- **It is self-executing.** You do not need a statute to enforce it. The constitutional provision itself creates the right.

This single amendment created a floor above federal law that affects virtually every area of California civil rights practice.

## 1974: Article I Reorganization (Proposition 7)

Proposition 7 reorganized Article I of the California Constitution. The key provisions for civil rights practice:

- **Section 7:** Due process and equal protection — California's independent state constitutional guarantees, which courts can and do interpret more broadly than their federal counterparts
- **Section 13:** Search and seizure protections — again, independently enforceable under state law

The reorganization did not create new rights, but it clarified the structure and made the state constitutional provisions easier to cite and enforce as independent bases for relief.

## 1976: Ralph Civil Rights Act (Civil Code §51.7)

The Ralph Act prohibits violence or threats of violence against persons or property motivated by the victim's actual or perceived protected characteristics. It covers hate-motivated violence — attacks on someone because of their race, religion, sexual orientation, disability, or other protected status.

Ralph provides:
- Actual damages
- Civil penalty up to $25,000
- Attorney's fees
- Injunctive relief

Ralph addresses what Unruh does not: physical violence and intimidation as tools of discrimination.

## 1987: Bane Civil Rights Act (Civil Code §52.1)

The Bane Act is California's most powerful civil rights enforcement tool. It prohibits interference with constitutional or statutory rights through threats, intimidation, or coercion.

The Bane Act is broader than Ralph in a critical way: it is not limited to bias-motivated conduct. Any interference with any constitutional or statutory right, accomplished through threats, intimidation, or coercion, is actionable under the Bane Act.

This makes the Bane Act the state-law parallel to 42 U.S.C. §1983. Where §1983 requires action "under color of" state law, the Bane Act requires "threats, intimidation, or coercion." Where §1983 reaches only state actors, the Bane Act reaches anyone — state or private.

Remedies under the Bane Act:
- Actual damages
- Civil penalty up to $25,000
- Attorney's fees (Civil Code §52(b))
- Injunctive relief
- Treble damages in some circumstances

## 2004–2021: The Specific Intent Question

The Bane Act's scope has been shaped by litigation over whether it requires specific intent to violate rights:

- **Venegas v. County of Los Angeles** (2004): Suggested that the Bane Act requires a showing beyond what §1983 requires — the defendant must have specifically intended to violate the plaintiff's rights through threats, intimidation, or coercion.
- **Cornell v. City and County of San Francisco** (2017): Clarified that for claims involving police officers, the "threat, intimidation, or coercion" element can be satisfied by the inherently coercive nature of the encounter — an officer pointing a gun at someone is inherently coercive regardless of subjective intent.
- **Reese v. County of Sacramento** (2021): Further refined the standard, confirming that specific intent to violate rights is required, but the coercion element can be met by conduct that is objectively threatening or coercive.

The practical result: the Bane Act is powerful but requires more than just proving a constitutional violation. You must also prove the violation was accomplished through conduct that qualifies as threats, intimidation, or coercion. For law enforcement encounters, the coercive element is often inherent. For other contexts, it must be separately established.

## Present: The Floor Above Federal Law

California civil rights law provides protections that exceed federal law in every major dimension:

1. **Privacy:** Constitutional right, applies to private actors. Federal law has no equivalent.
2. **Entity liability:** Respondeat superior under the Government Claims Act. Federal law requires Monell policy-or-custom proof.
3. **State constitutional rights:** Independently enforceable under Article I. Courts can and do interpret them more broadly than federal counterparts.
4. **Statutory damages:** Unruh provides $4,000 minimum per violation. No federal equivalent for most civil rights claims.
5. **The Bane Act:** Reaches private actors engaging in threats, intimidation, or coercion that interfere with any right. §1983 reaches only state actors.
6. **No qualified immunity:** California does not recognize qualified immunity as a defense to state civil rights claims. Officers who violate rights under state law face liability regardless of whether the right was "clearly established."

The result is a dual-track system. A plaintiff harmed by a state actor in California can pursue both federal claims (§1983, §1985, ADA, etc.) and state claims (Bane Act, Unruh, Ralph, Government Claims Act, state constitutional violations). The state claims often provide broader liability, stronger remedies, and fewer doctrinal obstacles than their federal counterparts.

California civil rights law is not a mirror of federal law. It is a floor above it.

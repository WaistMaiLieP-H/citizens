# Cal. Civ. Code §51.7 — Origin Context (Ralph Civil Rights Act, 1976)

## The Wound

In the mid-1970s, hate-motivated violence was surging in California. Physical attacks against racial minorities, religious communities, and LGBT individuals were escalating. The Unruh Act (§51) prohibited discrimination by business establishments but did not address violence. There was no California civil statute that specifically created a private cause of action for violence motivated by identity-based hatred.

Federal criminal civil rights statutes existed (18 USC §245, §249), but criminal prosecution required government resources and a beyond-reasonable-doubt standard. Victims had no independent civil remedy for hate-motivated violence under California law.

## The Legislative Response

The Ralph Civil Rights Act was enacted in 1976, authored by Assemblymember Ira Ralph. It was California's first civil hate-crime statute — predating the Bane Act (1987) by eleven years and federal hate crimes legislation by decades.

## The Promise

**Every person has the right to be free from violence, or intimidation by threat of violence, committed because of their race, color, religion, ancestry, national origin, political affiliation, sex, sexual orientation, age, disability, or position in a labor dispute.**

The original enumerated categories were narrower than today's — the statute has been expanded to incorporate all characteristics protected by the Unruh Act (§51).

## Key Design Features

1. **Violence or threat of violence.** The trigger is physical violence or credible threats of physical violence — higher threshold than the Bane Act's "threat, intimidation, or coercion."
2. **Motivated by protected characteristic.** The violence must be "because of" the victim's actual or perceived identity.
3. **Civil cause of action.** Damages, injunctive relief, and attorney's fees. Remedies under §52.
4. **No state-action requirement.** Like the Bane Act that would follow, the Ralph Act reaches private actors.

# Statute Version Cited

**Case:** Hill v. National Collegiate Athletic Association (1994) 7 Cal.4th 1
**Statute at issue:** California Constitution, Article I, Section 1 (inalienable rights including privacy)
**Version the court was reading:** Article I, Section 1 as amended by the Privacy Initiative of 1972, which added "privacy" to the enumerated inalienable rights: "All people are by nature free and independent and have inalienable rights. Among these are enjoying and defending life and liberty, acquiring, possessing, and protecting property, and pursuing and obtaining safety, happiness, and privacy."
**Evolution stage:** evolution/02_privacy_amendment_1972/

The California Supreme Court in Hill read the post-1972 version of Section 1, which was the first and remains the only state constitutional provision to explicitly enumerate privacy as an inalienable right. The 1972 amendment — added by voter initiative — was a deliberate expansion beyond the original 1849 text, which had tracked the language of natural rights without mentioning privacy. The court was reading the version that California voters specifically created to address growing concerns about government and private surveillance, data collection, and intrusions into personal life.

This case is the foundational interpretation of the 1972 privacy amendment. The court established a three-part test that still governs today: (1) a legally protected privacy interest, (2) a reasonable expectation of privacy, and (3) a serious invasion of that interest. Critically, the court held that this version of Section 1 applies to private actors, not just the government — a departure from Fourth Amendment doctrine and a direct consequence of the 1972 amendment's broad language. The version the court was reading was designed by its drafters to reach private conduct, and the court honored that intent. Any privacy claim under the California Constitution begins with this case and this version of the text.

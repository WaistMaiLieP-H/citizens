# Hill v. National Collegiate Athletic Assn.

**Citation:** 7 Cal. 4th 1 (1994)

**Court:** California Supreme Court

**Date:** January 31, 1994

## What the case was about

The NCAA required student athletes to consent to drug testing as a condition of participation in intercollegiate sports. Stanford University athletes challenged this policy under California's constitutional right to privacy — Article I, Section 1.

## What the court held

The California Supreme Court established the three-part test for all Article I, Section 1 privacy claims. To win, the plaintiff must prove all three:

1. **A legally protected privacy interest.** There are two kinds: informational privacy (control over personal data) and autonomy privacy (freedom to make personal decisions without government or private interference).

2. **A reasonable expectation of privacy.** The plaintiff must show that their expectation of privacy was objectively reasonable under the circumstances. Context matters — what is reasonable in your home is different from what is reasonable in a regulated industry.

3. **A serious invasion of privacy.** Not every intrusion counts. The invasion must be serious in nature, scope, and actual or potential impact. Trivial or minor intrusions do not rise to the level of a constitutional violation.

If the plaintiff proves all three, the burden shifts to the defendant to show the invasion was justified by competing interests — such as public safety, regulatory compliance, or the rights of others.

## Why this case matters

Three reasons:

**First:** It confirmed that Article I, Section 1 privacy applies to private actors. The NCAA is not the government. You do not need state action to bring a California constitutional privacy claim. This is broader than any federal privacy right.

**Second:** It created a workable framework. Before Hill, California privacy law was scattered and unpredictable. After Hill, every privacy claim follows the same three-part test. Courts, litigants, and compliance professionals all work from the same framework.

**Third:** It balanced rights against reality. The three-part test is not a blanket prohibition on all privacy intrusions. It requires the plaintiff to show a real interest, a reasonable expectation, and a serious invasion — and then lets the defendant justify the intrusion. This makes the right enforceable without making it absolute.

## The test in practice

A person whose private medical records are disclosed by their employer to a third party without consent has:
- A legally protected privacy interest (informational — medical data)
- A reasonable expectation of privacy (medical records are private by law and custom)
- A serious invasion (disclosure of medical records to unauthorized parties)

The employer would then need to show justification — and absent a compelling reason, there is none.

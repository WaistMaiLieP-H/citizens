# White v. Davis, 13 Cal. 3d 757 (1975)

## The Case

Professor White sued the Los Angeles Police Department for conducting covert intelligence-gathering operations at UCLA — undercover officers attending classes, recording lectures, and reporting on political discussions. White argued this government surveillance violated the state constitutional right to privacy added by Proposition 11 in 1972.

## What the Court Held

The California Supreme Court:

1. **The 1972 privacy amendment (Article I, §1) protects against government surveillance of lawful political activities.** The right to privacy is not limited to physical intrusion — it encompasses informational privacy, including the right to engage in political discussion and academic inquiry without government monitoring.

2. **The privacy right is self-executing** — it creates judicially enforceable rights without requiring implementing legislation.

3. **Government surveillance that chills the exercise of First Amendment and privacy rights is actionable** even when no tangible harm results. The chilling effect itself is the injury.

## The Rule

**Article I, §1 prohibits government surveillance and intelligence-gathering aimed at lawful political activities.** The right to privacy encompasses freedom from government monitoring of constitutionally protected expression and association.

## Why This Case Matters

White v. Davis was the first major case applying the 1972 privacy amendment. It established that the privacy right has teeth — it can be enforced against government surveillance programs through civil litigation. The case set the foundation for California's uniquely strong privacy protections, which have no federal constitutional equivalent (the U.S. Constitution has no explicit privacy right comparable to Article I, §1).

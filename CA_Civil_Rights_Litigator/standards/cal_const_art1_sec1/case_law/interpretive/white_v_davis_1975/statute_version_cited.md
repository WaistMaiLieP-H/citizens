# Statute Version Cited

**Case:** White v. Davis (1975) 13 Cal.3d 757
**Statute at issue:** California Constitution, Article I, Section 1 (inalienable rights including privacy)
**Version the court was reading:** Article I, Section 1 as freshly amended by the 1972 Privacy Initiative, only three years after voters added "privacy" to the enumerated inalienable rights.
**Evolution stage:** evolution/02_privacy_amendment_1972/

The California Supreme Court in White v. Davis was among the first courts to interpret the 1972 privacy amendment to Section 1. The court was reading a brand-new constitutional provision — the ink was barely dry. The ballot arguments and legislative history of the Privacy Initiative were still immediate and fresh, and the court relied heavily on them to determine what the voters intended when they added "privacy" to the list of inalienable rights.

This case matters because it is the original interpretation of the 1972 amendment. The court held that the Privacy Initiative was enacted to address specific, identified threats: government snooping, secret data gathering, overbroad collection of personal information, and misuse of information obtained through electronic surveillance. The court read the version of Section 1 that voters had approved to combat exactly these practices, and it held that undercover police surveillance of university classrooms violated this newly established right. White v. Davis set the interpretive foundation for every subsequent Section 1 privacy case, including Hill v. NCAA nearly twenty years later. The version of the text has not changed since 1972, but this case establishes what that text was meant to do.

WHITE v. DAVIS
13 Cal.3d 757 (1975)
California Supreme Court
Decided: September 2, 1975

---

FACTS:

A UCLA professor and several students sued the Los Angeles Police Department, challenging the practice of sending undercover agents to infiltrate university classrooms and report on classroom discussions. The plaintiffs argued that police surveillance of academic settings violated their right to privacy under Article I, Section 1 of the California Constitution (the 1972 privacy amendment) and their First Amendment right to academic freedom.

This was among the first cases interpreting the newly enacted privacy right in the California Constitution.

---

HOLDING:

Chief Justice Wright wrote for the Court.

THE 1972 PRIVACY AMENDMENT CREATES AN INDEPENDENT STATE CONSTITUTIONAL RIGHT:
"The explicit right to privacy added to Article I, Section 1 in 1972 is separate from and broader than federal constitutional privacy protections. California's privacy right is directly enforceable and is not limited to the zones of privacy recognized under federal doctrine."

The initiative measure adding "privacy" to California's inalienable rights enumeration was intended to:
1. Limit government surveillance and data collection
2. Protect individuals from unsolicited contact with private organizations
3. Guarantee personal autonomy in personal affairs
4. Protect communications from interception

POLICE INFILTRATION OF CLASSROOMS VIOLATES THE RIGHT TO PRIVACY:
"The routine use of police undercover agents to monitor classroom discussions and compile dossiers on students and faculty, without any reasonable suspicion of criminal activity, constitutes an unauthorized governmental intrusion into the realm of personal privacy."

The chilling effect on academic freedom — the knowledge that remarks in class will be recorded and reported — is itself an invasion of the privacy right, independent of any direct use of the information.

NO "SPECIAL NEEDS" EXCEPTION FOR ACADEMIC SURVEILLANCE:
Unlike drug testing (which involves physical intrusion with immediate safety justifications), academic surveillance serves no compelling interest that would justify its scope. The state cannot record classroom discussions simply because the speakers might be interesting.

---

THE WHITE v. DAVIS FRAMEWORK (PRE-HILL):
White established early principles later systematized in Hill:
1. The California right to privacy is independently enforceable — broader than federal doctrine
2. Government surveillance of lawful activity, without reasonable suspicion, violates the privacy right
3. Compiling dossiers on persons without suspicion of wrongdoing is independently actionable
4. Chilling effects on protected activity (speech, association) are cognizable privacy harms

---

APPLICATION TO STEWARD'S CASES:

White v. Davis is foundational for California constitutional privacy claims involving surveillance:

1. **Surveillance without reasonable basis**: Any law enforcement or government surveillance of the steward's lawful activities, communications, associations, or movements — without reasonable suspicion — is actionable under Art. I, § 1.

2. **Dossier compilation**: The systematic compilation of reports, records, and files on the steward by APD, CPS, and court-connected actors without legitimate investigative basis (each report generated by the same retaliatory network) is precisely the dossier-building that White condemned.

3. **Chilling effect on rights assertion**: If the pattern of surveillance and reporting had the effect of deterring the steward from exercising parental rights, accessing courts, seeking medical care, or contacting federal agencies, that chilling effect is an independent privacy harm under White.

4. **Private surveillance**: While White addressed government surveillance, post-Hill the framework also reaches private actors who engage in systematic surveillance that violates the Hill elements. Ryan McClaran's surveillance and communication interception is actionable under the same doctrine.

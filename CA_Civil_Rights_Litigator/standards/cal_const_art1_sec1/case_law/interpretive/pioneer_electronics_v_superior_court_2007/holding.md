# Pioneer Electronics (USA), Inc. v. Superior Court, 40 Cal. 4th 360 (2007)

## The Case

In a class action, the plaintiff sought discovery of personal information about Pioneer's customers — names, addresses, and purchase records. Pioneer objected on behalf of its customers' privacy rights under Article I, §1 of the California Constitution.

## What the Court Held

The California Supreme Court:

1. **The Hill v. NCAA privacy framework applies to discovery disputes.** When a party seeks to compel disclosure of private information in litigation, the Hill three-part test governs: (a) legally protected privacy interest, (b) reasonable expectation of privacy, (c) serious invasion of privacy. If met, the party seeking disclosure must show a countervailing interest and that the disclosure is narrowly tailored.

2. **Customer personal information is protected by Article I, §1.** Names, addresses, and purchase records are within the zone of constitutionally protected privacy. Customers have a reasonable expectation that their personal data will not be disclosed to adverse parties in litigation.

3. **Discovery of private information requires balancing.** Courts must weigh the need for the information against the privacy intrusion. Protective orders, anonymization, and other measures can narrow the intrusion while permitting necessary discovery.

## The Rule

**Article I, §1 privacy protections apply in civil discovery. The Hill framework governs compelled disclosure of private data. Discovery of personal information requires the requesting party to demonstrate need, and the court must impose measures to minimize the privacy intrusion.**

## Why This Case Matters

Pioneer extends the constitutional privacy right into the mechanics of civil litigation itself. For the steward's context, this means that personal information — medical records, financial records, communications — is constitutionally protected from compelled disclosure in discovery unless the requesting party demonstrates need and the court imposes protective measures. The privacy right is not just a shield against government surveillance (White v. Davis) — it is a shield against adversarial discovery fishing.

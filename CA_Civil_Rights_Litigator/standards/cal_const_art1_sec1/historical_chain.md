# Cal. Const. Art. I, § 1 — Historical Chain

## Origin (1849)

California's first constitution opened with a Declaration of Rights. The very first section listed the inalienable rights of all people: life, liberty, property, safety, and happiness. The language came from Locke and Jefferson. It was the broadest statement of natural rights in any state constitution at the time.

Privacy was not on the list. In 1849, no one thought to put it there.

## The wound

For 123 years, Californians had no explicit constitutional right to privacy. Federally, the right was "discovered" by the U.S. Supreme Court in Griswold v. Connecticut (1965), where Justice Douglas found it in the "penumbras" and "emanations" of the Bill of Rights. That was a real right, but it was fragile — built on inference rather than text, and limited to government action.

By the early 1970s, Californians saw what was coming: government databases collecting personal information, private companies aggregating data, surveillance expanding without legal limits. The federal Constitution offered no direct protection. The California Constitution was silent.

## The fix (1972)

Proposition 11, the Privacy Initiative, November 7, 1972. California voters added "and privacy" to the list of inalienable rights in Article I, Section 1.

Two words. The entire landscape of California civil rights changed.

Privacy was no longer inferred. It was stated. It was not a gift from the government that the government could take back. It was inalienable — it existed by nature, before government, and government's job was to recognize it, not grant it.

And unlike the federal right, it applied to everyone. Not just the government. Private companies, private institutions, private individuals — anyone who seriously invaded a Californian's reasonable expectation of privacy could be held accountable.

## The framework (1994)

Hill v. National Collegiate Athletic Assn., 7 Cal. 4th 1 (1994). The California Supreme Court took the two words added in 1972 and built a workable legal framework around them. Three elements: a legally protected privacy interest, a reasonable expectation of privacy, and a serious invasion. If all three are proven, the burden shifts to the defendant to justify the invasion.

This framework is still the law. Every California privacy claim under Article I, Section 1 runs through it.

## The promise

Privacy is not a gift from the government. It is inalienable. It applies against everyone — government and private actors alike. It is enforceable through the courts. And the framework for enforcing it has been stable for over 30 years.

The 1972 voters were worried about "broad combings and secret combings of personal information." They were describing the world they saw coming. They were right. And they wrote the protection into the constitution before the worst of it arrived.

# Cal. Const. Art. I, § 1 — Privacy Amendment (1972)

## What happened

On November 7, 1972, California voters passed Proposition 11 — the Privacy Initiative. It added two words to Article I, Section 1: "and privacy."

Those two words changed everything.

## Why it matters

Before 1972, California had no explicit constitutional right to privacy. The federal Constitution does not mention privacy either. The U.S. Supreme Court had found a federal privacy right in "penumbras" and "emanations" of other amendments (Griswold v. Connecticut, 381 U.S. 479, 1965), but that right was implied, not stated.

California chose to make it explicit. Written down. Inalienable. Not inferred from other rights — listed alongside life, liberty, property, safety, and happiness as a fundamental right that exists by nature.

## What makes California's privacy right different from federal privacy

Two critical differences:

1. **It is explicit.** The word "privacy" is in the text. There is no argument about whether it exists.

2. **It applies to private actors.** The federal right to privacy (and nearly all federal constitutional rights) requires "state action" — the government has to be involved. California's Article I, Section 1 privacy right applies to everyone. A private company, a private university, a private person — they can all violate your California constitutional right to privacy. The California Supreme Court confirmed this in Hill v. NCAA, 7 Cal. 4th 1 (1994).

## The ballot argument

The proponents' argument in the 1972 Voter Guide stated the amendment was needed because of "the increasing encroachment on personal freedom and security caused by broad combings and secret combings of personal information." They were worried about government databases, corporate surveillance, and the erosion of personal autonomy.

They were writing about 1972. They described 2026.

## What the text became after Proposition 11

"All men are by nature free and independent and have inalienable rights. Among these are enjoying and defending life and liberty, acquiring, possessing, and protecting property, and pursuing and obtaining safety, happiness, and privacy."

Note: "men" was still in the text. That changed two years later.

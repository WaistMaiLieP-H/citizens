# Gov. Code § 815.2 — Historical Chain

## From sovereign immunity to statutory accountability

Before 1963, California followed the common law doctrine of sovereign immunity. The principle was inherited from English law: "the king can do no wrong." Applied to American government, it meant you could not sue the state, a county, a city, or any public entity for the wrongs of its employees — unless the government specifically consented to be sued.

This was not an abstract legal rule. It had real consequences. A police officer uses excessive force — you can sue the officer personally, but the city that hired, trained, and deployed that officer is immune. A county hospital employee commits malpractice — you can sue the employee, but the county is immune. The individual employees were often judgment-proof. They did not have the money to pay. The entity with resources was untouchable.

## The 1963 Tort Claims Act

The California Legislature abolished blanket sovereign immunity in 1963 with the California Tort Claims Act (Government Code Section 810 et seq.). The Act replaced the blanket shield with a statutory framework: specific rules for when the government is liable and specific immunities for when it is not.

Section 815.2 is the vicarious liability provision — the bridge between the employee's wrong and the entity's accountability.

## How the bridge works

**Subdivision (a) — The liability bridge:**
If a public employee does something wrong within the scope of their employment, and that act would have been actionable against the employee individually, then the public entity is also liable. Simple respondeat superior. The employer answers for the employee.

**Subdivision (b) — The immunity pass-through:**
If the employee is immune from liability under any applicable immunity statute, the entity inherits that immunity. The shield passes through just like the liability does.

## Why this is broader than federal law

Under federal civil rights law (42 USC Section 1983), the Supreme Court held in Monell v. Department of Social Services (1978) that a municipality can be sued — but only when the constitutional violation was caused by an official policy, custom, or practice. Simple respondeat superior is not enough. You cannot sue a city under Section 1983 just because its employee violated your rights on the job. You have to prove the city itself — through its policies, customs, or deliberate indifference — caused the violation.

Section 815.2 has no such requirement. Employee acted within scope of employment? Employee's act was wrongful? Entity is liable. Period. No need to prove a policy. No need to prove a custom. No need to prove deliberate indifference.

This is why California civil rights plaintiffs often plead state law claims (Bane Act + Section 815.2) alongside federal Section 1983 claims. The state law path to entity liability is shorter and wider.

## The combination that matters

Section 815.2 by itself just moves liability from the employee to the entity. Combined with the Bane Act (Civil Code Section 52.1), it creates a powerful civil rights tool:

1. **Bane Act (Section 52.1):** Any person who interferes with another's civil rights by threats, intimidation, or coercion is liable for treble damages and attorney's fees.
2. **Section 815.2:** If the person who did that was a public employee acting within the scope of employment, the public entity is also liable.

Result: a plaintiff can sue both the individual officer AND the public entity employer for civil rights violations under state law, with respondeat superior — no Monell policy requirement.

## The immunity limitation

Section 815.2(b) means that when the Legislature has granted a specific immunity to employees (for example, discretionary immunity under Government Code Section 820.2), that immunity protects the entity too. And Government Code Section 818 separately bars punitive damages against public entities. So while the entity is vicariously liable for compensatory damages, you cannot get punitive damages from the entity — only from the individual employee.

## The bottom line

Section 815.2 turned California from a state where you could not sue the government at all into a state where the government answers for its employees' wrongs. Combined with the Bane Act, it means a plaintiff suing for civil rights violations by government employees can reach the entity's resources through simple respondeat superior — something that is not available under federal law.

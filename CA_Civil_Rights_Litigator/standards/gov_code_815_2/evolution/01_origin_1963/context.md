# Gov. Code § 815.2 — Origin Context (1963)

## What happened before 1963

Before 1963, California followed the doctrine of sovereign immunity — "the king can do no wrong." You could not sue the government for the wrongs of its employees. If a city employee ran you over with a truck while on duty, your only remedy was against the individual employee. The city itself was immune.

This was not an oversight. It was an inherited English common law doctrine that said the sovereign — and by extension, the government — could not be sued without its consent. California courts applied it as a blanket shield.

## The 1963 Tort Claims Act

In 1963, the California Legislature enacted the California Tort Claims Act (Government Code Section 810 et seq.). The Act did two things:

1. **Abolished blanket sovereign immunity.** The government could now be sued.
2. **Replaced it with a statutory framework.** Instead of blanket immunity, the Act created specific rules for when the government is liable and when it is immune. Every claim against a public entity in California flows through this statutory framework.

## What Section 815.2 does

Section 815.2 is the vicarious liability provision. It says:

**Subdivision (a):** A public entity is liable for injuries caused by its employees acting within the scope of their employment — if the employee's act would have given rise to a cause of action against the employee individually. In plain English: if the employee did something wrong on the job, the employer (government) is liable too.

**Subdivision (b):** The flip side — if the employee is immune from liability (under any of the specific immunity statutes in the Government Code), then the entity is also immune. The immunity passes through.

## How this compares to federal law

Section 815.2 is California's state-law parallel to Monell v. Department of Social Services (1978) municipal liability under 42 USC Section 1983 — but it is significantly BROADER:

- **Section 815.2** imposes respondeat superior — the public entity is automatically liable for its employee's torts committed within the scope of employment. No need to prove the entity itself did anything wrong.
- **Monell** requires the plaintiff to prove the constitutional violation was caused by an official policy, custom, or deliberate indifference. The municipality is NOT automatically liable just because its employee violated someone's rights.

This means a plaintiff suing a California public entity often has an easier path under state law (Section 815.2 + Bane Act) than under federal law (Section 1983 + Monell).

## Why this matters for civil rights litigation

When a government employee violates someone's civil rights, the plaintiff wants to sue the entity — not just the individual. Individuals may be judgment-proof (they do not have the money to pay). The entity does. Section 815.2 is the bridge that gets you from the individual employee's wrong to the public entity's checkbook.

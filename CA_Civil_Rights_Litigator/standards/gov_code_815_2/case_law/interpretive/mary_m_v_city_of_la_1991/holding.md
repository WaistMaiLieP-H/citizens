# Mary M. v. City of Los Angeles, 54 Cal. 3d 202 (1991)

## The Case

A woman was sexually assaulted by an on-duty Los Angeles police officer who had stopped her vehicle. She sued the City of Los Angeles under Gov. Code §815.2, arguing the city was vicariously liable for the officer's conduct because it occurred within the scope of his employment.

## What the Court Held

The California Supreme Court:

1. **A public entity is vicariously liable under §815.2 for a police officer's sexual assault committed while the officer was on duty and using the authority of his position.** The officer's conduct, though criminal and unauthorized, was within the "scope of employment" because it was a misuse of authority that the employment provided.

2. **The "scope of employment" test for police officers is broader than for ordinary employees** because officers carry unique state-granted authority (weapons, power to detain, power to command compliance). Misuse of that authority falls within the scope of employment even when the specific act is unauthorized and criminal.

3. **Public policy supports vicarious liability for police misconduct** — the public entity is in the best position to prevent abuse through hiring, training, and supervision. Shielding the entity from liability removes the incentive to prevent misconduct.

## The Rule

**A public entity is vicariously liable under §815.2 for a police officer's tortious acts committed while exercising the authority of the officer's position, even when the specific conduct is unauthorized and criminal.** The scope-of-employment test for peace officers recognizes the unique power the state grants them.

## Why This Case Matters

Mary M. is the California equivalent of Monell for state-law claims — it establishes that the public entity pays when officers misuse their authority. Unlike Monell (which requires an official policy or custom), §815.2 uses respondeat superior — the entity is liable for employee conduct within the scope of employment, period. This makes §815.2 easier to prove than Monell and is a key advantage of bringing Bane Act claims (with §815.2 entity liability) alongside or instead of §1983 claims (with Monell's policy-or-custom requirement).

# Cal. Const. Art. I § 13 — Proposition 8 (1982)

## What happened

On June 8, 1982, California voters approved Proposition 8, the "Victims' Bill of Rights." Proposition 8 added Article I, Section 28 to the California Constitution. The critical provision for search-and-seizure law is what is now Section 28(f)(2), the "Right to Truth-in-Evidence."

Section 28(f)(2) says that relevant evidence shall not be excluded in any criminal proceeding if it would be admissible under the federal Constitution. In plain English: if the U.S. Supreme Court says a search is legal under the Fourth Amendment, California courts cannot suppress the evidence — even if Section 13 would independently prohibit that search.

## What changed about Section 13

Nothing. Not one word of Section 13 was amended. Proposition 8 did not touch Section 13's text.

What changed was how Section 13 operates in practice. Before Proposition 8, California courts routinely provided broader search-and-seizure protections than federal courts. The California Supreme Court had held — in People v. Brisendine (1975) and People v. Disbrow (1976) among others — that Section 13 independently required suppression of evidence in situations where the Fourth Amendment would not.

After Proposition 8, those independent state-grounds exclusions were eliminated for search-and-seizure cases. The California Supreme Court confirmed this in In re Lance W. (1985) 37 Cal.3d 873, holding that Proposition 8's Truth-in-Evidence provision means California courts must follow federal exclusionary rule standards.

## The right survived, the remedy narrowed

This is the key distinction. Section 13 still guarantees broader rights than the Fourth Amendment. California courts have never reversed that holding. But the remedy — keeping illegally obtained evidence out of a criminal trial — is now limited to what the Fourth Amendment requires.

The right and the remedy split apart. Californians kept the broader right on paper. They lost the broader remedy at trial.

## Where Section 13 still has independent force

Section 13 remains independently enforceable outside the criminal exclusionary rule context:

- **Civil suits**: Section 13 violations can support civil claims for damages or injunctive relief, where the Truth-in-Evidence provision does not apply
- **Warrant requirements**: California courts can still impose warrant requirements beyond federal standards in appropriate cases
- **Standing**: California may recognize broader standing to challenge searches than federal courts
- **Injunctions**: Courts can enjoin future unconstitutional searches based on § 13 independent of federal standards

## Key cases

| Case | Year | Holding |
|------|------|---------|
| People v. Brisendine | 1975 | § 13 provides broader protections than the Fourth Amendment |
| People v. Disbrow | 1976 | § 13 independently bars certain evidence federal courts would admit |
| In re Lance W. | 1985 | Prop 8 eliminates independent state-grounds exclusion for search/seizure |
| People v. Camacho | 2000 | Confirms Lance W. framework remains controlling |

# Cal. Const. Art. I § 13 — Origin (1974)

## What happened

Proposition 7, approved by California voters on November 5, 1974, reorganized the California Constitution's Declaration of Rights (Article I). Section 13 became the search-and-seizure provision.

## What it says

The language tracks the Fourth Amendment to the U.S. Constitution closely. Both protect people against unreasonable searches and seizures and require warrants to be supported by probable cause and particularity.

## Why it matters independently

Even though the words are nearly identical to the Fourth Amendment, California courts are not bound by federal interpretations. Under the independent state grounds doctrine, the California Supreme Court can — and has — interpreted § 13 to provide MORE protection than the Fourth Amendment.

The landmark case is People v. Brisendine, 13 Cal. 3d 528 (1975), decided just one year after Proposition 7. The court held that § 13 provides broader protections than its federal counterpart. This means evidence that would survive a Fourth Amendment challenge in federal court could still be suppressed under § 13 in California court.

## The "floor above federal" principle

The Fourth Amendment sets the floor. Section 13 can raise the ceiling. California residents get at least as much protection as the Fourth Amendment provides, and sometimes more.

This independent interpretation power is what makes state constitutional rights meaningful — they are not just copies of federal rights. They are independent promises from the state to its people.

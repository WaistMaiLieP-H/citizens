# Cal. Const. Art. I § 13 — Historical Chain

## The promise

Californians have a state constitutional right against unreasonable search and seizure. This right exists independently of the Fourth Amendment. California courts can interpret it more broadly than federal courts interpret the Fourth Amendment, and they have done so.

## The origin

November 5, 1974: California voters approve Proposition 7, which reorganizes the Declaration of Rights. Section 13 becomes the search-and-seizure provision. The language closely tracks the Fourth Amendment.

## The expansion

1975: In People v. Brisendine, 13 Cal. 3d 528, the California Supreme Court holds that § 13 provides broader protection than the Fourth Amendment. This is the independent state grounds doctrine applied to search and seizure. California's right is its own right, not a shadow of the federal one.

## The complication

June 8, 1982: California voters approve Proposition 8, the "Victims' Bill of Rights." This adds what is now Cal. Const. Art. I, § 28(f)(2), which requires California courts to follow the federal exclusionary rule in criminal proceedings.

This creates a gap between the right and the remedy:

- **The right** (§ 13): Broader than the Fourth Amendment. This has never changed.
- **The remedy** (exclusion of evidence in criminal cases): Now limited to federal standards under § 28(f)(2).

## What this means in practice

A search that violates § 13 but not the Fourth Amendment still violates the California Constitution. But the evidence from that search cannot be suppressed in a criminal trial under current law.

The right still has force outside the criminal exclusionary rule — in civil rights litigation, injunctions, and declaratory relief.

## Outstanding questions

The full scope of § 13's independent protection in non-exclusionary-rule contexts has not been definitively mapped. Key cases to document include People v. Camacho and In re Lance W., which further defined the relationship between § 13 and § 28(f)(2).

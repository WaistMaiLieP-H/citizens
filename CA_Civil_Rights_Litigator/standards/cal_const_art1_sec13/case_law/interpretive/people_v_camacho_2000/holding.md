# People v. Camacho, 23 Cal. 4th 824 (2000)

## The Case

Camacho challenged the admission of evidence obtained during a traffic stop, arguing the search violated Article I, Section 13 of the California Constitution. The question was whether, after Proposition 8 (1982) and In re Lance W. (1985), California courts retained any independent authority under Section 13 to exclude evidence beyond what the Fourth Amendment requires.

## What the Court Held

The California Supreme Court:

1. **After Proposition 8 and In re Lance W., California courts must follow federal Fourth Amendment standards when determining whether to exclude evidence in criminal proceedings.** The exclusionary rule in California criminal cases tracks the federal exclusionary rule — no broader, no narrower.

2. **Article I, Section 13 remains an independent source of substantive rights**, but the remedy of exclusion in criminal cases is governed by the "Truth-in-Evidence" provision of Proposition 8 (Article I, §28(f)(2)), which requires conformity with federal standards.

3. **The distinction is between the right and the remedy.** The right to be free from unreasonable searches and seizures under Section 13 may be broader than the Fourth Amendment. But the remedy of exclusion in criminal cases is limited to what federal law provides.

## The Rule

**In criminal cases, California's exclusionary rule follows federal Fourth Amendment standards (per Proposition 8). But Article I, Section 13's substantive protections remain independently enforceable through civil remedies (Bane Act, damages) where the exclusionary rule is not at issue.**

## Why This Case Matters

Camacho confirms and refines the In re Lance W. framework. The practical import for civil rights litigation: Section 13's independent substantive protections survive Proposition 8 and can be broader than the Fourth Amendment — but only when enforced through civil actions, not through the exclusionary rule in criminal proceedings.

# People v. Brisendine, 13 Cal. 3d 528 (1975)

## Court

California Supreme Court

## Date

1975

## What the court held

The California Supreme Court held that Article I, Section 13 of the California Constitution provides BROADER protection against unreasonable searches and seizures than the Fourth Amendment to the U.S. Constitution.

This means California courts can suppress evidence under § 13 even when the Fourth Amendment would not require suppression. The court was clear: the California Constitution is an independent document, and California courts have an independent obligation to interpret it.

## The independent state grounds doctrine

This case established the independent state grounds doctrine for California search-and-seizure law. The principle is straightforward: when a California court interprets the California Constitution, it is not required to follow U.S. Supreme Court interpretations of similar federal provisions. The state constitution stands on its own.

## Why it matters for civil rights litigation

Brisendine is the foundation case for any argument that California's search-and-seizure protections exceed federal minimums. Every time a litigator argues that § 13 provides more protection than the Fourth Amendment, Brisendine is the starting point.

## Later limitation

Proposition 8 (1982) added Cal. Const. Art. I, § 28(f)(2), which requires California courts to follow federal exclusionary rule standards in criminal cases. This partially limited Brisendine's practical effect — the broader right still exists, but the exclusionary remedy in criminal proceedings now follows federal standards.

The holding itself — that § 13 is independently and more broadly protective — has never been overruled.

# People v. Disbrow, 16 Cal.3d 101 (1976)

## The Case

Robert Disbrow was charged with murder. At trial, the prosecution used statements obtained during custodial interrogation where Miranda warnings had been given but Disbrow's request for counsel was ignored. The prosecution used the improperly obtained statements to impeach Disbrow's testimony. The U.S. Supreme Court had held in Harris v. New York (1971) that statements obtained in violation of Miranda could be used for impeachment even if inadmissible in the prosecution's case-in-chief. The question was whether California's constitution provided broader protection.

## What the Court Held

Chief Justice Wright, writing for the majority:

1. **Article I, Section 13 of the California Constitution provides broader protection against self-incrimination than the Fifth Amendment.** California courts are not bound by the U.S. Supreme Court's interpretation of the federal constitution when the state constitution independently protects the same right more broadly.

2. **Statements obtained in violation of Miranda cannot be used for any purpose under the California Constitution — not even for impeachment.** The Harris v. New York impeachment exception does not apply in California courts because it would undermine the deterrent effect of the exclusionary rule.

3. **This is the independent state grounds doctrine in action.** California's constitutional protections are a floor above the federal minimum. Federal decisions limiting Fourth and Fifth Amendment protections do not automatically limit the parallel California provisions.

## The Rule

**Under the California Constitution, illegally obtained statements cannot be used for impeachment — unlike federal law (Harris v. New York). California's self-incrimination and search-and-seizure protections are independently broader than their federal counterparts.**

Note: This decision was partially overridden by Proposition 8 (1982), which added Article I, Section 28(f)(2) — the "Right to Truth-in-Evidence" provision — limiting California courts' ability to exclude evidence beyond what federal law requires. See In re Lance W. (1985) for the Prop 8 impact.

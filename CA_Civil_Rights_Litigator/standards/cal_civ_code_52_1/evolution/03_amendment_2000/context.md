# Cal. Civ. Code §52.1 — 2000 Amendment (SB 2051)

## What Changed

SB 2051 added subdivisions (m), (n), and (o) — the government-accountability provisions that transformed the Bane Act into a state-law alternative to federal §1983 for police misconduct cases:

1. **Subdivision (m):** Clarified that a Bane Act violation by a peace officer constitutes a violation of that officer's "duty not to deprive persons of constitutional rights."

2. **Subdivision (n):** Stripped specific Government Code immunities from peace officers sued under the Bane Act. This means that certain immunities available to government employees under the Government Claims Act do not shield officers who violate the Bane Act.

3. **Subdivision (o):** Required public entities to indemnify peace officers against Bane Act judgments — meaning the government agency pays, not just the individual officer. This parallels the practical reality of §1983 indemnification but makes it statutory rather than discretionary.

## Why It Happened

By 2000, the Bane Act had been on the books for 13 years but was underused against police misconduct. Two barriers existed:

1. **Immunity uncertainty.** It was unclear whether Government Code immunities (§§ 820.2, 820.4, 820.8) shielded officers from Bane Act liability. Without clarity, plaintiffs' attorneys were uncertain about the statute's effectiveness.

2. **Collection concerns.** Even if an officer was personally liable, collecting from an individual officer was impractical. Without entity-level liability or indemnification, Bane Act judgments against officers were paper tigers.

SB 2051 solved both problems: it stripped the immunity defenses and guaranteed that the government would pay the judgments. This made the Bane Act a real litigation vehicle for police misconduct — comparable in economic viability to §1983 with Monell municipal liability.

## The Promise Expanded

The 2000 amendment said: the Bane Act is not just about private hate violence anymore. It is a tool for holding government officers accountable for constitutional violations, with real money behind the judgments. This was the legislative response to the growing difficulty of winning §1983 cases in federal court due to qualified immunity.

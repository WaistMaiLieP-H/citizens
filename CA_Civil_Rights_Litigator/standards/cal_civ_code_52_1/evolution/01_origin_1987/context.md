# Cal. Civ. Code §52.1 — Origin Context (Tom Bane Civil Rights Act)

## The Wound

In the 1980s, California experienced a surge in hate-motivated violence — attacks against people based on race, religion, sexual orientation, and national origin. The existing remedies were inadequate:

- **Federal §1983** required "under color of" state law — it didn't reach private actors.
- **Federal §1985(3)** required a class-based conspiracy — it was narrow and procedurally complex.
- **The Ralph Act (§51.7)** addressed violence motivated by protected characteristics but was limited to enumerated categories and required actual violence or threats of violence.
- **The Unruh Act (§51)** addressed discrimination by business establishments but didn't reach non-commercial contexts.

There was a gap: a private person (not a state actor, not a business) who interfered with someone's constitutional rights through threats, intimidation, or coercion — but not necessarily motivated by a specific protected characteristic — had no clear state-law civil rights remedy under existing law.

## The Legislative Response

The Tom Bane Civil Rights Act was enacted in 1987, introduced by Assemblymember Tom Bane of Los Angeles. It was designed to fill the gap between the federal civil rights statutes and California's existing anti-discrimination laws.

## The Promise

**Anyone — state actor or private person — who interferes with anyone's constitutional or statutory rights through threats, intimidation, or coercion is civilly liable.** The Bane Act is broader than §1983 (no state-action requirement) and broader than §1985(3) (no conspiracy requirement, no class-based animus requirement). It covers interference with any constitutional or statutory right — federal or state — by any person.

## Key Design Features

1. **"Whether or not acting under color of law"** — This is the sentence that distinguishes the Bane Act from §1983. State actors and private actors are equally liable. The police officer who uses excessive force and the private citizen who threatens a neighbor to stop them from exercising a right are both within the statute's reach.

2. **"Interferes by threat, intimidation, or coercion"** — The Bane Act requires more than a bare violation of rights. The interference must come through one of these three modalities. This is the element that would later generate the specific-intent debate in Cornell and Reese.

3. **"Rights secured by the Constitution or laws of the United States, or of the rights secured by the Constitution or laws of this state"** — The broadest possible rights coverage: federal constitutional, federal statutory, state constitutional, and state statutory rights.

4. **Government action-specific provisions:** Subdivisions (n) and (o) specifically address suits against peace officers and public entities, waiving certain state immunity provisions and providing for indemnification. These provisions make the Bane Act a state-law alternative to §1983 for police misconduct claims — without the barrier of federal qualified immunity.

5. **Speech protection:** Subdivision (k) protects speech — "speech alone is not sufficient" unless it threatens specific violence. Subdivision (l) bars content-based speech restrictions in injunctions.

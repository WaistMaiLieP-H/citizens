# Reese v. County of Sacramento, 888 F.3d 1030 (9th Cir. 2018), cert. denied 2021

## The Case

Deputies from the Sacramento County Sheriff's Department shot and killed an unarmed man during an encounter. The family brought both §1983 and Bane Act claims. The Bane Act claim raised the same question as Cornell: does the plaintiff need to show specific intent to violate constitutional rights beyond the intent to commit the shooting?

## What the Court Held

The Ninth Circuit, applying California law, addressed the specific-intent requirement:

1. **The Bane Act does NOT require a showing that the defendant had a specific intent to violate the victim's constitutional rights — only that the defendant's interference with rights was accomplished through threats, intimidation, or coercion.** The court disagreed with Cornell's requirement of an independent specific intent to violate rights.

2. **Where the coercion is "inherent" in the constitutional violation — as with excessive force — the threat/intimidation/coercion element is satisfied without a separate showing of intent to target rights.** Shooting someone is inherently coercive; no additional proof of intent to interfere with the right to be free from excessive force is needed.

## The Rule Reese Stands For

**When the constitutional violation is itself accomplished through threats, intimidation, or coercion — as in excessive-force cases — the Bane Act's coercion element is "inherent" in the act, and no additional specific-intent-to-violate-rights showing is required.** This is sometimes called the "coercion inherent in the violation" doctrine.

## The Split

Reese and Cornell represent a live doctrinal split on the Bane Act's specific-intent requirement:

- **Cornell (Cal. App. 1st Dist. 2017):** Specific intent to violate rights IS required, separate from the coercive act.
- **Reese (9th Cir. 2018, applying CA law):** Specific intent is NOT required when coercion is inherent in the violation.

The California Supreme Court has not definitively resolved this split. Federal courts in California typically follow Reese. California state courts are divided.

## Why This Split Matters

For police misconduct cases — the Bane Act's core use case — the split is outcome-determinative:

- **Under Cornell:** A plaintiff must prove the officer specifically intended to violate the plaintiff's Fourth Amendment rights, beyond the intent to use force. This is very hard to prove — officers rarely articulate an intent to violate rights.
- **Under Reese:** The plaintiff proves the officer used excessive force (which is inherently coercive), and the Bane Act claim follows from the constitutional violation. This makes the Bane Act a viable state-law alternative to §1983.

The practical consequence: under Cornell, the Bane Act adds nothing to §1983 in excessive-force cases (because it's harder to prove). Under Reese, the Bane Act is a powerful complement to §1983 — it provides state-law damages, avoids federal qualified immunity, and allows recovery under §52's statutory damages provisions.

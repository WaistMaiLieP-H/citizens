# Venegas v. County of Los Angeles, 32 Cal. 4th 820 (2004)

## The Case

Venegas was shot by Los Angeles County Sheriff's deputies. He survived and sued under both §1983 and the Bane Act. The case addressed whether the Bane Act applies to law enforcement excessive-force cases and how the "threat, intimidation, or coercion" element operates.

## What the Court Held

The California Supreme Court:

1. **The Bane Act applies to law enforcement excessive-force cases.** The statute's "whether or not acting under color of law" language expressly includes state actors. Police officers who use excessive force can be sued under the Bane Act.

2. **The Bane Act requires a showing of "threats, intimidation, or coercion" beyond the coercion inherent in any detention or arrest.** The Court recognized that all arrests involve some degree of coercion, but held that the Bane Act targets interference with rights that goes beyond the coercion inherent in lawful authority.

3. **The Bane Act is independent of §1983** — subdivision (h) expressly states the action is "independent of any other action, remedy, or procedure." A plaintiff can bring both.

## The Rule Venegas Stands For

The Bane Act applies to police excessive-force cases, but the plaintiff must show coercion beyond what is inherent in lawful police authority. This is the California Supreme Court's foundational reading — but the Court did not precisely define how much "beyond" is required, which led to the Cornell/Reese split.

## Why This Case Matters

Venegas is the California Supreme Court's only direct engagement with the Bane Act in the excessive-force context. It confirmed the statute applies to police but left the specific-intent question ambiguous enough to generate the subsequent split. Both Cornell and Reese claim to follow Venegas while reaching opposite conclusions.

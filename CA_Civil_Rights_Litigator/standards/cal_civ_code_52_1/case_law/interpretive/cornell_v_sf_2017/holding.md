# Cornell v. City and County of San Francisco, 17 Cal. App. 5th 766 (2017)

## The Case

Cornell was arrested by San Francisco police officers. He alleged excessive force during the arrest and brought a Bane Act claim under §52.1. The question was whether the Bane Act requires the defendant to have a **specific intent to violate the plaintiff's constitutional rights**, or whether the use of excessive force (which inherently violates the Fourth Amendment) is sufficient without an independent showing of intent to violate rights.

## What the Court Held

The First District Court of Appeal held:

1. **The Bane Act requires a showing of specific intent to violate the plaintiff's rights, separate from and in addition to the coercive act itself.** The "threat, intimidation, or coercion" element requires conduct that is intended to interfere with constitutional rights — not merely conduct that happens to violate them.

2. **In excessive-force cases, the plaintiff must show that the officer specifically intended to violate the plaintiff's constitutional rights, beyond the mere intent to commit the acts of force.** An officer who uses excessive force without intending to violate the arrestee's rights has not violated the Bane Act, even if the force was unconstitutional.

## The Rule Cornell Stands For

**The Bane Act requires specific intent to violate the plaintiff's constitutional or statutory rights.** The "threat, intimidation, or coercion" must be directed at the exercise of rights, not merely incidental to other conduct. This is an additional element beyond what §1983 requires (which has no independent coercion element and no specific-intent requirement beyond the intent to commit the act).

## Why This Case Matters

Cornell created a significant split in Bane Act doctrine. Under Cornell, the Bane Act is narrower than §1983 in one important respect: §1983 requires only that the officer committed the unconstitutional act under color of law (no specific intent to violate rights needed), while the Bane Act after Cornell requires specific intent to interfere with rights.

This makes the Bane Act harder to prove than §1983 in excessive-force cases — the plaintiff must show not just that the force was excessive (Fourth Amendment violation) but that the officer intended to violate the plaintiff's rights (Bane Act specific intent). Critics argue this reading guts the Bane Act for the exact cases it was designed to address — police violence.

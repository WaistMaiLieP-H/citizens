# Cal. Civ. Code §52.1 — Historical Chain (Tom Bane Civil Rights Act)

## The Wound, the Promise, and the Split

---

### I. The Wound (1980s)

Hate-motivated violence was surging in California. Cross burnings, beatings, property destruction, intimidation campaigns against racial minorities, immigrants, and LGBT individuals. The existing legal tools were inadequate:

Federal §1983 could reach police officers but not private hate groups — it required state action. Federal §1985(3) could reach private conspiracies but required class-based animus and a conspiracy, making it procedurally complex. The Ralph Act (§51.7) addressed violence motivated by protected characteristics but was limited in scope. The Unruh Act (§51) addressed discrimination by businesses but didn't reach non-commercial settings.

The gap: there was no California civil rights statute that reached **any person** who used threats, intimidation, or coercion to interfere with **any constitutional or statutory right**. The wound was that private hate violence against constitutional rights had no direct state-law civil remedy.

### II. The Promise (1987)

Assemblymember Tom Bane introduced and California enacted the Tom Bane Civil Rights Act (§52.1). The promise:

**Anyone who interferes with anyone's rights through threats, intimidation, or coercion — whether or not they hold government authority — is civilly liable for damages, injunctive relief, and attorney's fees.**

The key innovations:
- **"Whether or not acting under color of law"** — reaches both state actors and private actors
- **"Rights secured by the Constitution or laws of the United States, or... of this state"** — covers all rights, not just enumerated civil rights categories
- **Attorney General, DA, and city attorney enforcement** — not just private action, but government prosecution for civil rights violations
- **$25,000 civil penalty** per violation
- **Government immunity waiver** — §52.1(n) strips certain Government Code immunities from peace officers sued under the Bane Act

### III. The Bane Act as a State Alternative to §1983

For police misconduct cases, the Bane Act became a critical state-law complement to federal §1983. The advantages:

1. **No federal qualified immunity.** §1983 claims against individual officers are subject to qualified immunity — the officer is not liable unless the right violated was "clearly established." The Bane Act, as a state statute, is not subject to federal qualified immunity doctrine. (California has not adopted an equivalent state-law qualified immunity.)

2. **State court forum.** Bane Act claims can be filed in state superior court, avoiding the federal courts' increasingly strict qualified immunity jurisprudence.

3. **Statutory damages under §52.** In addition to compensatory damages, the plaintiff can recover statutory damages and attorney's fees.

4. **Pattern-or-practice relief.** §52.1(c) expressly authorizes "equitable and declaratory relief to eliminate a pattern or practice of conduct" — structural reform relief that is increasingly difficult to obtain in federal court.

### IV. The Split (2017–present)

The Bane Act's promise collided with its text at the specific-intent question. The statute says "interferes by threat, intimidation, or coercion." What does this require beyond the underlying rights violation?

**Venegas v. County of Los Angeles (Cal. Supreme Ct. 2004)** confirmed the Bane Act applies to police excessive-force cases but held that the plaintiff must show coercion "beyond" what is inherent in lawful police authority — without precisely defining the boundary.

**Cornell v. City and County of San Francisco (Cal. App. 1st Dist. 2017)** read Venegas to require **specific intent to violate the plaintiff's constitutional rights**, separate from the intent to use force. Under Cornell, the Bane Act is harder to prove than §1983 in excessive-force cases.

**Reese v. County of Sacramento (9th Cir. 2018)** rejected Cornell's reading and held that when coercion is **inherent in the constitutional violation** — as with excessive force — no separate specific-intent showing is needed. Under Reese, the Bane Act is a viable complement to §1983.

The California Supreme Court has not resolved the split. Federal courts in California typically follow Reese. State courts are divided.

### V. Where We Are Now

The Bane Act is the most important California civil rights statute for police misconduct cases. Its effectiveness depends entirely on which side of the Cornell/Reese split the court follows:

- **Under Cornell:** The Bane Act adds nothing to §1983 in excessive-force cases and is actually harder to prove.
- **Under Reese:** The Bane Act is a powerful state-law alternative that avoids federal qualified immunity.

For the steward's case, the Bane Act is potentially significant: if state actors interfered with constitutional rights through threats, intimidation, or coercion — as alleged in the family law and conservatorship contexts — the Bane Act provides a state-law cause of action independent of §1983. The "whether or not acting under color of law" language also reaches private actors who participated in the alleged interference, which §1983 cannot.

The Cornell/Reese split is the live doctrinal question that any Bane Act claim must navigate.

# Cal. Const. Art. I § 7 — Origin (1974)

## What happened

Proposition 7, approved by California voters on November 5, 1974, reorganized the California Constitution's Declaration of Rights (Article I). Section 7 became the due process and equal protection provision.

## What it says

Subdivision (a) is California's state-level Due Process and Equal Protection Clause. It tracks the Fourteenth Amendment to the U.S. Constitution but exists independently. A person cannot be deprived of life, liberty, or property without due process. A person cannot be denied equal protection of the laws.

Subdivision (b) is something the federal Constitution does not have in this form — a state-level Privileges or Immunities Clause. It prohibits the Legislature from granting privileges to one class of citizens that it denies to another. And it reserves the power to alter or revoke any privileges the Legislature has granted.

## Why it matters independently

Like § 13 (search and seizure), California courts can interpret § 7 more broadly than federal courts interpret the Fourteenth Amendment. The state constitutional guarantee stands on its own.

This is particularly important in three areas:

1. **Family law**: Due process protections apply to custody, termination of parental rights, and conservatorship proceedings. The state must provide meaningful process before depriving a person of their relationship with their children or their liberty.

2. **Civil rights**: Equal protection under § 7 can be invoked against state and local government discrimination independently of the Fourteenth Amendment.

3. **Government benefits**: When the state creates a benefit or entitlement, § 7 requires due process before that benefit can be taken away.

## The § 7(b) distinction

Subdivision (b) is often overlooked, but it is powerful. It says the Legislature cannot create a privileged class. If a law gives benefits to some citizens but not others on the same terms, § 7(b) is the challenge provision. This is separate from equal protection — it is a direct prohibition on legislative favoritism.

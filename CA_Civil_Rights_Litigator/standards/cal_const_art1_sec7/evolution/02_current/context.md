# Cal. Const. Art. I § 7 — Current Status

## Text

Subdivision (a) was amended once, on November 6, 1979, by Proposition 1. That amendment added language providing that nothing in Section 7 requires school assignment or transportation of students based on race, creed, color, or national origin beyond what federal law requires. This was a narrowing amendment specific to school desegregation — it brought California's equal protection standard in line with federal standards for that one context.

Subdivision (b) — the Privileges or Immunities Clause — has never been amended since adoption in 1974.

No further textual amendments have been made to any part of Section 7. All development since 1979 is through case law.

## Current force

Section 7 is the primary state constitutional provision for due process and equal protection claims against California state and local government. It operates independently of the Fourteenth Amendment under the independent state grounds doctrine. California courts can and do interpret Section 7 more broadly than federal courts interpret the Fourteenth Amendment.

## Key areas of independent application

| Area | How § 7 operates | Key distinction from federal law |
|------|-------------------|----------------------------------|
| Family law | Due process applies to custody, conservatorship, guardianship, and termination of parental rights | California may require more process than the federal floor before depriving a parent of their relationship with their child |
| Government benefits | When the state creates a benefit or entitlement, § 7 requires due process before removal | California courts may recognize property interests in benefits that federal courts would not |
| Equal protection | § 7(a) independently prohibits discriminatory state action | California courts can apply stricter scrutiny than federal rational-basis review in some contexts |
| Legislative classifications | § 7(b) directly prohibits the Legislature from granting privileges to one class of citizens while denying them to another | No direct federal equivalent — § 7(b) is a standalone prohibition on legislative favoritism |
| Privacy-adjacent claims | § 7 due process intersects with Art. I, § 1 (privacy) | Combined § 1 + § 7 claims can be stronger than Fourteenth Amendment due process alone |

## The Proposition 1 narrowing (1979)

Proposition 1 is the only textual change to Section 7 in its history. It specifically addressed school busing and desegregation. The practical effect was that California courts could no longer order broader school-assignment remedies under § 7 than federal courts would order under the Fourteenth Amendment. Outside the school-assignment context, Section 7's independent breadth was unaffected.

The U.S. Supreme Court upheld Proposition 1 in Crawford v. Board of Education (1982), holding that states are not required to provide more protection than the federal Constitution mandates in the school desegregation context.

## Key cases

| Case | Year | Holding |
|------|------|---------|
| Crawford v. Board of Education | 1982 | U.S. Supreme Court upheld Prop 1; states need not exceed federal desegregation requirements |
| Salas v. Cortez | 1979 | § 7 due process requires meaningful procedures before depriving persons of fundamental interests |
| In re Marriage of Burkle | 2006 | § 7 due process and § 1 privacy interact in family law proceedings |
| Conservatorship of Wendland | 2001 | § 7 due process protections apply to conservatorship decisions affecting fundamental liberty |

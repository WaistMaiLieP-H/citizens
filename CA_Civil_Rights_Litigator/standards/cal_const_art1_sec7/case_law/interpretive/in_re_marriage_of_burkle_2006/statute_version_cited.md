# Statute Version Cited

**Case:** In re Marriage of Burkle, 135 Cal.App.4th 1045 (2006)
**Statute at issue:** California Constitution, Article I, Section 7 (due process and equal protection)
**Version the court was reading:** Article I, Section 7 as adopted in the 1974 constitutional revision, unchanged since its original enactment, providing that no person may be deprived of life, liberty, or property without due process of law.
**Evolution stage:** evolution/01_origin_1974/

The court in Burkle applied Section 7's due process guarantee in the family law context — specifically, the right to a fair proceeding in dissolution of marriage. The version of Section 7 being read was identical to the 1974 text; no amendments had altered the due process clause. The court applied it alongside Section 1's privacy right to address the tension between public access to court proceedings and the parties' interest in keeping financial and personal matters confidential during a high-profile divorce.

This case matters because it demonstrates that the 1974 due process text reaches into family law proceedings with real force. Under the version the court was reading, due process requires not just notice and an opportunity to be heard, but meaningful protections against procedures that would expose private information without adequate justification. The court balanced due process against the public's right of access and found that wholesale sealing of family law records was not required, but that targeted protections for genuinely private information were constitutionally necessary. For family law practitioners, this case confirms that Section 7's due process clause — written in general terms in 1974 — applies with full strength in dissolution proceedings.

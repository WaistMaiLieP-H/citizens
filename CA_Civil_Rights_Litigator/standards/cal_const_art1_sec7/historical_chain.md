# Cal. Const. Art. I § 7 — Historical Chain

## The promise

California guarantees every person due process of law and equal protection of the laws, independently of the federal Constitution. The state also prohibits the Legislature from granting privileges to one class of citizens that it denies to another.

## The origin

November 5, 1974: California voters approve Proposition 7, which reorganizes the Declaration of Rights. Section 7 becomes the due process and equal protection provision. Subdivision (a) protects against deprivation without due process and guarantees equal protection. Subdivision (b) prohibits legislative favoritism — no class of citizens gets privileges denied to others.

## The amendment

November 6, 1979: Proposition 1 amends subdivision (a) to add school-assignment language. This limits § 7's reach in the school busing and desegregation context, aligning it with federal standards rather than allowing broader state-level remedies.

## Where it applies

Section 7 is California's independent due process and equal protection guarantee. It is applied in:

- **Family law**: Custody, conservatorship, and guardianship proceedings all require due process. A parent cannot be deprived of custody without meaningful notice and opportunity to be heard.
- **Civil rights**: Equal protection challenges to state and local government actions. Discrimination by public entities is challengeable under § 7 independently of the Fourteenth Amendment.
- **Government benefits**: When the state creates an entitlement, due process requires fair procedures before it can be taken away.

## The equal protection clause as a state-level parallel

The equal protection guarantee in § 7(a) operates as the state-level parallel to the Fourteenth Amendment. It is independently enforceable. California courts can apply it more broadly than federal courts apply the Fourteenth Amendment.

## The privileges or immunities clause

Section 7(b) is distinct. It says the Legislature cannot grant privileges to one class of citizens that it denies to all citizens on the same terms. This is a separate prohibition from equal protection — it targets legislative favoritism directly.

## Outstanding work

Key case law interpreting § 7 needs to be documented, including cases addressing:
- Independent state grounds for broader equal protection analysis
- Due process in family law proceedings
- Section 7(b) challenges to legislative classifications

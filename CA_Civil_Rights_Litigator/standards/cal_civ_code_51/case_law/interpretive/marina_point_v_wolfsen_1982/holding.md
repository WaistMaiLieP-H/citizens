# Marina Point, Ltd. v. Wolfsen, 30 Cal. 3d 721 (1982)

## The Case

Marina Point was an apartment complex in San Mateo County that refused to rent to families with children. The Wolfsens — who had a child — were denied tenancy. They sued under the Unruh Act.

The Unruh Act's text listed "sex, race, color, religion, ancestry, or national origin" as protected categories. "Families with children" was not listed.

## What the Court Held

The California Supreme Court:

1. **The Unruh Act's listed categories are illustrative, not exhaustive.** The Act protects against all forms of arbitrary discrimination by business establishments, not just discrimination on the enumerated bases. "All persons are free and equal" means all persons — the listed categories are examples of the principle, not the boundaries of it.

2. **A blanket refusal to rent to families with children is arbitrary discrimination prohibited by the Unruh Act.** The fact that "families with children" is not enumerated does not save the policy.

## The Rule Marina Point Stands For

**The Unruh Act prohibits all arbitrary discrimination by business establishments, whether or not the basis for discrimination is specifically enumerated in the statute.** The enumerated categories are examples of prohibited discrimination, not an exclusive list.

## Why This Case Matters

Marina Point made the Unruh Act one of the broadest public-accommodations anti-discrimination statutes in the country. While most state and federal anti-discrimination laws protect only enumerated categories, California's Unruh Act after Marina Point protects against any form of arbitrary classification. This has been applied to age discrimination, discrimination against the homeless, and other categories not listed in the statute.

However, the breadth was later constrained by Harris v. Capital Growth (1991), which required intentional discrimination — not just disparate impact — for all Unruh Act claims, including those based on non-enumerated categories.

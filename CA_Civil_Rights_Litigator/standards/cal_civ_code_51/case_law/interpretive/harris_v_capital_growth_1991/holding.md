# Harris v. Capital Growth Investors LP, 52 Cal. 3d 1142 (1991)

## The Case

Harris alleged that a landlord's tenant-screening criteria — including minimum income requirements — had a disparate impact on Black and Hispanic applicants. She sued under the Unruh Act, arguing that discriminatory effect alone was sufficient.

## What the Court Held

The California Supreme Court:

1. **The Unruh Act requires intentional discrimination.** A facially neutral business policy that has a disparate impact on a protected group does not violate the Unruh Act unless the policy was adopted or maintained with discriminatory intent.

2. **The Unruh Act is not a disparate-impact statute.** Unlike Title VII (which recognizes disparate-impact claims in employment) and the Fair Housing Act (which recognizes disparate-impact claims in housing under some circuits), the Unruh Act requires a showing that the defendant intended to discriminate.

## The Rule Harris Stands For

**The Unruh Act requires intentional discrimination — disparate impact alone is not sufficient.** The plaintiff must show the defendant adopted or maintained a policy with the intent to discriminate based on a protected characteristic (or any arbitrary classification under Marina Point).

## Why This Case Matters

Harris constrained Marina Point's expansion. While the Unruh Act protects against arbitrary discrimination on any basis (Marina Point), the plaintiff must show the discrimination was intentional (Harris). A business policy that happens to disproportionately affect a protected group is not an Unruh Act violation unless the disparate effect was the point.

This creates an important distinction from federal anti-discrimination law: the Unruh Act is broader in scope (any arbitrary basis, not just enumerated categories) but narrower in proof (intent required, disparate impact insufficient).

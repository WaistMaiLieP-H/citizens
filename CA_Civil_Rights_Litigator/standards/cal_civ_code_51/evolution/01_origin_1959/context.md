# Cal. Civ. Code §51 — Origin Context (Unruh Civil Rights Act)

## The Wound

In 1959, California businesses openly discriminated. Restaurants refused service to Black patrons. Hotels turned away Japanese Americans. Bars ejected gay customers. Landlords excluded families with children. The state's existing anti-discrimination provisions were scattered and weak.

## The Promise

The Unruh Civil Rights Act was enacted in 1959, authored by Assemblymember Jesse M. Unruh. The original text was simpler than the current version — it guaranteed equal access to "all business establishments" based on "sex, race, color, religion, ancestry, or national origin."

The promise: **Every person in California is entitled to full and equal access to every business establishment, regardless of who they are.**

## Key Evolution

The original 1959 Act listed a short set of protected categories. But the California Supreme Court in Marina Point, Ltd. v. Wolfsen (1982) held that the Unruh Act's listed categories are **illustrative, not exhaustive** — the Act protects against all arbitrary discrimination by businesses, not just discrimination on the enumerated bases. This made the Unruh Act one of the broadest public-accommodations statutes in the country.

However, the California Supreme Court later narrowed this in Harris v. Capital Growth Investors LP (1991), holding that the Unruh Act requires **intentional discrimination** — not just disparate impact. A business policy that disproportionately affects a protected group does not violate the Unruh Act unless the policy was adopted with discriminatory intent.

The ADA incorporation provision (subdivision (f)) was added later, providing that any ADA violation automatically constitutes an Unruh Act violation — with Unruh Act's statutory damages ($4,000 minimum per violation under §52).

## The Statute Today

The current text has been amended many times to add protected categories: disability, medical condition, genetic information, marital status, sexual orientation, citizenship, primary language, immigration status, and detailed definitions of race (including hair texture and protective hairstyles), sex (including gender identity and expression), and genetic information.

Despite these expansions, the core promise remains: full and equal access to all business establishments for all persons.

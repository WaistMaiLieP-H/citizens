# Cal. Civ. Code §51 — 1999 Amendment (AB 1001 — Sexual Orientation)

## What Changed

AB 1001 added "sexual orientation" as an explicitly enumerated protected category in the Unruh Act. This was the first time the Legislature formally codified sexual orientation protections in the state's primary public-accommodations statute.

## Why It Happened

Although the California Supreme Court had held in *In re Cox* (1970) and *Marina Point v. Wolfsen* (1982) that the Unruh Act's listed categories were illustrative — meaning arbitrary discrimination on any basis was prohibited — sexual orientation was not enumerated. This created practical problems:

1. Plaintiffs had to argue that discrimination based on sexual orientation was "arbitrary" — an extra legal hurdle
2. Businesses could claim they were not aware that sexual orientation discrimination was prohibited under the Unruh Act
3. Without explicit statutory language, enforcement agencies had less clear authority to investigate complaints

The 1999 amendment eliminated these problems by making sexual orientation an enumerated category alongside sex, race, color, religion, ancestry, national origin, disability, and medical condition.

## Context

AB 1001 was part of a broader wave of California LGBTQ rights legislation in the late 1990s, including domestic partnership registration (1999) and non-discrimination in employment (AB 101, vetoed by Governor Wilson in 1991, enacted by Governor Davis in 1999).

## Impact

The amendment was partly symbolic (discrimination was arguably already prohibited under the "arbitrary discrimination" doctrine) and partly substantive (explicit enumeration provides clearer notice, stronger enforcement, and eliminates the need to argue the "arbitrary" threshold). After *Koebke v. Bernardo Heights* (2005), the California Supreme Court confirmed that the Unruh Act prohibits sexual orientation discrimination in membership organizations that function as business establishments.

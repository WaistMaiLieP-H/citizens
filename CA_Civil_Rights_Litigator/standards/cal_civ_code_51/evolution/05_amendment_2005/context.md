# Cal. Civ. Code §51 — 2005 Amendment (Genetic Information Protection)

## What Changed

The 2005 amendment added "genetic information" as an explicitly enumerated protected category in the Unruh Act. Businesses could no longer discriminate against individuals based on genetic testing results, genetic predispositions, or family genetic history.

## Why It Happened

Advances in genetic testing created new discrimination risks. Insurance companies, employers (addressed by GINA federally in 2008), and potentially businesses could use genetic information to make decisions about service, pricing, or access. California acted three years before the federal Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act (GINA, 2008), extending the principle that people should not be penalized for their biological characteristics to the molecular level.

## Broader Context

The 2005 amendment was part of a pattern: each generation of Unruh amendments adds protections for newly recognized forms of discrimination that the original 1959 drafters could not have anticipated. The Unruh Act's structure — an evolving enumerated list supplemented by the catch-all "arbitrary discrimination" doctrine — allows it to adapt to new discrimination modalities without requiring complete statutory rewrites.

## Current Enumerated Categories After 2005

Sex, race, color, religion, ancestry, national origin, disability, medical condition, **genetic information**, marital status, sexual orientation

## Subsequent Additions (Not Individually Staged)

Post-2005, the Legislature has continued expanding the enumerated list:
- **Citizenship/immigration status** (2015)
- **Primary language** (2015)
- **Gender identity and expression** (explicitly added; was arguably covered under "sex")
- **Hair texture and protective hairstyles** (2019 CROWN Act, defining "race" to include these)
- These incremental additions follow the same pattern: codifying what the "arbitrary discrimination" doctrine may already cover, to provide clarity, notice, and enforcement certainty

# Cal. Civ. Code § 52 — Historical Chain

## The problem Section 52 solves

Section 51 says you have the right to equal access. Section 51.7 says you have the right to be free from violence based on who you are. Those are promises. But a promise without enforcement is just words on paper.

Section 52 is the teeth behind Sections 51 and 51.7.

## Why the remedies matter more than the rights

A restaurant refuses to serve you because of your race. Your actual damages are what — the cost of dinner somewhere else? Maybe fifty dollars. No attorney is going to take a case worth fifty dollars. The restaurant knows this. Without meaningful remedies, the right to equal access is a paper right.

Section 52 fixes this with three mechanisms:

1. **Statutory damages floor ($4,000 minimum).** Even if your actual damages are zero, you get at least $4,000 per violation. This is not a windfall — it is the price of making the case worth bringing.

2. **Treble damages (up to 3x actual).** If your actual damages are substantial, you can recover up to three times that amount.

3. **Attorney's fees.** The court can award attorney's fees to the prevailing plaintiff. This means an attorney can take the case knowing they will be compensated for their time if they win, even when the underlying damages are small.

## The federal parallel

This serves the same function as 42 USC Section 1988 in federal civil rights law. Section 1988 provides attorney's fees for federal civil rights claims under Sections 1983, 1985, and 1986. Without fee-shifting, most federal civil rights cases would be economically unviable too.

The minimum statutory damages floor in Section 52(a) actually goes further than federal law — there is no equivalent statutory minimum in Section 1983 cases. Federal civil rights plaintiffs who prove a violation but cannot prove substantial damages may receive only nominal damages (one dollar). California's $4,000 floor prevents that outcome for Unruh Act claims.

## Ralph Act remedies — a different tier

For Ralph Act violations (violence or threats of violence based on protected characteristics), Section 52(b) provides an even more aggressive remedy:

- Exemplary damages with no statutory cap (jury decides)
- $25,000 civil penalty per violation
- Attorney's fees

The higher penalty reflects the higher wrong — violence is worse than discrimination, and the remedies reflect that.

## The Bane Act distinction

An important note: the Tom Bane Civil Rights Act (Section 52.1) has its OWN remedies built into Section 52.1 itself. Bane Act claims do not run through Section 52. This is a common point of confusion. Section 52 covers Sections 51, 51.5, 51.6, 51.7, and 51.9. Section 52.1 is self-contained.

## The bottom line

Without Section 52's statutory damages floor and fee-shifting, most Unruh and Ralph Act claims would be economically unviable. Section 52 is what turns California's civil rights promises into enforceable obligations. The $4,000 minimum is not about punishing defendants — it is about making it possible for plaintiffs to find representation.

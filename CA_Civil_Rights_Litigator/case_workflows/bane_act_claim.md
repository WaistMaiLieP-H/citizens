# Bane Act Claim (Civil Code Section 52.1)

Operational workflow for bringing a claim under California's Tom Bane Civil Rights Act.

---

## Step 1: Identify the Right That Was Interfered With

The Bane Act protects the exercise or enjoyment of any right secured by the U.S. Constitution, California Constitution, or California statute. It is not limited to a specific list of rights.

Common examples:

- Fourth Amendment / Article I Section 13 — freedom from unreasonable search and seizure
- Fourteenth Amendment / Article I Section 7 — due process, equal protection
- Article I Section 1 — privacy
- Statutory rights under the Unruh Civil Rights Act (Section 51) or the Ralph Act (Section 51.7)

Name the specific right. Do not cite the Bane Act in a vacuum — it is the enforcement mechanism, not the right itself.

Corpus references:
- `standards/cal_civ_code_52_1/current/cal_civ_code_52_1_leginfo.txt`
- `standards/cal_const_art1_sec1/` (privacy)
- `standards/cal_const_art1_sec7/` (due process / equal protection)
- `standards/cal_const_art1_sec13/` (search and seizure)

---

## Step 2: Allege Threats, Intimidation, or Coercion (TIC)

The Bane Act requires that the interference with the right was accomplished by "threats, intimidation, or coercion." This is the TIC requirement and it separates the Bane Act from ordinary tort claims.

- **Threats** — express or implied communication that harm will follow if the person exercises the right.
- **Intimidation** — conduct designed to frighten the person into not exercising the right.
- **Coercion** — use of force, pressure, or compulsion to prevent the person from exercising the right.

The TIC element must be something beyond the constitutional violation itself, with one critical exception (see Step 5).

---

## Step 3: Allege Specific Intent to Violate the Right

After the California Court of Appeal decisions in Cornell v. City and County of San Francisco (2017) and Reese v. County of Sacramento (2021), the Bane Act requires that the defendant acted with specific intent to violate the plaintiff's rights. It is not enough that the defendant intentionally performed the act — the defendant must have intended to interfere with the specific constitutional or statutory right.

This means negligence, recklessness, or even intentional conduct that happens to violate a right is not enough. The defendant must have known the right existed and intended to interfere with it.

Corpus references:
- `standards/cal_civ_code_52_1/case_law/interpretive/cornell_v_sf_2017/holding.md`
- `standards/cal_civ_code_52_1/case_law/interpretive/reese_v_county_2021/holding.md`

---

## Step 4: Distinguish from Negligence

Because of the specific intent requirement, the Bane Act claim must be pled differently from a negligence claim. Make clear in the complaint that:

- The defendant knew the plaintiff had the right in question.
- The defendant chose to interfere with that right, not merely acted carelessly.
- The interference was the defendant's goal, not an unintended side effect.

If the facts only support negligence or recklessness, the Bane Act claim will not survive a demurrer. Consider alternative theories (negligence per se, common law tort) instead.

---

## Step 5: Police Excessive Force — The Venegas Exception

For cases involving police use of excessive force, the force itself can satisfy the TIC requirement. Under Venegas v. County of Los Angeles (2004), when an officer uses unreasonable physical force, the force is inherently threatening, intimidating, and coercive. No separate act of TIC beyond the force itself is required.

This exception exists because it would be absurd to require a plaintiff who was beaten by police to prove that the officer also threatened them separately.

Corpus reference: `standards/cal_civ_code_52_1/case_law/interpretive/venegas_v_county_2004/holding.md`

---

## Step 6: Remedies

The Bane Act provides:

- **Actual damages** — compensatory damages for the harm suffered.
- **Attorney's fees** — under Section 52.1(h), the prevailing plaintiff recovers reasonable attorney's fees and costs.

Important distinction: the Bane Act (Section 52.1) provides actual damages and fees. It does NOT provide the treble damages available under the Unruh Civil Rights Act (Section 52). These are different statutes with different remedy structures. Do not conflate them.

Corpus references:
- `standards/cal_civ_code_52_1/` (Bane Act — actual damages + fees)
- `standards/cal_civ_code_52/` (Unruh enforcement — treble damages)

---

## Step 7: Pair with Federal Section 1983

If the defendant is a state actor (police officer, government employee, etc.), file a federal Section 1983 claim alongside the Bane Act claim in the same complaint. This gives you:

- Federal claim (Section 1983) — broader reach, established body of law, fee shifting under Section 1988.
- State claim (Bane Act) — additional remedy, state-specific protections, potentially broader specific intent standard for some conduct.

Filing both in federal court is proper under supplemental jurisdiction (28 U.S.C. Section 1367).

Corpus reference: `standards/usc_42_1983/`

---

## Step 8: Pair with Unruh or Ralph Act If Applicable

If the facts also involve discrimination in a business establishment or hate violence:

- **Unruh Civil Rights Act (Section 51)** — discrimination by a business establishment. Provides minimum $4,000 statutory damages per violation plus treble actual damages under Section 52.
- **Ralph Act (Section 51.7)** — violence or threats of violence motivated by the victim's actual or perceived protected characteristic.

These claims can stack with the Bane Act if the facts support them.

Corpus references:
- `standards/cal_civ_code_51/` (Unruh)
- `standards/cal_civ_code_51_7/` (Ralph Act)

---

## Step 9: Government Entity Liability

If suing a government entity (city, county, state agency), California allows respondeat superior liability for Bane Act claims through Government Code Section 815.2. This is broader than federal Monell liability, which does not allow respondeat superior.

Under Section 815.2, a public entity is liable for injury caused by an act or omission of an employee within the scope of employment, if the act would make the employee personally liable. You do not need to prove an official policy or custom.

Corpus reference: `standards/gov_code_815_2/`

# State Constitutional Privacy Claim (Article I, Section 1)

Operational workflow for bringing a claim under the California Constitution's right to privacy.

---

## Step 1: Identify the Legally Protected Privacy Interest

California recognizes three categories of privacy interests under Article I, Section 1:

1. **Informational privacy** — the right to control personal information. This includes medical records, financial records, sexual history, communications, and other data that a reasonable person would consider private.
2. **Autonomy privacy** — the right to make personal decisions without government interference. This includes decisions about family, reproduction, appearance, and personal associations.
3. **Physical privacy** — the right to be free from physical intrusions into private spaces. This includes the home, the body, and personal effects.

Identify which category applies and describe the specific information, decision, or space that was invaded.

Corpus reference: `standards/cal_const_art1_sec1/current/cal_const_art1_sec1_text.txt`

---

## Step 2: Allege a Reasonable Expectation of Privacy

The plaintiff must have had a reasonable expectation of privacy in the specific context. This is an objective test — would a reasonable person in the plaintiff's position expect the information, decision, or space to be private?

Factors that support a reasonable expectation:

- The information was not publicly available.
- The plaintiff took steps to maintain privacy (passwords, locks, confidentiality agreements).
- Social norms treat the subject as private (medical conditions, financial status, family matters).
- The setting was private (home, doctor's office, private conversation).

Factors that undermine a reasonable expectation:

- The information was shared publicly or with many people.
- The plaintiff voluntarily disclosed the information.
- The setting was public.
- The information is part of a public record.

---

## Step 3: Allege a Serious Invasion

The invasion must be serious. A minor or trivial intrusion does not give rise to a constitutional privacy claim. Under the Hill v. NCAA (1994) framework, the invasion must be sufficiently serious in its nature, scope, and actual or potential impact to constitute an egregious breach of the social norms underlying the privacy right.

Describe:

- What the defendant did (the specific act of intrusion or disclosure).
- The scope of the invasion (how much information was exposed, how many people saw it, how long the intrusion lasted).
- The impact on the plaintiff (emotional distress, financial harm, damaged relationships, chilling effect on future conduct).

Corpus reference: `standards/cal_const_art1_sec1/case_law/interpretive/hill_v_ncaa_1994/holding.md`

---

## Step 4: Address the Defendant's Justification

California's privacy right is not absolute. The defendant may offer a justification for the invasion, and the court will balance the plaintiff's privacy interest against the defendant's competing interest.

Common justifications:

- **Public safety** — the government claims the intrusion was necessary to protect public safety.
- **Law enforcement** — the intrusion was part of a criminal investigation with proper authorization.
- **Business necessity** — an employer or business claims the information was needed for legitimate operations.
- **Public interest** — the information is newsworthy or serves the public good.

The balancing test weighs the seriousness of the invasion against the strength of the justification. A weak justification will not excuse a serious invasion.

If suing a private actor, the defendant's justification may include contractual consent, legitimate business interest, or statutory authorization.

---

## Step 5: Emphasize — No State Action Required

This is the most powerful feature of California's constitutional privacy right. Under Hill v. NCAA (1994), Article I, Section 1 applies to private actors, not just the government. This means:

- A private employer who reads employee medical records can be sued under Article I, Section 1.
- A private company that discloses customer data can be sued.
- A private individual who conducts surveillance can be sued.

This is different from the federal Fourth Amendment, which only applies to government actors. California's privacy right is self-executing against all persons and entities.

Corpus references:
- `standards/cal_const_art1_sec1/case_law/interpretive/hill_v_ncaa_1994/holding.md`
- `standards/cal_const_art1_sec1/evolution/02_privacy_amendment_1972/context.md`

The privacy right was added to the California Constitution by ballot initiative in 1972 specifically to reach private actors. The ballot arguments make clear that the amendment was intended to address corporate and private data collection, not just government surveillance.

Corpus reference: `standards/cal_const_art1_sec1/case_law/interpretive/white_v_davis_1975/holding.md`

---

## Step 6: Pair with Bane Act If Government Actor Used Coercion

If the privacy invasion was committed by a government actor who used threats, intimidation, or coercion, add a Bane Act (Section 52.1) claim. The Bane Act provides attorney's fees and actual damages as a statutory enforcement mechanism for the constitutional right.

See `case_workflows/bane_act_claim.md` for the full Bane Act procedure.

Corpus reference: `standards/cal_civ_code_52_1/`

---

## Step 7: Pair with Article I, Section 13 If Search or Seizure Involved

If the privacy invasion involved a physical search of the person, home, or effects, or a seizure of property, add a claim under Article I, Section 13 (California's search-and-seizure provision).

Section 13 provides independent protections beyond the federal Fourth Amendment. California courts have historically interpreted Section 13 more broadly than the Fourth Amendment, although Proposition 8 (1982) limited the exclusionary rule to match federal standards.

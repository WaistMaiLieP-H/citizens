## The Wound: Two Centuries of Systematic Housing Exclusion

### Racially Restrictive Covenants (1880s–1948)

Before federal and state fair housing law, racially restrictive covenants were standard instruments in real estate transactions. These were deed provisions that literally prohibited sale or rental to non-white persons. A typical covenant read: "This property shall not be used or occupied by any person not of the white or Caucasian race."

These were not fringe instruments. They were standard practice, enforced by real estate professional associations, required by the Federal Housing Administration's underwriting standards (1934–1948), and used to create and maintain racially segregated neighborhoods across California and the United States.

In California, racially restrictive covenants locked Black, Asian, and Mexican-American families out of entire cities and suburbs. The equity built in those neighborhoods by white homeowners — who could buy there while others could not — was unavailable to everyone else. That equity gap, compounded over decades, is a direct ancestor of the wealth gap between white and non-white families today.

**The 1948 turning point:** The U.S. Supreme Court held in *Shelley v. Kraemer* (1948) 334 U.S. 1 that racially restrictive covenants are unenforceable in court — courts cannot enforce private discrimination. But covenants remained on the deeds. They remained in the chain of title. They signaled to buyers and real estate agents which neighborhoods were "intended" for whom.

California's §12955(l) directly addresses this: the **existence** of a restrictive covenant in a document is itself a §12955 violation, regardless of whether accompanied by a statement that the covenant is void.

### Redlining (1934–1968)

From 1934, the Federal Housing Administration systematically refused to guarantee mortgages in neighborhoods with non-white residents. FHA underwriting manuals explicitly listed "infiltration of inharmonious racial groups" as a negative factor for neighborhood ratings. Neighborhoods were graded A (best) through D (hazardous). D-rated neighborhoods — "redlined" on maps — were almost entirely Black or immigrant neighborhoods. No FHA-backed mortgage money flowed there.

Banks followed the FHA's lead. Whole communities were denied access to the mortgage market that was building middle-class white America after World War II. Black veterans eligible for GI Bill benefits could not use those benefits to buy homes in neighborhoods where lenders would finance. White veterans could.

The 30-year mortgages taken out by white homeowners in the 1940s and 1950s were paid off by the 1970s and 1980s, leaving those families with free-and-clear assets to pass to the next generation. The families who were locked out during those decades — who could not build that equity — had nothing to pass. The compounding effect of that 30-year exclusion is not over.

### Blockbusting (1940s–1970s)

After *Shelley v. Kraemer* (1948) made restrictive covenants unenforceable, real estate speculators developed blockbusting: they induced white homeowners to sell quickly (at below-market prices) by warning them that Black families were moving into the neighborhood and property values would collapse. The speculators then sold those properties to Black buyers at above-market prices, with the same warnings used to drive white flight.

Blockbusting combined racial fear, predatory pricing, and fraudulent representation to extract wealth from both white and Black homeowners simultaneously — while the speculators profited from the spread. §12955(h) is the specific anti-blockbusting provision.

### The California Proposition 14 Wound (1964–1967)

In 1964 — one year after the Rumford Fair Housing Act was passed — California voters approved Proposition 14 by a 65%–35% margin. Proposition 14 was a constitutional amendment that:

1. Repealed the Rumford Fair Housing Act
2. Prohibited the California Legislature from enacting any fair housing law without a statewide vote
3. Declared that every property owner had the absolute right to sell or rent to "such person or persons as he, in his absolute discretion, chooses"

In explicit terms: California voters constitutionally enshrined the right to refuse to sell or rent to Black people.

Proposition 14 was struck down by the California Supreme Court in *Reitman v. Mulkey* (1967) and affirmed by the U.S. Supreme Court. But between 1964 and 1967, California had no fair housing law. Housing discrimination was not only lawful — it was constitutionally protected at the state level.

The sponsors of Proposition 14 understood what they were doing. The campaign materials were explicit about protecting property owners from being "forced" to sell to or rent to "any person regardless of race." Sixty-five percent of California voters agreed.

### What Was Preserved by Systematic Exclusion

The wounds of redlining, restrictive covenants, and blockbusting were not erased by the 1968 Fair Housing Act or California's §12955. They were fossilized into the existing distribution of property ownership and equity. The wealth gap in homeownership between white and non-white families in California today is a direct inheritance of this history.

§12955(l)'s rule that even a void restrictive covenant is a §12955 violation acknowledges that the historical record itself — the deed, the covenant, the chain of title — has ongoing discriminatory meaning. The wound doesn't disappear when the law changes. It persists in the paper.

## The Modern Forms of the Wound

### Appraisal Discrimination

Studies consistently show that homes in Black neighborhoods are appraised at lower values than identical homes in white neighborhoods, even controlling for all other variables. The FHA's 1930s-1940s "hazardous" neighborhood ratings became informal professional norms that persisted long after redlining was legally prohibited.

§12955(i)(2) — which specifically covers appraisers — was added because the legislature recognized that appraisal discrimination was an ongoing form of exclusion that the original statute didn't explicitly cover.

### Source of Income Discrimination

"No Section 8" policies functioned as racial exclusion in the post-civil-rights era: facially neutral, documented in widely discriminatory effect. SB 329 (2020) closed this loophole. But the 50+ years between the FHA's passage and SB 329's effective date represented a half-century window during which landlords in California could legally post "no Section 8" signs.

### The Conservatorship / Controlled-Housing Wound

When a person is under conservatorship control, their housing choices are made by the conservator. If the conservatorship was established through fraud — as is alleged in the UA342 pattern — then every housing decision made by the conservator was made without the conserved person's meaningful consent. This is not a new wound. Control of Black and disabled persons' housing decisions through legal mechanisms (vagrancy laws, institutionalization, conservatorship) is part of the same historical arc as redlining and restrictive covenants: the law as an instrument for denying housing autonomy to people deemed incapable of exercising it.

§12955(m)'s perception rule and the ADA's housing provisions (42 USC §3604(f)) answer this modern form by saying: actual disability status is irrelevant. Treating a person as though they lack housing autonomy based on perceived disability is the violation.

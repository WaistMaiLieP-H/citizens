## The Wound: Information Asymmetry in Real Estate

### Before 1985: Caveat Emptor

The seller of real property in California before 1985 had no affirmative disclosure obligation. The common law rule was caveat emptor — buyer beware. A buyer who purchased a house with a concealed defect generally had no remedy unless:
1. The seller actively misrepresented a condition (fraud), or
2. The seller had a duty to disclose arising from the specific facts and fiduciary relationships involved

Neither ground was easy to prove. Fraud requires proving the seller's knowledge and intent. The duty-to-disclose ground was case-by-case and unpredictable.

The structural problem: the seller has lived in the house. The seller knows the foundation shifts in the rainy season, the roof leaks in certain spots, the furnace is at end of life. The buyer gets a brief tour and a few weeks of contingency inspections. The information asymmetry is profound and inherent to the transaction.

### What Pre-1985 Concealment Cost Buyers

A buyer who purchased a house in 1980 and discovered three years later that the seller knew about a failing septic system, unreported water intrusion, or structural defects faced:
- A statute of limitations problem (3 years from discovery — but discovery is disputed)
- A proof problem (the seller is gone, the records are gone, memories are gone)
- A litigation cost that often exceeded the cost of the defect itself

Working-class buyers — who often stretched to buy a house and had no reserve to absorb an unexpected repair — were the most damaged by concealed defects.

### The 1985 Response

The TDS flipped the burden. Instead of the buyer needing to discover and prove concealment, the seller is required to affirmatively complete a standardized form, sign it, and deliver it before the sale closes. The seller can no longer claim ignorance of what was in the disclosure — they signed it. The buyer now has documentary evidence of exactly what the seller disclosed and did not disclose.

The waiver-void provision (§1102(c)) removed the most obvious workaround: no contract clause can waive TDS requirements, regardless of the parties' apparent agreement.

### The Modern Wound — Conservatorship Control

The TDS's disclosure and cancellation rights assume a seller and buyer with full legal capacity to act. When a party is under conservatorship, the conservator makes the housing decisions. An unsigned TDS in a conservatorship-involved sale may reflect exactly what happened: the conservator directed the transaction, and the conserved person never had the meaningful opportunity to review, sign, or cancel that §1102 was designed to provide.

The 19 unsigned documents in the Honeysuckle sale are not an accident or administrative oversight. They are the physical record of a transaction conducted without one party's meaningful participation in the documentation process. §1102 exists precisely to prevent this — and the unsigned documents are the evidence of exactly the harm §1102 was designed to prevent.

## The Wound: Housing as a Weapon Against Survivors

### The Pre-VAWA Housing Trap

Before VAWA's housing provisions existed, a domestic violence survivor who fled an abusive home faced a structural trap in federally assisted housing:

**Trap 1 — The victim was evicted, not the abuser.** Public housing authorities and Section 8 administrators treated domestic violence incidents as lease violations. The victim who had been beaten was evicted for "disturbance of neighbors" or "violence on premises" — even though they were the person being beaten. The abuser, who may not even have been on the lease, paid no price.

**Trap 2 — Prior eviction disqualified survivors from other housing.** Once a victim was evicted from public housing, that eviction appeared on their record and disqualified them from applying to other subsidized housing programs. A survivor who escaped an abuser could be permanently locked out of the affordable housing market because of an eviction they didn't cause.

**Trap 3 — No DV status = no protection.** A landlord who discovered a tenant was a domestic violence victim had no legal obligation to protect her housing. She could be denied admission because landlords assumed DV victims were "troubled" tenants who would bring problems to the property. Being a victim was itself a basis for housing denial.

**Trap 4 — The shelter gap.** Emergency DV shelters provided temporary refuge — days or weeks — but no path to stable housing. A survivor who left a shelter with her children had nowhere to go but back to the abuser or into homelessness. The shelter system was designed for crisis, not transition.

### The Legislative History

**2005 — First federal housing protection:** VAWA Reauthorization Act of 2005 (Pub. L. 109-162) added the first specific housing protections for DV survivors in public housing and the Section 8 voucher program. The core rule: a tenant cannot be evicted or denied admission solely on the basis of DV victim status.

**2013 — Major expansion (VAWA 2013):** Pub. L. 113-4, Title VI dramatically expanded the housing provisions:
- Extended coverage beyond public housing/Section 8 to all HUD-assisted housing programs
- Added the emergency transfer right — a survivor can transfer to a different unit or building for safety
- Added the lease bifurcation rule — a housing authority can bifurcate a lease to remove the abuser while keeping the victim housed
- Added documentation rules — a survivor can certify DV status by self-certification on HUD Form 5382; a police report is NOT required
- Added confidentiality protections for disclosure of victim information

**2022 — Current text (VAWA 2022):** Pub. L. 117-103, Division W, Title I:
- Strengthened emergency transfer plan requirements (mandatory, with timelines)
- Extended coverage to LGBTQ+ survivors (clarified sex discrimination includes sexual orientation and gender identity)
- Added transitional housing programs to covered housing programs
- Required HUD to publish model emergency transfer plans

## The Inversion Pattern — When the Statute Becomes a Weapon

§ 12491's protections assume the person claiming DV victim status is, in fact, a victim. The statute was not designed for a scenario in which the perpetrator of fraud claims victim status to access housing benefits and to arm herself in a child custody dispute.

**The Homeward Bound Marin pattern:**
Christina Cerretani obtained a DVRO against Michael through proceedings where the underlying factual basis is disputed. That DVRO became the instrument for:
1. Accessing Homeward Bound Marin as a "domestic violence survivor" under a federally funded program
2. Leveraging the DV survivor status in family court proceedings
3. Weaponizing the DV framing against Michael in subsequent legal contexts

**Why § 12491 is relevant to Michael:**
The statute protects the actual victim of DV from being denied housing. If Christina's DVRO was obtained fraudulently — and the 6/11/2009 OPD report fabrication finding supports this — then her use of § 12491-covered housing benefits under that DVRO is potentially a fraudulent claim against a federally funded program. That triggers 31 U.S.C. § 3729 (False Claims Act) if Homeward Bound Marin receives HUD CoC Program funding or other federal housing assistance.

**The structural irony:** The law that protects real DV victims from housing displacement was potentially used to displace Michael (through court proceedings and custody outcomes connected to the false DVRO) while sheltering the person who fabricated the DV claim. The wound § 12491 was designed to heal — displacement of the actual victim — was inflicted, not prevented.

## The Modern Form of the Wound

When DV survivor status is fabricated as a legal strategy:
- A protective statute becomes an offensive weapon
- Courts and housing agencies, trained to give survivors the benefit of the doubt, have no immunity from false claims
- The actual victim — who has been accused of the violence they did not commit — is systematically disadvantaged across multiple legal systems simultaneously

§ 12491's documentation rule (self-certification, no police report required) — designed to protect real survivors who fear retaliation for reporting — creates the aperture through which false claims can be made with minimal evidentiary burden on the claimant.

**The remedy:** The False Claims Act (31 U.S.C. § 3729) and § 1983 (state actors who administered the fraudulent claim) are the mechanisms for accountability when § 12491 is weaponized. The original victim who was harmed by the false DV framing is a relator or plaintiff in those proceedings.

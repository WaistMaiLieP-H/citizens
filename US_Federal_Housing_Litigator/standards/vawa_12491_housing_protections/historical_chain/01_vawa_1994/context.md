# Historical Chain — Layer 1: VAWA 1994 — Housing Gap
## 34 U.S.C. § 12491 — Origin of the Legislative Need

**Era:** 1990–2005 (pre-housing-protection period)
**Relevant law:** Violence Against Women Act of 1994, Pub. L. 103-322 (did NOT include housing protections)

---

## What VAWA 1994 Did and Did Not Do

### What It Did
The original Violence Against Women Act (1994) was a landmark criminal law and victim services statute:
- Enhanced federal penalties for sex crimes
- Interstate domestic violence and stalking crimes made federal offenses
- Mandatory restitution for sex offense victims
- $1.6 billion over six years for law enforcement, prosecution, and victim services
- Established the Office on Violence Against Women within DOJ

### What It Did Not Do
VAWA 1994 did not address **housing** as a standalone domain. The omission was documented but not corrected in the original legislation. The focus was on criminal law reform and emergency service funding.

---

## The Evidence Base for Housing Harm (1994–2005)

Between 1994 and the 2005 Reauthorization, multiple studies and HUD reports documented what the original VAWA missed:

**Homelessness data:**
- Studies by the National Coalition for the Homeless documented that domestic violence was consistently among the top three causes of homelessness for families.
- HUD data from the late 1990s showed that women leaving violent relationships cited inability to find affordable housing as the primary barrier to permanent departure.

**The eviction-for-violence problem:**
- Public housing authorities in multiple cities were evicting victims of domestic violence (not just perpetrators) because the incident of violence constituted a lease violation under "one strike" or "zero tolerance" policies.
- The "one strike" policy (formalized in the Clinton administration's 1996 HUD guidance) required housing authorities to evict any household where a member engaged in drug use or criminal activity — domestic violence incidents were classified as criminal activity.
- Result: the victim's call to police could trigger her own eviction.

**The documentation barrier:**
- Housing providers who wanted to accommodate DV victims (by offering transfers or lease modifications) had no legal authority to do so under existing law.
- Any modification of lease terms required following normal procedures — which meant the victim had to either stay in the unit with the perpetrator or leave voluntarily without any bridge to new housing.
- Police reports were the only documentation most providers recognized — and most DV victims did not report to police.

**Congressional testimony (2003–2004):**
Hearings before the Senate Judiciary Committee for VAWA 2005 received testimony from housing advocates, survivors, and local housing authority officials documenting:
- Prevalence of victim-eviction policies at public housing authorities
- The documentation barrier preventing access to informal housing accommodations
- The need for a federal statutory framework that housing providers could rely on to justify victim-protective actions

---

## Why Congress Did Not Include Housing in VAWA 1994

The 1994 Act was already a politically difficult passage. Senator Biden (the bill's primary sponsor) prioritized criminal law reform and funding streams — areas with broader bipartisan support. Housing was a more complex regulatory domain involving HUD, public housing authorities, private landlords, and federalism concerns. The decision was strategic: pass the achievable, come back for the rest.

The Housing Opportunities for Victims of Violence provisions were drafted for the 2000 Reauthorization but did not make it through the Senate. They were finally included, in limited form (public housing and Section 8 only), in the 2005 Reauthorization.

---

## The Wound

The wound is this: Between 1994 and 2005, a woman in a federally subsidized housing unit who called 911 on a violent partner could lose her housing because of her own call. The federal government funded the housing that evicted her. The federal government funded the domestic violence services she would need after losing housing. The two funding streams — HUD housing and DOJ victim services — were not connected by any legal protection. The housing gap was the space where the criminal law reform met the survivor's daily reality and failed.

That wound is also the inversion vulnerability: a system designed to protect self-reporting survivors, without adversarial inquiry, creates a documentation pathway that is exploitable by someone prepared to fabricate the underlying facts.

# Evolution Layer 3 — Current State: VAWA 2022
## Strengthened Enforcement, LGBTQ+ Inclusion, Mandatory Transfer Plans

**Era:** 2022–present
**Enacted:** Violence Against Women Act Reauthorization Act of 2022, Pub. L. 117-103, Division W, Title I
**Signed:** March 15, 2022
**Codified at:** 34 U.S.C. § 12491 (Title 34 recodification from 42 U.S.C. § 13925 family)

---

## What VAWA 2022 Added or Strengthened

### 1. LGBTQ+ Inclusion Expressly Codified

VAWA 2022 explicitly extended coverage to LGBTQ+ survivors. The statute now expressly provides that sex discrimination includes sexual orientation and gender identity for purposes of § 12491 protections. This addressed years of uncertainty about whether LGBTQ+ DV survivors were covered.

**Practical effect:** A gay, lesbian, bisexual, transgender, or nonbinary survivor of intimate partner violence now has explicit statutory protection — not just administrative guidance.

### 2. Mandatory Emergency Transfer Plans with Timelines

VAWA 2013 required housing providers to have emergency transfer plans but did not specify timelines. VAWA 2022 made emergency transfer plans mandatory and required HUD to publish model plans with specific response timelines.

**Under current law:** A covered housing provider must:
- Have an emergency transfer plan in place
- Process emergency transfer requests with urgency
- Document reasons if an emergency transfer cannot be made immediately

### 3. Expanded Covered Programs

VAWA 2022 added additional transitional and supportive housing programs to the § 12491 definition of "covered housing program," ensuring that the full range of federal housing assistance — not just the original public housing / Section 8 programs — carries VAWA protections.

### 4. Strengthened Confidentiality

VAWA 2022 tightened confidentiality protections for victim information. Housing providers face liability for unauthorized disclosure.

## Current § 12491 Framework Summary

**Trigger:** Individual is a victim of domestic violence, dating violence, sexual assault, or stalking
**Protected actions (cannot be done on basis of victim status):**
1. Denial of admission to covered program
2. Denial of housing assistance or continued assistance
3. Eviction
4. Imposition of fees or other consequences for the incident

**Documentation:** Self-certification on HUD Form 5382 OR third-party letter OR court order OR medical documentation. Police report NOT required.

**Emergency transfer:** Available upon request when victim is in danger.

**Confidentiality:** Victim information protected from disclosure.

**Enforcement:**
- HUD administrative enforcement (complaints to HUD Office of Fair Housing and Equal Opportunity)
- Private right of action — status in 9th Circuit: uncertain; some district courts have recognized implied right; verify before filing standalone § 12491 claim
- § 1983 against state and local government actors who administer covered programs
- False Claims Act (31 U.S.C. § 3729) where federal program funds are obtained through fraudulent DV claims

---

## The False Claims Act Intersection

Where a person falsely certifies DV victim status on HUD Form 5382 to obtain access to a federally funded housing program, that false certification is a "false claim" within the meaning of 31 U.S.C. § 3729(a)(1)(A). Elements:
1. A false statement or claim — the Form 5382 self-certification
2. To a federal program — any § 12491 covered housing program receiving HUD funds
3. Made to obtain money or property — housing assistance provided through the program
4. Knowingly — actual knowledge or deliberate ignorance or reckless disregard

**Qui tam:** A private relator (the person harmed by the fraud) may file a False Claims Act qui tam action under 31 U.S.C. § 3730(b). The government may intervene or decline; if the government declines, the relator may proceed and retains 25–30% of any recovery.

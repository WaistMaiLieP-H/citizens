# Evolution Layer 1 — VAWA 2005 Housing Provisions
## First Federal Housing Protections for DV Survivors

**Era:** 2005
**Enacted:** Violence Against Women and Department of Justice Reauthorization Act of 2005, Pub. L. 109-162
**Signed:** January 5, 2006
**Effective:** Immediately upon enactment for public housing; phased implementation for Section 8

---

## What the 2005 Act Created

For the first time, federal law prohibited housing providers in covered programs from:
1. Denying admission to a public housing unit or Section 8 voucher program participant solely because the applicant is a victim of domestic violence, dating violence, or stalking
2. Terminating housing assistance or evicting a tenant solely because the tenant is a victim of DV, dating violence, or stalking
3. Charging fees or other consequences solely on the basis of DV victim status

**Coverage in 2005:** Limited to:
- Public housing (42 U.S.C. § 1437 et seq.)
- Section 8 housing choice voucher program (42 U.S.C. § 1437f)

**Sexual assault** was not initially listed as a covered crime; it was added in VAWA 2013.

---

## What Was NOT Included in 2005

- No private right of action (enforcement through HUD only)
- No emergency transfer right
- No lease bifurcation authority
- No documentation / self-certification rule (how a victim proved DV status was unclear)
- No confidentiality protections for victim information
- No coverage of other HUD-assisted programs (HOME, CDBG, McKinney-Vento)

These gaps meant that 2005 protections were largely paper rights without enforcement mechanisms.

---

## Why 2005 Was Not Enough

The covered program limitation was the critical gap. Most federal housing assistance flows through programs other than public housing and Section 8 vouchers. Survivors in HOME-funded housing, rural HUD programs, low-income housing tax credit (LIHTC) properties, or McKinney-Vento funded transitional housing had no § 12491 protection. They remained subject to the pre-VAWA trap: DV incidents as lease violations, prior eviction bars, denial based on victim status.

The absence of a lease bifurcation rule also left housing authorities without a clear tool to remove the abuser while protecting the victim. The practical result: housing authorities often evicted both parties rather than navigate the legal complexity of removing only the abuser.

# GOV §§ 12989.1 / 12989.2 — Reasoning

**Standard:** gov_12989_2_feha_housing_remedies
**Layer:** current/reasoning
**Status:** VERIFIED (live MCP fetch 2026-04-13)

---

## The Problem This Statute Solves

Housing discrimination often destroys the largest asset a family will ever own. A discriminatory home sale, a discriminatory refusal to finance, a forced sale under undue influence — these are not abstract violations. They are wealth-extraction events. The discrimination converts home equity into someone else's asset. Without a robust civil remedy — actual damages, punitive damages, attorney fees — the violation is costless to commit. The discriminating party keeps the equity. The victim has no legal mechanism to recover it.

GOV §12989.2 is California's answer to this problem: a civil action that reaches the full scope of discriminatory harm, awards punitive damages to deter future conduct, and shifts attorney fees to make enforcement economically viable for plaintiffs who have already lost significant assets.

---

## Why No Exhaustion

The decision to allow civil actions without CRD exhaustion (§12989.1(b)) reflects a structural lesson from Title VII employment discrimination law: administrative exhaustion requirements create bottlenecks that delay justice and allow discriminators to benefit from bureaucratic delay. The legislature removed that bottleneck for housing cases. The result is a direct path from injury to court.

---

## The Ongoing Practice Rule

The SOL in §12989.1(a) runs from the OCCURRENCE or TERMINATION of the practice, whichever is last. This language captures ongoing discrimination. A discriminatory home lending arrangement that continues to charge different rates, a deed fraud scheme in which proceeds continue to flow to the wrongdoers, a continuing pattern of housing denial — all of these have SOLs that do not begin until the ongoing practice stops. The Honeysuckle equity dispersal may still be ongoing, which means the SOL may not yet have run.

---

## Historical Loss — Without §§ 12989.1 / 12989.2

Before the FEHA housing enforcement provisions were in place, a person subjected to housing discrimination had only:
- A federal FHA claim with its own procedural requirements
- A possible common-law fraud claim with a shorter SOL and no punitive damages as a matter of right
- No attorney fee shifting to make litigation economically viable for plaintiffs who had already lost equity

The result was that housing discrimination claims were often economically irrational to bring. The cost of litigation exceeded the recoverable damages when no fee shifting was available and the discriminating party's resources could outlast the plaintiff's. §12989.2's fee-shifting provision changes the economics: a plaintiff with a valid discrimination claim can retain an attorney on a contingency basis knowing that attorney fees are recoverable if they prevail.

---

## The State-Agency Offset Provision

The final sentence of §12989.2 — allowing courts to offset money owed by defendants to state agencies against the judgment — is an unusual provision that reflects a practical reality: defendants who engage in housing discrimination may simultaneously have state contracts, state subsidies, or other obligations to state agencies. The offset provision is a collection mechanism, not a substantive remedy. If any defendant in the Honeysuckle chain has a state contract, outstanding state agency debt, or pending state payment — the court may apply that toward the judgment.

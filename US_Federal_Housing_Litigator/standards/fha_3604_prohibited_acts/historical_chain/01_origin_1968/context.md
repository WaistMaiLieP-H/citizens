# Historical Chain — Layer 1: Origin 1968
## 42 U.S.C. § 3604 — Civil Rights Act of 1968, Title VIII

**Era:** 1948–1968 (pre-FHA architecture and legislative trigger)
**Law enacted:** Civil Rights Act of 1968, Title VIII, Pub. L. 90-284, signed April 11, 1968

---

## The Architecture That Required the Rule

### Federal Complicity in Segregation

The Fair Housing Act was not enacted to correct a purely private market failure. The federal government was a central architect of racially segregated housing through:

**Federal Housing Administration (FHA) Underwriting Manual (1938):**
The FHA's actuarial manual directed appraisers to lower property valuations in racially mixed neighborhoods and explicitly recommended racial covenants as a method of maintaining neighborhood "stability." The manual stated that "if a neighborhood is to retain stability it is necessary that properties shall continue to be occupied by the same social and racial classes." This directive channeled mortgage insurance — the engine of post-war suburban growth — away from minority neighborhoods.

**Home Owners' Loan Corporation (HOLC) Redlining Maps:**
HOLC color-coded neighborhood maps assigned "D" (red) designations to minority neighborhoods, making them ineligible for refinancing under HOLC programs. Private lenders adopted the same maps. The term "redlining" entered the language from these literal colored-map designations.

**Veterans Administration Loan Guarantee Program:**
VA loans under the Servicemen's Readjustment Act of 1944 (GI Bill) followed FHA appraisal standards, systematically excluding Black veterans from the suburban housing boom that built the American middle class. A 1947 study found that in Mississippi, only two of 3,229 VA-guaranteed loans went to Black veterans.

### Private Market Mechanisms

**Racial Covenants:**
Until *Shelley v. Kraemer*, 334 U.S. 1 (1948) (state enforcement unconstitutional), deed covenants routinely prohibited sale to non-white buyers. After *Shelley*, the covenants remained in deeds but could not be judicially enforced — yet continued to exert informal market pressure.

**Steering:**
Real estate brokers maintained dual market systems, directing white buyers to white neighborhoods and Black buyers to Black neighborhoods. Membership in professional associations (National Association of Real Estate Boards) included an ethics code provision until 1950 that real estate agents should "never be instrumental in introducing into a neighborhood... members of any race or nationality... whose presence will clearly be detrimental to property values."

**Blockbusting:**
Agents would purchase a property on a block, arrange a sale to a Black family, then canvas white neighbors with panic-selling tactics ("the neighborhood is changing, sell now before values drop"), purchase at depressed prices, and resell to Black buyers at inflated prices who had no other market option. The mechanism profited from racial fear and exploited both sides.

**Denial:**
Outright refusal to show, negotiate, or transact with minority buyers — with no legal consequence until 1968.

## Legislative Trigger: 1968

**Kerner Commission (February 29, 1968):**
The National Advisory Commission on Civil Disorders, appointed after the 1967 urban uprisings, concluded: "Our nation is moving toward two societies, one black, one white — separate and unequal." Housing discrimination was identified as a primary mechanism. The Commission explicitly recommended a federal fair housing law.

**Assassination of Dr. Martin Luther King, Jr. (April 4, 1968):**
Three years of Senate filibuster had blocked fair housing legislation. The assassination provided the political juncture that broke the impasse. President Johnson signed the Civil Rights Act of 1968 — including Title VIII — on April 11, 1968, seven days after the assassination.

**Congressional findings embedded in the Act:**
The legislative history reflects Congress's determination that discriminatory housing practices "have a direct and substantial adverse effect on the interstate commerce" — establishing the Commerce Clause basis — and that equal opportunity in housing is a matter of national policy.

## What § 804 (Now § 3604) Originally Prohibited

The 1968 Act's § 804 prohibited discrimination in housing transactions based on **race, color, religion, and national origin**. It covered:
- Refusal to sell or rent (§ 804(a))
- Discriminatory terms and conditions (§ 804(b))
- Discriminatory advertising (§ 804(c))
- Misrepresentation of availability (§ 804(d))
- Blockbusting (§ 804(e))

**Sex was NOT in the original 1968 Act.** It was added in 1974.
**Disability and familial status were NOT in the original 1968 Act.** They were added in 1988.

## Same Year: Jones v. Alfred H. Mayer Co. (1968)

*Jones v. Alfred H. Mayer Co.*, 392 U.S. 409 (1968), decided June 17, 1968 — two months after the FHA's enactment — held that 42 U.S.C. § 1982 (the Civil Rights Act of 1866's property clause) bars all racial discrimination in the sale or rental of property, public or private. The Court held that § 1982 was a valid exercise of Congress's Thirteenth Amendment enforcement power to eliminate the "badges and incidents of slavery," which included racially stratified property ownership.

The FHA and § 1982 thus operate concurrently for race-based claims: § 1982 has no explicit SOL (courts apply state analogues, typically longer than 2 years); the FHA provides a comprehensive enforcement structure but a 2-year SOL.

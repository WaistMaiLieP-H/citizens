# Historical Chain — Layer 2: FHAA 1988
## Fair Housing Amendments Act of 1988 — Disability + Familial Status Added

**Era:** 1968–1988 (gaps in original FHA coverage and the political campaign for FHAA)
**Law enacted:** Fair Housing Amendments Act of 1988, Pub. L. 100-430, signed September 13, 1988

---

## The Gaps the Original 1968 Act Left

### Adults-Only Housing and Familial Status Exclusion

In the two decades after the original FHA's enactment, a practice became widespread and legally unaddressed: **"adults only" complexes**. Landlords and condominium associations adopted policies explicitly restricting occupancy to adults, excluding families with children under 18. Surveys conducted in the 1980s found that in some markets, a majority of rental units were unavailable to families with children.

Congress found in 1988 that this practice had become "so pervasive that families with children who seek housing in the rental market find that a significant portion of the available rental housing is closed to them solely on the basis of their family status." The absence of any prohibition in the 1968 Act had allowed adults-only restrictions to proliferate without legal risk.

### Disability Exclusion from Mainstream Housing

Persons with physical and mental disabilities faced systematic exclusion through mechanisms the 1968 Act did not reach:

**No-modification policies:** Standard lease terms prohibiting alterations to the premises — regardless of whether the alteration was necessary to make the unit accessible or functional for a person with a disability. A wheelchair user might be barred from installing grab bars; a person with visual impairment from making wiring modifications for alerting systems.

**"Community character" standards:** HOA and condo association rules about "appearance" or "character" that, while facially neutral, functioned to exclude persons with visible disabilities, assistive equipment, or medical apparatus.

**Lease conditions:** Requirements that all residents be physically capable of self-evacuation, which could operate to deny housing to mobility-impaired persons even in buildings with accessible egress.

**Group homes:** Community resistance to group homes for persons with developmental disabilities, backed by facially neutral zoning ordinances requiring single-family occupancy — effectively maintaining institutional placement as the only option.

The 1986 Supreme Court decision in *City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center*, 473 U.S. 432 (1985), had invalidated a zoning ordinance requiring special permits for group homes for persons with intellectual disabilities under the rational basis standard, but left the broader statutory framework undeveloped.

## The FHAA 1988 Legislative Record

Congress held extensive hearings from 1987 to 1988. Key findings:
- Disability advocates documented systematic exclusion from mainstream housing, forcing institutionalization for persons who could live independently with accessible housing.
- Child-welfare advocates documented the harms to families of housing unavailability.
- Real estate industry testimony acknowledged the practices but contested the legislative remedy.

**Representative James Sensenbrenner (R-WI)** and **Senator Edward Kennedy (D-MA)** were primary sponsors. The bill passed the House 376-23 and the Senate 94-3 — one of the largest bipartisan margins for a civil rights bill.

## What FHAA 1988 Added to § 3604

### New Protected Classes

**Handicap (disability) — § 3604(f)(1)-(3):**
Three-part definition of "handicap" at § 3602(h): physical or mental impairment substantially limiting a major life activity; record of such impairment; or regarded as having such impairment. (Mirrors the Rehabilitation Act of 1973, § 504 standard.)

**Familial status — § 3604(a)-(e) as amended:**
Defined at § 3602(k): having one or more children under 18 with a parent or legal guardian, or being pregnant, or being in the process of securing legal custody.

### New Substantive Rights for Persons with Disabilities — § 3604(f)(3)

The most operationally significant addition: affirmative obligations on housing providers.

**(A) Reasonable Modifications:** Landlord must *permit* the person with a disability to make reasonable modifications to the dwelling at the person's expense. The landlord may require restoration upon vacating.

**(B) Reasonable Accommodations:** Housing provider must make reasonable accommodations in rules, policies, practices, or services where necessary to afford the person equal opportunity to use and enjoy the dwelling.

These provisions imported from the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 (§ 504) the principle that equal access sometimes requires affirmative steps, not merely prohibition of differential treatment.

### Strengthened Enforcement

- Administrative enforcement: HUD authority to file complaints and seek civil penalties ($10,000 for first offense, $25,000 for second, $50,000 for subsequent)
- ALJ proceedings as an alternative to federal court
- Expanded injunctive relief authority
- Attorney's fees provision

## Connection to Case Docket

**Case #19 — Honeysuckle disability nexus:**
Michael's documented disability status (bilateral ankle injuries, SIRVA) activates § 3604(f)(1) (discriminatory sale terms due to disability) and § 3604(f)(2) (discriminatory terms and conditions of sale connected to disability). If the coerced or manipulated sale was connected in any way to disability-based targeting, the FHAA 1988 additions are directly on point.

**Case #2 — Bilateral ankles:**
If any housing-related action (lease termination, denial of modification, denial of accommodation) was connected to Michael's ankle disability, § 3604(f)(3)(A)-(B) applies.

**Reasonable accommodation in legal proceedings:**
The ADA Title II / § 504 companion track (separate standard) covers court-access accommodation, but § 3604(f) covers the housing itself. The two tracks run in parallel where housing is the locus of discrimination.

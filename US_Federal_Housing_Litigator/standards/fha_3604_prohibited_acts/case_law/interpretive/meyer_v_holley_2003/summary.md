# Case Law — Meyer v. Holley
## 537 U.S. 280 (2003) — Vicarious Liability Under the FHA

**Citation:** Meyer v. Holley, 537 U.S. 280 (2003)
**Court:** United States Supreme Court
**Decided:** January 22, 2003
**Holding:** The Fair Housing Act imposes liability on principals for the discriminatory acts of their agents, but only under ordinary agency principles — not strict liability for corporate officers simply by virtue of their position.

---

## Facts

David Meyer, president and sole owner of Triad Realty, was sued individually along with Triad Realty for discriminatory housing practices. The Ninth Circuit had held that the FHA imposes vicarious liability on corporate officers regardless of whether they personally participated in or authorized the discriminatory act.

The Supreme Court reversed.

---

## Holding

**The FHA incorporates traditional agency-based vicarious liability:**
- The FHA does not impose strict vicarious liability on corporate officers merely because of their position
- Liability follows ordinary agency principles: a principal (employer) is vicariously liable for the discriminatory acts of an employee/agent acting within the scope of employment
- A corporate officer is personally liable only if:
  1. They personally participated in the discriminatory act, OR
  2. They directed, authorized, or ratified the discriminatory conduct of the agent

**What the Court rejected:** The Ninth Circuit's rule that a corporate officer is automatically liable for the discriminatory acts of their employees simply by virtue of being the corporate officer.

---

## Vicarious Liability Framework Under Meyer v. Holley

| Defendant type | FHA liability standard |
|---|---|
| Employer/Principal | Vicariously liable for agent's acts within scope of employment |
| Corporate officer | Personally liable only if personally participated, directed, or ratified |
| Individual employee | Directly liable for own discriminatory acts |
| Franchisor | Vicariously liable only if franchisee is actual agent (not independent contractor) |

---

## Agency Principles That Apply

Under ordinary agency law incorporated by Meyer:
- **Respondeat superior:** Employer liable for employee's discriminatory acts within scope of employment
- **Scope of employment:** Acts performed on behalf of employer, even if specifically forbidden, if within the kind of acts employee was hired to perform
- **Authorization/Ratification:** Principal who authorizes or ratifies discriminatory act shares liability

---

## Application to Michael's Cases

**Case #19 — Honeysuckle / Real estate transaction:**
- If a real estate broker, escrow officer, or title company employee committed discriminatory acts in the Honeysuckle transaction, the employing brokerage is vicariously liable under Meyer's ordinary agency standard
- Individual officers of the brokerage are liable only if they directed, authorized, or ratified the discriminatory conduct
- **Practical impact:** Name both the brokerage entity and the responsible supervising officer; allege the officer's specific direction or ratification of the discriminatory terms or misrepresentations

**HILLBERGMANN deed fraud:**
- If the fraud chain involved real estate professionals operating within their agency relationship, the principals behind the transaction (companies, supervising brokers) carry vicarious liability for their agents' discriminatory acts
- Officers who directed or ratified the fraudulent deed manipulation are personally exposed

---

## Doctrinal Significance

Meyer v. Holley confirmed that the FHA is subject to ordinary tort/agency principles unless Congress has explicitly displaced them. This means:
- Courts look to Restatement (Second) and (Third) of Agency for the liability framework
- Strict liability for corporate officers is unavailable under FHA
- But respondeat superior (employer liability for employee acts in scope) is fully available

---

## Subsequent Treatment

- Routinely applied in FHA cases involving employer/employee discriminatory conduct
- The "scope of employment" question is fact-specific and frequently litigated
- Some courts have extended Meyer's ordinary agency analysis to aiding and abetting questions under the FHA

# Civil RICO Origin — 1970 Organized Crime Control Act

## The wound this statute answered

By the late 1960s, organized crime had infiltrated legitimate businesses — not by force, but by
investment. Criminal organizations were using proceeds from traditional criminal activities
(narcotics, gambling, extortion) to acquire ownership or control of legitimate businesses, and
then using those businesses to launder money, shelter income from prosecution, and gain economic
power that further insulated criminal operations from law enforcement.

The existing legal framework had two failures:
1. **Conspiracy law** was limited to specific conspiracies. It did not address the infiltration of
   a legitimate business by an organized criminal group acting over time across multiple crimes.
2. **Forfeiture law** was primitive. Federal prosecutors could convict individuals but could not
   reach the business assets that had been built with criminal proceeds.

Senator John McClellan and the Nixon Justice Department proposed a statute that would:
- Define the criminal enterprise as the unit of prosecution (not just individual acts)
- Create forfeiture for enterprise assets derived from racketeering
- Give private victims a treble-damages remedy (borrowed from antitrust) to incentivize private enforcement

## The logical structure Congress chose

RICO's architecture mirrors antitrust law (particularly Sherman Act §1):
- **The enterprise** is the organizational structure (like a market)
- **The pattern of racketeering** is the predicate conduct (like anticompetitive acts)
- **§1962** defines four prohibited combinations of enterprise and racketeering
- **§1964(c)** creates the private treble-damages action (like Clayton Act §4)

The borrowing from antitrust was deliberate. Congress wanted the private-enforcement success of
antitrust law applied to organized crime's infiltration of legitimate business.

## What the 1970 statute did NOT anticipate

Congress did not anticipate that §1964(c)'s civil private right of action would be used primarily
against legitimate corporations and ordinary white-collar fraud, not organized crime. By the 1980s,
RICO had become a commonplace weapon in business litigation — fraud cases, securities cases,
commercial disputes.

In Sedima, S.P.R.L. v. Imrex Co. (1985), the Supreme Court rejected judicially imposed limitations
on RICO's reach and acknowledged that Congress had created an extraordinarily broad civil remedy.
Congress never narrowed the statute to limit it to organized crime.

## The "enterprise" as distinct from the "person"

A critical 1970 design choice: §1962 prohibits a "person" from conducting the affairs of an
"enterprise" through a pattern of racketeering. The person and the enterprise must be distinct
under §1962(c) (United States v. Turkette, 1981, confirmed enterprises can be purely criminal).

This "distinctness" requirement matters operationally: a corporation cannot be both the RICO
"person" and the RICO "enterprise" in a §1962(c) claim. The enterprise must be separate from
the defendant.

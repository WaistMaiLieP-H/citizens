# Civil RICO — Sedima (1985): Private Right Confirmed Broad

## The wound this decision answered

By the early 1980s, RICO had spread beyond organized crime into mainstream commercial litigation.
Plaintiffs in ordinary fraud and breach of contract cases were adding RICO counts, invoking the
treble damages and attorney fees that made any successful RICO claim enormously valuable.

Defendants — typically corporations facing RICO-enhanced fraud claims — argued that courts should
impose a "prior criminal conviction" requirement or a "racketeering injury" limitation: plaintiffs
should only be able to use §1964(c) if the predicate acts had already resulted in criminal
convictions, and the harm should be the distinctive kind associated with organized crime's
infiltration of legitimate business (the "mobbed up" injury), not ordinary fraud losses.

The Second Circuit adopted these limitations. The Supreme Court reversed.

## The Sedima holding: §1964(c) is as broad as it reads

**Sedima, S.P.R.L. v. Imrex Co., Inc., 473 US 479 (1985):**

1. No prior criminal conviction required. A civil plaintiff does not need a predicate criminal
   conviction before bringing a §1964(c) claim. The civil case can be the first proceeding.

2. No "racketeering injury" requirement. The injury need only be to "business or property" caused
   by the RICO violation — not a distinctive organized-crime-type injury.

3. The broad reach of civil RICO is Congress's problem, not the Court's. If RICO has metastasized
   beyond its organized crime origins, Congress can narrow it. Until then, §1964(c) means what it says.

## The logical shift post-Sedima

Before Sedima: lower courts were creating judge-made limitations on RICO to prevent overreach.
After Sedima: §1964(c) is available in any federal court for any injury to business or property
caused by a pattern of predicate racketeering acts, whether or not organized crime is involved.

The Court noted (in a passage often cited by defendants): RICO "has become a tool for everyday
fraud cases." Congress had the opportunity to narrow RICO after Sedima and chose not to.

## What Sedima did NOT address

Sedima left open the crucial question of what constitutes a "pattern." The Court noted the pattern
requirement deserves more than the minimal "two acts" reading but did not define it. That
question was left to H.J. Inc. v. Northwestern Bell (1989).

## Diff from prior stage

| Dimension | Pre-Sedima (1970-1984) | Post-Sedima (1985-present) |
|---|---|---|
| Prior conviction required | Many circuits: yes | No |
| "Racketeering injury" required | Some circuits: yes | No — ordinary business/property loss suffices |
| Pattern definition | Unresolved | Still unresolved (left to H.J. Inc.) |
| RICO applicability | Contested in commercial context | Confirmed: RICO is available in any commercial fraud |

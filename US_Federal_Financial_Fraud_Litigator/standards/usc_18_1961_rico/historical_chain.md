# Civil RICO Historical Chain — 18 USC §§1961-1968

## The wound (pre-1970)

By the late 1960s, organized crime had generated sufficient capital through traditional criminal
enterprises (narcotics, gambling, extortion) to purchase controlling interests in legitimate
American businesses. The infiltration of labor unions, construction firms, and commercial
businesses by Mafia-connected organizations was documented by the Kefauver Committee (1950-51)
and the McClellan Committee (1963-64). The problem was structural: no law targeted the
organizational structure of the criminal enterprise itself — only individual criminal acts.

Conviction for gambling, extortion, or narcotics trafficking could put individuals in prison but
left the enterprise intact and its assets untouched. The existing conspiracy law required a
specific conspiracy charge and did not reach the broader enterprise structure.

---

## 1970: RICO enacted — enterprise as the unit of prosecution

Congress enacted RICO as Title IX of the Organized Crime Control Act (Pub. L. 91-452). The
statute defined the "enterprise" as the unit of prosecution, created four §1962 prohibited
combinations of enterprises and racketeering, and borrowed the antitrust private treble-damages
remedy (Clayton Act §4) to create §1964(c)'s private civil right.

The Legislative history made clear the intent: reach organized crime's infiltration of legitimate
business by targeting the enterprise structure, not just individual criminal acts.

---

## 1970s-1980s: Civil RICO escapes organized crime context

Within a decade, plaintiffs' lawyers recognized that §1964(c)'s elements — two predicate acts
(easily satisfied by two fraudulent communications), an enterprise (any business relationship),
treble damages — were available in ordinary commercial and fraud cases. RICO counts proliferated
in securities fraud, commercial fraud, and business litigation.

---

## 1985: Sedima — private right confirmed; no limitations

The Supreme Court in Sedima, S.P.R.L. v. Imrex Co. (1985) rejected judicially-imposed barriers
(prior criminal conviction, racketeering-type injury) and confirmed §1964(c) as broadly available.
Justice Powell's dissent warned RICO had become "a tool for everyday fraud cases." Congress did
not respond with restrictions.

---

## 1989: H.J. Inc. — pattern = relatedness + continuity

The Supreme Court in H.J. Inc. v. Northwestern Bell (1989) finally gave content to "pattern":
two predicate acts are necessary but not sufficient — they must be related and continuous.
Open-ended and closed-ended continuity provide alternative paths to satisfy the requirement.
The pattern requirement prevents purely isolated two-act frauds from triggering RICO.

---

## 1993: Reves — enterprise conduct requires direction, not just facilitation

Reves v. Ernst & Young (1993) drew the operation-or-management line: §1962(c) requires the
defendant to have directed the enterprise, not merely served it. Outside professionals (accountants,
lawyers, consultants) who facilitate but do not manage are excluded from §1962(c) liability.

---

## 1996: Securities fraud exclusion added

RICO Private Securities Litigation Reform Act (1996) amended §1964(c) to preclude using
securities fraud predicates that would be actionable under the Exchange Act. Congress
pushed securities fraud out of RICO and back into the Exchange Act framework. Does not affect
non-securities fraud cases.

---

## 2002-2004: Identity theft added to predicate list

§1028 (identity theft) added to §1961(1)(B) in 2002; §1028A (aggravated identity theft) added
in 2004. This expansion completed the connection between RICO and modern financial identity crime.

---

## Current framework (2026)

Civil RICO is a powerful but demanding tool:
- **Powerful:** Treble damages + mandatory attorney fees; federal court; no prior criminal case needed
- **Demanding:** Pattern (relatedness + continuity per H.J. Inc.); enterprise must be distinct from person;
  defendants must have directed the enterprise (Reves); proximate cause (Holmes)

**The arc:** Organized crime solution (1970) → commercial fraud tool (1970s-80s) → confirmed broad reach
(Sedima 1985) → pattern requirement defined (H.J. Inc. 1989) → enterprise conduct requirement defined
(Reves 1993) → securities fraud exclusion (1996) → identity theft predicates added (2002/2004) → current
stable framework requiring pattern + enterprise direction + proximate cause.

# Evolution Stage 01 — Origin 1984
## Counterfeit Access Device and Computer Fraud and Abuse Act

### THE WOUND

In 1984 computer crime law was entirely undeveloped. The existing federal fraud statutes
(wire fraud §1343, mail fraud §1341) required the transmission of communications over
wire or mail — but a hacker who accessed a computer system without transmitting anything
fraudulent via wire might fall outside those statutes. There was also genuine legal
uncertainty about whether unauthorized computer access constituted theft, fraud, or
trespass — or whether existing criminal statutes reached it at all.

WarGames (1983 film) had dramatized the scenario of a teenager accessing NORAD through a
telephone-connected computer. The scenario was fictionalized but reflected real concerns:
dial-up modem connections to commercial mainframes, university systems, and government
computers were proliferating; unauthorized access was occurring; no clear federal statute
prohibited it.

Congress also faced the problem of financial institution computer fraud: bank employees
and external actors using computers to manipulate financial records, transfer funds, or
access account information without authorization. These acts were either prosecuted
under existing fraud statutes or not prosecuted at all.

### DESIGN AT ENACTMENT (1984)

The 1984 Act was narrow — it covered:
- Computers used exclusively by financial institutions or the federal government
- Classification-related information (national security)
- Financial records of financial institutions
- Access to federal government computers to commit fraud

It did not have a civil cause of action; it was purely criminal.

**Key gap:** The 1984 Act covered only government and financial institution computers.
Commercial computers, university systems, and personal computers were outside its scope.

### PROVENANCE

Counterfeit Access Device and Computer Fraud and Abuse Act of 1984
Pub. L. 98-473, §2102(a), 98 Stat. 2190 (Oct. 12, 1984)
Codified at 18 U.S.C. §1030

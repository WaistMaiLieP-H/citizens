# Doe v. Chao — Holding

**Citation:** 540 U.S. 614 (2004)
**Court:** Supreme Court of the United States
**Decided:** February 24, 2004
**Author:** Souter, J.

## Facts

Buck Doe was a Chinese national whose claim for workers' compensation benefits was being
processed by the Department of Labor. During the administrative proceedings, the Department
disclosed Doe's Social Security Number in court documents filed in a federal workers'
compensation proceeding. The disclosure was made to multiple parties. Doe sought damages
under 5 U.S.C. §552a(g)(1)(D) — the "intentional or willful" invasion of personal privacy
provision — arguing the disclosure was an intentional, clearly unwarranted invasion of privacy.

The district court dismissed for failure to state a claim. The Fourth Circuit reversed,
holding that an adverse effect on the individual was not required for recovery under
§(g)(1)(D). The government petitioned.

## Holding

The Supreme Court reversed in part:

**For §552a(g)(1)(C) claims** (failure to maintain accurate records causing adverse
determination): The plaintiff must prove **actual damages** to recover. The adverse
effect on rights, benefits, or privileges is a threshold element — it proves the
violation harmed the plaintiff — but specific monetary or other concrete damages
must be shown. A plaintiff cannot recover on the adverse determination alone.

**For §552a(g)(1)(D) claims** (intentional or willful invasion of personal privacy):
The statute provides a $1,000 minimum; actual damages are the alternative floor.
The Court left open whether emotional distress alone qualifies as "actual damages"
under the Act.

## Key Language

"[A]ctual damages" means proven, demonstrated harm — not merely the fact that a
violation occurred or that the plaintiff was adversely affected. The term carries its
ordinary meaning in federal damages law.

## Rules Distilled

1. §552a(g)(1)(C): Must prove actual damages — adverse determination establishes
   causation and violation, but damages still must be proven
2. §552a(g)(1)(D): $1,000 minimum floor; Court did not require proof of specific
   monetary loss when willfulness and privacy invasion are established
3. Emotional distress as "actual damages": unresolved — circuits continue to diverge;
   some allow it, some require economic loss
4. SSN disclosure without consent = potential §(g)(1)(D) violation if willful

## Significance

Post-Doe v. Chao, the strongest Privacy Act civil claims are those where:
(a) the lost benefit is calculable (disability payments, wages, specific dollar amounts), OR
(b) the violation is provably willful and intentional (§(g)(1)(D) $1,000 floor applies
    without needing to itemize monetary loss)

The SSA/DDS phantom contact scenario satisfies (a) — lost SSI/SSDI payments are
calculable — and potentially (b) if the false contact entries are provably intentional
rather than clerical error.

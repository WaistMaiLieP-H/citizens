# Evolution Stage 01 — Origin 1974
## Privacy Act of 1974, Pub. L. 93-579

### THE WOUND

By 1974 the federal government operated an estimated 858 data systems containing records
on American citizens. No statute governed what information could be collected, how long
it could be retained, whether it could be shared across agencies, or whether any citizen
had a right to know what the government had recorded about them.

The Watergate investigations (1972-1974) revealed the specific abuses: FBI maintaining
political dossiers; CIA domestic surveillance; military intelligence files on civilian
anti-war protesters; HEW sharing Social Security records across agencies without
authorization; IRS records shared with political opponents' campaigns.

The Church Committee (Senate Select Committee to Study Governmental Operations with
Respect to Intelligence Activities, 1975) later documented the full scope — but Congress
acted on the known record in December 1974 before the Church Committee completed its work.

The core wound: **a citizen could have a federal record containing false, outdated, or
defamatory information that was being shared across government and used to deny benefits
or privileges — with no knowledge, no ability to correct it, and no remedy.**

### DESIGN AT ENACTMENT

The 1974 Act established five core pillars:

**Pillar 1 — Transparency (§552a(e)(4)):**
Every agency maintaining a system of records must publish notice in the Federal Register
describing the system name, location, categories of individuals covered, categories of
records, routine uses, and storage policies. Citizens could at least know systems existed.

**Pillar 2 — Access (§552a(d)(1)):**
Each individual can request records the agency maintains on them. Agency must provide
copies within a reasonable time and at reasonable cost.

**Pillar 3 — Amendment (§552a(d)(2)-(4)):**
Individual who believes a record is inaccurate can demand amendment. Agency must
acknowledge within 10 days, complete review within 30 days. If agency refuses to amend,
individual can demand a statement of disagreement be placed in the file.

**Pillar 4 — Limitation on Disclosure (§552a(b)):**
No agency shall disclose records from a system of records to any person or agency
except with written consent of the individual — or under 12 enumerated exceptions
(law enforcement, routine use, statistical research, congressional oversight, etc.).

**Pillar 5 — Civil Remedy (§552a(g)):**
Four categories of civil action with damages, fees, and costs.

### LIMITATION AT ORIGIN

The 1974 Act did not address computer matching — the cross-agency comparison of records
databases to identify individuals appearing in multiple systems (welfare fraud detection,
Social Security verification, etc.). As computer matching grew through the 1980s, this
became the dominant form of records use the 1974 Act had not anticipated.

The civil remedy also had ambiguity: did "actual damages" require proving specific monetary
loss, or did the adverse determination itself suffice? Courts split on this question for
nearly 30 years until Doe v. Chao (2004).

### PROVENANCE

Privacy Act of 1974, Pub. L. 93-579, 88 Stat. 1896
Signed: December 31, 1974
Effective: September 27, 1975 (270-day delayed implementation to allow agencies to
comply with system of records publication requirements)

# Historical Chain — 5 U.S.C. §552a (Privacy Act of 1974)

## Chain Summary

The Privacy Act's evolution reflects a recurring pattern: Congress enacts a protection,
technology outpaces it, courts interpret ambiguous terms in competing directions, and
either Congress amends or the Supreme Court resolves the split.

## Stage 1 — Origin 1974 (Pub. L. 93-579)

**Wound:** Post-Watergate exposure of unchecked federal surveillance databases;
citizens had no right to know what records agencies kept about them, no ability to
correct false records, no remedy when false records caused harm.

**Response:** Privacy Act of 1974 — transparency, access, amendment, disclosure limits,
civil remedy. Five pillars. Four civil action categories.

**Gap left open:** Computer matching (bulk cross-agency database comparison) entirely
unaddressed. "Actual damages" undefined, inviting circuit split.

## Stage 2 — Computer Matching Amendment 1988 (Pub. L. 100-503)

**Wound:** Computer matching programs proliferated without any of the 1974 Act's
protections — no notice, no verification, no pre-action opportunity to challenge;
adverse actions (benefit suspension, termination) flowing from machine-generated
matches before individuals knew a match had occurred.

**Response:** Computer Matching and Privacy Protection Act (CMPPA) — matching agreements,
pre-action verification, 30-day response period, Data Integrity Boards.

**Gap left open:** "Actual damages" circuit split not addressed by Congress.

## Stage 3 — Doe v. Chao (2004) (540 U.S. 614)

**Wound:** Circuit split on "actual damages" — Fourth Circuit allowed recovery on
adverse determination alone; D.C. Circuit required proven monetary loss. Privacy Act
functioned differently depending on geography.

**Response:** Supreme Court resolved: actual damages must be proven for §(g)(1)(C)
claims. Adverse determination is the threshold; monetary harm must be shown.
§(g)(1)(D) $1,000 minimum survives — intentional/willful invasion = floor without
need to prove specific monetary harm.

**Gap left open:** Whether emotional distress alone qualifies as "actual damages"
remains unresolved across circuits.

## Current State

§552a as amended through 1988 CMPPA, interpreted through Doe v. Chao (2004):
- Agency must maintain accurate, relevant, timely, complete records
- Individual may access and challenge records via §(d) process
- Civil action available in four tracks
- §(g)(1)(C): actual damages required; adverse determination establishes violation/causation
- §(g)(1)(D): $1,000 minimum for intentional/willful invasion; Albright reckless-disregard
  standard defines "intentional or willful"
- 2-year SOL from date of violation or discovery of willful concealment

## Cases in Chain

1. Albright v. United States, 631 F.2d 915 (D.C. Cir. 1980) — intentional/willful standard
2. Doe v. Chao, 540 U.S. 614 (2004) — actual damages required for §(g)(1)(C)

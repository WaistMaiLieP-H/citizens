# Jewel v. NSA — Holding

**Citation:** Jewel v. National Security Agency, 673 F.3d 902 (9th Cir. 2011)
**Court:** United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit
**Decided:** December 29, 2011

## Note on Usage

Jewel v. NSA is included not as a controlling precedent on the merits but for its
analysis of §2511's scope and the government surveillance exception. The case addresses
the boundary between §2511's prohibition and the government surveillance carve-outs —
which is relevant for understanding what the statute covers vs. excludes in the context
of unauthorized private-party interception (which is NOT subject to the government
surveillance exceptions).

## Facts

Carolyn Jewel and other AT&T customers brought a class action against the NSA and AT&T
alleging that the NSA conducted warrantless mass surveillance of telecommunications
traffic through equipment installed in AT&T's facilities ("Room 641A" at AT&T's
San Francisco switching center). The plaintiffs alleged this violated the Wiretap Act
(§2511), the SCA, and the Fourth Amendment.

## Holding (Ninth Circuit — on standing and justiciability)

The Ninth Circuit held that the plaintiffs had standing — they had alleged a
particularized injury (their communications were included in the surveillance dragnet)
sufficient to establish Article III standing. The case was remanded for further
proceedings.

**On §2511 scope (dicta and analysis relevant here):**
The court's analysis confirmed that §2511 prohibits the interception of electronic
communications regardless of who is doing the intercepting — including government actors
when acting outside the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act (FISA) authorization.
The government surveillance exceptions (§2511(2)(a)(ii) for carrier maintenance,
§2511(2)(e) for FISA) are narrow and do not create a blanket government exception.

## Rules Distilled (relevant to private party interception)

1. §2511 prohibits interception by anyone — government or private party
2. Government surveillance exceptions (FISA, court-ordered) are narrow and specific
3. Carrier maintenance exception (§2511(2)(a)(i)) covers only monitoring in the
   ordinary course of business — not surveillance outside normal operations
4. Unauthorized mass access to telecommunications infrastructure = §2511 violation
5. Standing: each individual whose communications were intercepted has a particularized
   injury supporting §2511 civil action

## Significance

**For the SIM swap case:**
Jewel's analysis confirms that AT&T's facilitation of the SIM swap — even if AT&T
was deceived rather than complicit — does not fall within AT&T's §2511(2)(a)(i)
carrier maintenance exception. The maintenance exception covers monitoring "to protect
the rights or property of the provider" and "in the ordinary course of business" —
a fraudulent SIM port executed by deception is outside AT&T's ordinary business course.

**AT&T carrier exception analysis:**
AT&T will argue it was deceived — a passive victim — rather than a willing participant.
But §2511 analysis of AT&T's own systems matters if AT&T's equipment routed the
hijacked communications: the routing itself could implicate AT&T if AT&T had or
should have had technical means to detect the anomalous port.

**Separate from AT&T:** Ryan McClaran's interception through the ported SIM = no
exception applies. Jewel confirms the government surveillance exceptions don't help
private actors.

IN RE AT&T INC.
FCC Consent Decree — EB-TCD-14-00016243
Filed: April 8, 2015
$25 Million Settlement

(Regulatory enforcement action — not a court opinion)

---

AGENCY: Federal Communications Commission, Enforcement Bureau
RESPONDENT: AT&T Inc.
PROCEEDING: EB-TCD-14-00016243
RESULT: Consent Decree; $25 Million Civil Penalty

---

I. BACKGROUND AND FACTUAL FINDINGS

The FCC Enforcement Bureau investigated AT&T after discovering that call center employees at AT&T's overseas vendor facilities — in Mexico, Colombia, and the Philippines — had accessed customers' account information without authorization and provided it to third parties.

The unauthorized access involved:
- Customer account information including Social Security numbers (last four digits);
- Account PINs and passcodes used as CPNI authentication;
- Customer call records (CPNI per se);
- Device unlock codes generated from customers' IMEI/account data.

The data was provided to third parties who used it to:
- Unlock AT&T phones (enabling use on other carriers' networks);
- Facilitate identity fraud with customers' SSNs and account data.

The FCC found that approximately 280,000 customer accounts were affected.

---

II. VIOLATIONS FOUND

The FCC found that AT&T violated:

1. 47 U.S.C. § 222 — the general duty to protect CPNI;

2. 47 C.F.R. § 64.2010(a) — the duty to take reasonable measures to discover and protect against attempts to gain unauthorized access to CPNI;

3. 47 C.F.R. § 64.2010 (employee/vendor oversight) — the duty to ensure that employees and vendors do not misuse CPNI access;

4. Failure to adequately supervise overseas call center vendors who had access to customer CPNI.

---

III. SETTLEMENT TERMS

AT&T agreed to:

1. Pay $25 million in civil penalties to the United States;

2. Implement a compliance plan including:
   a. Enhanced authentication procedures (moving beyond biographical-info-only authentication);
   b. Improved oversight of overseas call center vendors;
   c. Employee training on CPNI obligations;
   d. Annual CPNI compliance certifications for three years;
   e. Appointment of a compliance officer responsible for CPNI;
   f. Regular audits of call center access to CPNI;

3. Submit compliance reports to the FCC for a period of three years.

AT&T neither admitted nor denied the findings, as is standard in consent decree settlements.

---

IV. LEGAL AND FACTUAL SIGNIFICANCE FOR AT&T SIM SWAP LIABILITY

A. ACTUAL NOTICE:
AT&T executed and signed the consent decree on April 8, 2015. From that date forward, AT&T had actual, documented notice that:
- Its call centers were being used as vectors for unauthorized CPNI access;
- Biographical information (SSN, address) was being used to authenticate customers and then stolen;
- Vendor oversight was insufficient to prevent CPNI fraud;
- Enhanced authentication was required and committed to as a specific settlement obligation.

If the SIM swap attack on Michael Hartmann occurred in or after 2018, AT&T had been operating under this specific compliance commitment for approximately three years. Any SIM swap executed without the enhanced authentication AT&T committed to in the consent decree is a breach of both the FCC settlement and the duty of care.

B. STANDARD OF CARE AFTER THE CONSENT DECREE:
AT&T's voluntary compliance commitments in the consent decree (enhanced authentication, vendor oversight, regular audits) define AT&T's own standard of care for CPNI protection. If AT&T failed to implement those commitments — or implemented them but they were insufficient to prevent the SIM swap — the question is whether AT&T actually carried out what it promised.

C. NEGLIGENCE PER SE THEORY:
The consent decree confirms AT&T's understanding of its legal obligations under § 222 and § 64.2010. An AT&T SIM swap failure post-consent-decree is not an unknowing violation — it is a violation by a company that paid $25 million to settle exactly this type of failure.

D. PUNITIVE DAMAGES RELEVANCE:
Under California Civil Code § 3294 (punitive damages in tort), punitive damages require proof that the defendant acted with "malice, oppression, or fraud," or with "conscious disregard" for others' rights. Evidence that AT&T paid $25M for authentication failures, promised enhanced authentication in the consent decree, and then permitted a similar SIM swap attack to occur — supports the argument that AT&T's subsequent failure represents conscious disregard.

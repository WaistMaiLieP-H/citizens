# Flores-Figueroa v. United States — §1028A Knowledge Requirement

**Citation:** 556 US 646 (2009)
**Court:** United States Supreme Court
**Decided:** May 4, 2009
**Author:** Breyer, J. (unanimous)

## Note on Citation

Flores-Figueroa is primarily cited in the §1028 standard for its construction of §1028(a)(7)'s
"knowingly" requirement. It is documented here because the same knowledge principle applies
to §1028A's "knowingly...uses...a means of identification of another person."

## Facts

An undocumented worker used a real Social Security number (belonging to a real person, unbeknownst
to him) to obtain employment. He was convicted of aggravated identity theft under §1028A and the
related §1028(a)(7) charge. The question was whether the "knowingly" mens rea extended to the
fact that the identification belonged to a real person.

## Holding

**"Knowingly" in §1028(a)(7) — and by extension §1028A — requires that the defendant know the
identification belongs to a real person, not just that they knowingly used identification.**

The government argued "knowingly" applied only to the transfer/possession/use itself, not to the
"of another person" element. The Court rejected this, holding that ordinary syntax requires
"knowingly" to modify the entire actus reus, including the fact that the identification belongs
to an actual person.

## Application to §1028A

The same principle applies to §1028A. A defendant who uses a means of identification believing
it is fictitious cannot be convicted under §1028A even if the identification coincidentally belongs
to a real person.

**Implication for Michael's cases:**
The perpetrators in Michael's cases **knew** they were using Michael's real identity:
- Treasury: They used his name, SSN, and address — information associated with a specific real person
- SIM swap: They targeted Michael's specific phone number to access his specific accounts
- Hillberg: "HILLBERGMANN" is a deliberate compound incorporating Michael's real identity components

Flores-Figueroa poses no barrier in these cases. The knowledge element is satisfied by the
targeting itself — the perpetrators chose Michael's identity because it was his.

**Contrast:** A defendant who uses a randomly generated SSN that happens to match a real person
could raise a Flores-Figueroa defense. Not applicable where the perpetrator deliberately targeted
a specific individual.

# Dubin v. United States — Holding

**Citation:** 599 US 110 (2023)
**Court:** United States Supreme Court
**Decided:** June 8, 2023
**Author:** Sotomayor, J. (8-1; Barrett, J., concurring in part; no dissent on the core holding)

## Facts

David Dubin submitted Medicare claims on behalf of a psychological testing company, overstating
the level of care provided to patients. He used the patients' Medicaid IDs to identify the claims
in the billing system. He was convicted of healthcare fraud and aggravated identity theft under
§1028A. The Fifth Circuit (en banc) upheld the §1028A conviction under a broad reading that
covered any use of a real person's identification during a fraud.

## Holding

A defendant "uses" a means of identification "in relation to" a predicate offense under §1028A(a)(1)
only when the **use of that identification is at the crux of the underlying offense** — meaning the
defendant used or transferred another person's identity in a way that was not merely incidental but
was the key, defining element of what made the conduct criminal.

Using a patient's Medicaid ID to reference their billing record while submitting fraudulent claims
about the level of services does NOT satisfy this standard. The fraud (the overbilling) was not
about the patient's identity — the patient ID was an administrative reference. The aggravation that
§1028A targets is identity impersonation, account takeover, and similar conduct where the perpetrator
is "stealing" an identity in the meaningful sense.

## Rule distilled

**§1028A "at the crux" test:**
The use of the other person's means of identification must be what makes the crime "aggravated" —
the identity is not incidental or administrative, but is the object or instrument of the fraud itself.

**Core applications:**
- Impersonating another person (identity = the means of deception) → §1028A applies
- Taking over another person's account using their credentials → §1028A applies
- Opening accounts in another person's name → §1028A applies
- Using a real person's ID to access a billing system while committing unrelated fraud → §1028A does NOT apply

## Significance for Michael's portfolio

**Dubin narrows §1028A for billing/administrative fraud but confirms it for Michael's cases.**

In Michael's Treasury Securities case, SIM swap/crypto case, and Hillberg compound-identity case,
the identity use IS the crux:
- The fraudsters were impersonating Michael as an accountholder or claiming his identity as a
  financial instrument, not using his ID administratively.
- The at-the-crux test is satisfied: the fraudulent scheme's success required "taking" Michael's
  identity, not merely referencing it.

**Key language:** "The 'aggravated' element is not that the fraud happened to involve someone's
information — it is that the perpetrator stole an identity in the sense that gives the statute its name."

# §1028A Historical Chain — 18 USC § 1028A Aggravated Identity Theft

## The wound (pre-2004)

By the late 1990s and early 2000s, §1028 identity theft convictions routinely resulted in
sentences that ran concurrently with the predicate fraud offense. A defendant convicted of wire
fraud (potential 20-year maximum) and identity theft would serve essentially the wire fraud
sentence alone. The identity theft charge had no independent deterrent effect.

Congress heard testimony from victims and prosecutors: the "add-on" of an identity theft conviction
was meaningless in sentencing because it disappeared into the concurrent calculation. Identity theft
as a low-cost fraud instrument had exploded in the late 1990s — FTC estimates suggested millions
of Americans were victimized annually, and the fraud losses were growing geometrically with
internet commerce.

---

## 2004: Identity Theft Penalty Enhancement Act — mandatory consecutive

Congress's solution was categorical: remove judicial discretion. §1028A imposed a mandatory 2-year
consecutive sentence for any predicate offense on the enumerated list when the defendant used a
real person's identification "during and in relation to" that offense.

The statute had two key design features:
1. **Mandatory** — no probation, no suspended sentence, no going below 2 years
2. **Consecutive** — must follow the predicate sentence; cannot be merged or concurrent

The anti-stacking provision (§1028A(b)(4)) preserved limited judicial discretion only for multiple
§1028A counts from the same scheme — those could run concurrently with each other, though
consecutively to the predicate.

---

## 2004-2022: Circuit split on scope

The text's ambiguity created nearly two decades of divergent interpretation. Courts split on whether
"uses a means of identification of another person" during a predicate offense covers:
- Only impersonation/account-takeover (narrow): 1st, 2nd, certain district courts
- Any use of a real ID including administrative/billing (broad): 4th, 5th, 6th, 11th circuits

The government consistently advocated the broad interpretation to maximize leverage in complex
fraud prosecutions.

---

## 2023: Dubin narrows — "at the crux"

The Supreme Court's unanimous holding in Dubin v. United States (2023) resolved the circuit split
in favor of the narrower reading. The "at the crux" test requires that the use of the other person's
identification be the central element of the crime's aggravated character — not merely an
administrative tool incidental to a different fraud.

The logical arc: Congress created mandatory-consecutive identity theft liability → prosecutors
expanded it to cover all complex fraud → courts tolerated the expansion → Supreme Court corrects
it by anchoring the statute to its namesake crime (identity theft, not fraud-that-happens-to-use-IDs).

---

## Current framework (2026)

§1028A is a surgical tool for cases where the identity itself was stolen in the meaningful sense —
impersonation, account takeover, fraudulent account opening, SIM swap-enabled access. The Dubin
narrowing preserves the statute's core deterrent function while preventing it from becoming an
automatic doubling of all fraud sentences.

**The arc:** Concurrent-sentence gap (pre-2004) → mandatory consecutive enhancement (2004) →
prosecutorial overextension (2004-2022) → judicial narrowing to true identity-theft cases
(Dubin 2023) → current stable framework.

**For Michael's cases:** The arc leads squarely to §1028A applicability — these are Dubin-compliant
identity-theft cases where the identity taking was the crux of the fraud, not incidental to it.

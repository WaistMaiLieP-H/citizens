# §1028A Origin — 2004 Identity Theft Penalty Enhancement Act

## The wound this statute answered

By 2004, §1028 (the 1982/1998 identity theft statute) had a documented enforcement failure: sentences
were too short and too often ran **concurrently** with the predicate fraud offense. A defendant convicted
of wire fraud AND identity theft would serve essentially the wire fraud sentence alone — the identity
theft add-on dissolved into the concurrent sentence.

The consequence was predictable: identity theft became a **low-cost input** to fraud schemes. Fraudsters
had no marginal incentive to avoid using real people's identities, because the punishment for doing so
produced no additional prison time beyond what the fraud itself carried.

The wound in 2004 was the opposite of the usual "too few laws" problem — it was a **gap in deterrence
architecture**. The law existed; the sentences didn't sting.

## The logical structure Congress chose: mandatory consecutive

The ITPEA added §1028A as a forced consecutive sentencing enhancement. The logical structure:

**Input:** Conviction for any enumerated predicate offense (§1028A(c) list)
**Trigger:** During and in relation to that offense, the defendant knowingly used a real person's
means of identification without lawful authority
**Output:** A mandatory consecutive 2-year sentence that cannot be reduced by the sentencing court
and cannot run concurrently with the predicate sentence

This structure was deliberately categorical. Congress did not give courts discretion to impose less
than 2 years or to make it concurrent. The message was architectural: identity theft adds time, always.

## The original scope problem (pre-Dubin)

The 2004 text read: "uses, without lawful authority, a means of identification of another person."

Courts immediately split on what "uses" meant:
- **Narrow reading:** Only impersonation — where the defendant is pretending to BE the victim
- **Middle reading:** Only where the identity is the instrument of the crime
- **Broad reading (government's position):** Any fraud that happens to involve another person's
  identifying information, including routine billing fraud using patient IDs

The government consistently pushed the broad reading because it allowed §1028A to attach to
essentially any healthcare fraud, tax fraud, or benefits fraud — a massive expansion of mandatory
minimum exposure.

## What the 2004 statute did NOT resolve

The text was ambiguous enough to generate a circuit split that persisted for nearly 20 years,
until Dubin v. United States (2023) finally resolved it in the defendant's favor with the
"at the crux" test.

The 2004 Congress intended the narrow/middle position (impersonation and account-takeover fraud)
but wrote the text broadly enough that prosecutors could argue for attaching §1028A to any
complex fraud scheme. Dubin corrected the overreach.

# §1028A Judicial Refinement — Dubin v. United States (2023)

## The wound this decision answered

By 2022, prosecutors had extended §1028A to attach to virtually any complex fraud that touched a
real person's identifying information — even routine healthcare billing fraud where the "identity
theft" was simply referencing a patient's Medicaid ID to submit inflated claims. Defendants were
facing mandatory consecutive sentences for conduct that the 2004 Congress almost certainly did not
intend to reach.

The result was sentencing disproportion: a healthcare administrator who overbilled Medicaid faced
the same mandatory consecutive 2 years as someone who stole a stranger's identity to open 50 credit
cards. The circuit split on what "uses" meant had created a tool that prosecutors could deploy in
almost any case involving another person's information.

## The logical shift: from "any use" to "use at the crux"

**Dubin v. United States, 599 US 110 (2023)** — The Supreme Court resolved the circuit split by
holding that §1028A requires that the use of the other person's means of identification be
**"at the crux of what makes the conduct criminal."**

The Court's reasoning:
1. §1028A's title is "Aggravated **Identity Theft**" — the statute is about identity theft, not
   about any fraud that happens to involve an identifier.
2. The "in relation to" language requires a meaningful relationship between the identity use and
   the predicate offense — not mere incidentality.
3. If any use of a real person's ID during any fraud triggered §1028A, the statute would attach
   to essentially all of Medicare/Medicaid fraud, IRS fraud, and benefits fraud — an absurd result
   given the mandatory minimum consequence.

**What "at the crux" means operationally:**

| Identity use IS at the crux | Identity use is NOT at the crux |
|---|---|
| Opening accounts in another person's name | Overbilling using a patient's ID to access billing system |
| Impersonating another person to commit fraud | Referencing a beneficiary's ID to submit inflated claims |
| SIM swap to take over another person's identity and accounts | Using employee IDs to facilitate embezzlement |
| Using a stolen SSN to obtain credit, benefits, or employment | Misrepresenting hours worked to an employer under your own identity |
| Account takeover where the identity IS the access mechanism | Forging a signature while using your own identity |

## Diff from prior stage

**Before Dubin:** Courts of appeals split, with many circuits allowing §1028A to attach whenever
a real person's ID was involved in the fraudulent transaction in any way.

**After Dubin:** §1028A attaches only when the identity use is what makes the crime aggravated —
impersonation, account-takeover, and identity-as-instrument. Billing fraud, benefits fraud, and
routine financial fraud using administrative patient/beneficiary IDs does NOT trigger §1028A unless
the identity use itself is the crux.

## Impact on Michael's referral strategy

Dubin HELPS Michael's position. In Michael's cases, the identity use IS the crux:
- Treasury: someone claiming to be Michael (the account owner)
- SIM swap/crypto: taking over Michael's phone identity to access accounts
- Hillberg compound identity: opening new accounts in a synthetic identity containing Michael's components

These are all textbook "at the crux" cases — the identity was not incidental to the fraud. The
fraud **was** the identity theft. Dubin does not narrow the §1028A exposure in these cases.

Where Dubin would help a defendant: if the referral target argues that using Michael's account
number to access records was merely administrative. Anticipate this defense; the counter is that
the **opening** of the account / takeover of the account was the crux, not any single subsequent
transaction.

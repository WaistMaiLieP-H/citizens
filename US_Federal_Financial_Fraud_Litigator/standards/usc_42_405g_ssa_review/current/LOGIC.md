# Current Rule — 42 U.S.C. §405(g) (Social Security Act — Federal Court Review)

## THE WOUND

The Social Security disability program (SSDI) is an administrative claims system.
When SSA denies a disability claim, the claimant must exhaust administrative remedies
(reconsideration → ALJ hearing → Appeals Council review) before seeking federal court
review. The wound that §405(g) addresses: without explicit statutory authorization,
federal courts would have no jurisdiction to review SSA administrative decisions.

For Michael Hartmann's case, the deeper wound is different: the SSA/DDS phantom
contact fraud. SSA records show three phantom contact dates — 9/18/2019, 3/23/2020,
and 12/17/2020 — that record supposedly successful communications with Michael, which
never occurred. This fraud appears to have been used to: (1) deny the disability
claim on "non-response" grounds; or (2) create false administrative record justifying
the denial. If Michael's communications with SSA were intercepted via SIM swap, calls
he placed or received from SSA may have been routed to the attacker's device, making
it appear Michael was unresponsive.

## THE §405(g) FRAMEWORK

### Administrative Exhaustion Requirement

Before seeking federal court review, a claimant must complete the SSA administrative
process:

1. **Initial determination** — SSA/DDS evaluates the claim
2. **Reconsideration** — if denied, claimant may request reconsideration within 60 days
3. **ALJ hearing** — if denied again, claimant may request hearing before an
   Administrative Law Judge within 60 days
4. **Appeals Council review** — if denied by ALJ, claimant may request Appeals Council
   review within 60 days
5. **Federal court review (§405(g))** — after Appeals Council denial (or failure to act),
   claimant has 60 days to file in federal district court

### 42 U.S.C. §405(g) — The Civil Action

§405(g) provides:

"Any individual, after any final decision of the Commissioner of Social Security made
after a hearing to which he was a party, irrespective of the amount in controversy,
may obtain a review of such decision by a civil action commenced within sixty days
after the mailing to him of notice of such decision or within such further time as
the Commissioner of Social Security may allow."

**Court's scope of review (§405(g), sentence four):**
The court may:
- Affirm the decision
- Modify the decision
- Reverse the decision
- Remand the case for further administrative proceedings

**Standard of review:** "Substantial evidence" — whether the administrative record
contains sufficient evidence to support the Commissioner's decision. The court does
not conduct a de novo review; it reviews the administrative record.

### The 60-Day Limitation and Equitable Tolling

The 60-day filing deadline for federal court review runs from the date of the Appeals
Council denial. This is a jurisdictional limitation that courts have been willing to
equitably toll in limited circumstances — where the claimant was prevented from filing
by circumstances beyond their control.

**Equitable tolling applies where:**
- The agency itself was responsible for the delay or failure (Bowen v. City of New York)
- The claimant had no knowledge of the right to appeal
- The claimant was mentally or physically incapacitated
- Fraud by the agency deprived the claimant of the opportunity to appeal

**Phantom contact fraud and equitable tolling:**
If SSA's false contact records (9/18/2019, 3/23/2020, 12/17/2020) were used to
construct a false denial record that prevented Michael from knowing the true basis
for the denial, equitable tolling applies to the 60-day federal court deadline.

### Benefits Recovery — Scope of §405(g) Remedy

If the federal court finds the denial was not supported by substantial evidence (or
remands for new ALJ proceedings that result in an award):

**Past-due benefits:** SSDI pays from the established onset date of disability, minus
a 5-month waiting period. Benefits awarded through federal court review cover the
full period from the onset date to the present.

**Monthly benefit amount × months denied = past-due benefits recovery.**

For SSDI: the primary insurance amount (PIA) is based on the claimant's average
indexed monthly earnings (AIME). For a skilled tradesperson (UA Local 342 plumber)
with significant prior earnings, the monthly SSDI benefit can be substantial.

**Attorney fees:** Under 42 U.S.C. §406(b), attorney fees in SSDI cases are capped
at 25% of past-due benefits, up to $7,200 (the SSA-set maximum). The attorney fee
comes out of past-due benefits; no separate fee from current benefits.

### §405(g) Procedural Posture

For the phantom contact fraud to be addressed in federal court, Michael must:
1. Determine whether the disability claim was formally denied (and when)
2. Determine whether the administrative exhaustion process was completed (or whether
   the phantom contacts prevented proper exhaustion)
3. If administrative process was not completed, determine whether equitable tolling
   applies to allow initiation of the process now
4. If the deadline for administrative steps has passed: argue waiver/equitable tolling
   based on SSA's own fraud (Bowen v. City of New York)

**New claim vs. reactivation:** If the prior claim was abandoned (not formally denied),
Michael can file a new disability claim with an onset date going back to the original
disability onset (subject to res judicata and prior application limitations — see
Soc. Sec. Ruling SSR 91-5p for reopening prior applications).

### The Federal Lawsuit Track — Non-§405(g)

If the phantom contact fraud is characterized as:
- Privacy Act violation (5 U.S.C. §552a): separate civil action in federal court
- CFAA violation (18 U.S.C. §1030): SSA portal unauthorized access
- Constitutional deprivation (42 U.S.C. §1983): state actor involvement

These claims do not require §405(g) exhaustion — they are independent civil actions.
§405(g) is specifically for challenging the substantive denial of benefits on the
administrative record.

# Historical Chain — 42 U.S.C. §405(g) (Social Security Act §205(g))

## The Three-Stage Evolution

### Stage 01 — 1935 Origin: Social Security Act Enacted

The Social Security Act of 1935 (Pub. L. 74-271) created the federal old-age insurance
program. Section 205(g) was included in the original Act to provide a mechanism for
judicial review of benefit determinations. The original framework:

- Federal district court review of "final decisions" of the Social Security Board
- Substantial evidence standard: court examines whether the agency finding is supported
  by substantial evidence on the record as a whole
- No explicit statute of limitations in the original enactment
- No administrative exhaustion requirement in the original framework

**The limitation:** The original Act provided no disability insurance program. Section 205(g)
review covered only retirement and survivors benefits. The administrative record review
framework was established but not yet tested against the complexity of medical disability
determinations.

---

### Stage 02 — 1956: Social Security Disability Insurance Added

The Social Security Amendments of 1956 (Pub. L. 84-880) created the Social Security
Disability Insurance (SSDI) program. This fundamentally changed the nature of §205(g)
review:

- Disability determinations introduced medical complexity far beyond retirement age calculations
- State Disability Determination Services (DDS) created as the first-level evaluators
- Multi-step administrative process: DDS → Reconsideration → ALJ → Appeals Council → Federal Court
- The 60-day filing deadline from "mailing of notice" became a jurisdictional prerequisite

**The wound:** The disability evaluation process was complex and the administrative record
long. Claimants frequently did not understand why they were denied or what evidence would
have changed the outcome. The SSA's internal policies — not always shared with claimants
— drove outcomes that claimants had no way to challenge because they didn't know what to
challenge.

---

### Stage 03 — 1983–1986: Benefits Reform Crisis and Bowen Equitable Tolling

The Social Security Amendments of 1983 (Pub. L. 98-21) and the Social Security Disability
Benefits Reform Act of 1984 (Pub. L. 98-460) responded to a massive crisis: the Reagan
administration had directed the SSA to review and terminate hundreds of thousands of
disability benefits. The Review had generated massive litigation.

**Bowen v. City of New York, 476 U.S. 467 (1986)** arose directly from this crisis:

- The SSA had adopted a secret internal policy of applying different evaluation criteria than
  those published in its regulations — claimants were denied based on criteria they could
  not know about, could not challenge, and were never told about
- Claimants missed the 60-day deadline because they did not know the real basis for denial
- The Supreme Court held: equitable tolling applies where the SSA conceals material
  information from claimants; the government cannot benefit from its own wrongdoing;
  class action relief is available for systematic violations

**The design answer:** §205(g) review, as interpreted by Bowen, is not a technical filing
deadline that can be weaponized by an agency that conceals the basis for its own decisions.
Equitable tolling runs from the date of discovery of the concealment, not from the date of
the agency's fraudulent notice.

---

## What the History Tells Us

The 1935 Act established substantial evidence review. The 1956 SSDI expansion created the
medical complexity that makes the administrative record both essential and manipulable.
The 1983-1986 reform crisis exposed exactly how the SSA could abuse its internal policies
to defeat judicial review — and Bowen plugged that hole.

**Phantom contact records are a direct descendant of the Bowen concealment pattern:**
The SSA created records of contacts (9/18/2019, 3/23/2020, 12/17/2020) that did not
occur, and those false records substituted for the genuine administrative process that
would have generated a real notice, real reasons, and a real 60-day clock. The Bowen
equitable tolling doctrine was built precisely for this.

## The Mathews v. Eldridge Framework

Before Bowen, Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319 (1976), established that the process
due to a disability claimant is measured by three factors:
1. The private interest affected
2. The risk of erroneous deprivation and value of additional safeguards
3. The government's interest, including fiscal and administrative burden

**Mathews + Bowen together:** If the SSA substitutes phantom contacts for genuine process,
the "process provided" is fraudulent. The Mathews balancing applies to a genuine hearing;
it cannot justify a fraudulent one. Phantom contacts = zero process provided, regardless
of what the file shows.

# Califano v. Sanders — Holding

**Citation:** 430 U.S. 99 (1977)
**Court:** Supreme Court of the United States
**Decided:** February 22, 1977
**Author:** Stewart, J. (for the Court)

---

## Facts

A Social Security benefits claimant sought judicial review in district court after the
SSA denied his claim for reopening a previously adjudicated benefits determination.
The SSA contended that §405(g) did not confer jurisdiction to review discretionary
denial-of-reopening decisions because those decisions were not part of a "final decision"
of the Commissioner subject to judicial review. The claimant argued that either §405(g)
or the Administrative Procedure Act's general review provision conferred jurisdiction for
constitutional claims even absent exhaustion.

---

## Holding

**Section 405(g) is the exclusive jurisdictional vehicle for SSA benefits claims.
The APA's general judicial review provision (5 U.S.C. §702) does not independently
provide jurisdiction over Social Security Act claims — §405(h) eliminates that route.
However, where a claimant raises a colorable constitutional claim, the district court
has jurisdiction to hear it even without satisfying the exhaustion requirements of §405(g).**

The Court held that the APA does not supply an independent basis for judicial review of
SSA decisions — §405(h) explicitly forecloses it. But the Court also recognized that
constitutional challenges cannot be entirely foreclosed by administrative exhaustion
requirements: where a claimant raises a "colorable constitutional claim," the district
court has federal question jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. §1331 that cannot be stripped
away by the SSA's administrative structure.

---

## Rules Distilled

1. **§405(h) exclusivity:** The Social Security Act's §405(h) makes §405(g) the exclusive
   route for judicial review of any claim "arising under" the Social Security Act. The APA's
   general review provision does not apply. There is no separate APA route around §405(g).

2. **Constitutional claims exception:** A "colorable constitutional claim" — one that is not
   wholly insubstantial or frivolous — may be heard in district court even without satisfying
   the §405(g) administrative exhaustion requirement. The basis is federal question
   jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. §1331.

3. **"Colorable" standard:** The constitutional claim must not be "wholly insubstantial,
   immaterial, or frivolous." A plausible argument that the SSA's action violated due process,
   equal protection, or other constitutional rights satisfies this threshold.

4. **Discretionary reopening not §405(g) "final decision":** The SSA's discretionary refusal
   to reopen a previously adjudicated determination is not a "final decision" subject to
   §405(g) review. But if the refusal rests on constitutional grounds, §1331 jurisdiction
   attaches.

5. **Interplay with Bowen equitable tolling:** Califano's constitutional claims exception
   and Bowen's equitable tolling of the §405(g) 60-day deadline work together: (a) Califano
   allows district court jurisdiction for constitutional claims even without exhaustion;
   (b) Bowen tolls the 60-day filing deadline where SSA's own conduct — including
   concealment — prevented timely filing.

## Significance for SSA Fraud / Phantom Contact Claims

For the steward's case — where SSA/DDS made "phantom contact" through an intercepted or
spoofed communication channel, causing a missed deadline, while the claimant was unaware
of the actual communication — Califano and Bowen operate as a two-part shield:

**First shield (Califano):** If the steward can assert a colorable constitutional claim —
that SSA's phantom-contact scheme violated procedural due process (failure to provide
meaningful notice and an opportunity to be heard before denial) — then §405(g)'s
exhaustion requirement does not bar district court jurisdiction. The due process claim
is cognizable under 28 U.S.C. §1331 regardless of whether the claimant exhausted
administrative remedies.

**Second shield (Bowen):** Even if §405(g) exhaustion is required, Bowen's equitable
tolling doctrine tolls the 60-day filing deadline where SSA's own action — including
a pattern of concealment — prevented the claimant from knowing about the adverse decision.
The phantom contact is precisely the kind of governmental conduct Bowen contemplated as
justifying tolling.

**Third thread (§1983 + §1985):** Where state actors (Christina Cerretani operating as a
state-court-created instrumentality + Antioch PD + DDS contact persons) conspired to
intercept communications between the SSA and the claimant, the SSA fraud claim has a
parallel §1983 / §1985(3) civil rights dimension that does not require §405(g) exhaustion
at all. See US_Federal_Civil_Rights_Litigator corpus.

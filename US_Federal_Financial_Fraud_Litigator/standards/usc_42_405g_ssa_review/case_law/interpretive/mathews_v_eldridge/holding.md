# Mathews v. Eldridge — Holding

**Citation:** Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319 (1976)
**Court:** Supreme Court of the United States
**Decided:** February 24, 1976
**Author:** Powell, J.

## Facts

George Eldridge was a SSDI recipient whose benefits were terminated following
an administrative review, without a pre-termination evidentiary hearing. Eldridge
argued that due process required a hearing before his benefits could be terminated.
He had already been through the administrative review process but claimed he was
entitled to additional procedural protections under the Fifth Amendment's Due Process
Clause before termination took effect.

## Holding

**The government need not provide an evidentiary hearing prior to terminating
Social Security disability benefits — post-termination administrative review and
judicial review under §405(g) are constitutionally sufficient due process.**

The Court established a three-factor balancing test for determining what process is
"due" in administrative benefit termination cases:

1. **Private interest** affected by the official action
2. **Risk of erroneous deprivation** through existing procedures and probable value
   of additional procedures
3. **Government's interest**, including fiscal and administrative burdens of additional procedure

Applying this test: disability benefits (unlike AFDC welfare in Goldberg v. Kelly) are
based on previous earnings and medical determinations, not current need; risk of
erroneous deprivation is reduced because decisions are based on objective medical
criteria (not subjective credibility assessments); and the administrative burden of
pre-termination hearings for all disability cases would be substantial.

## Rules Distilled

1. **Mathews balancing test:** Three factors determine what process is constitutionally
   due: (1) private interest at stake, (2) risk of erroneous deprivation + value of added
   process, (3) government's interest
2. **Post-termination review sufficient for SSDI:** Pre-termination hearing not required
   for SSDI — administrative exhaustion (including ALJ hearing) + §405(g) federal court
   review = sufficient due process
3. **Medical-evidence-based decisions:** SSDI decisions based on objective medical criteria
   have lower risk of erroneous deprivation than credibility-based welfare determinations
4. **But process must be real:** If the process provided was fraudulently undermined (phantom
   contacts replacing real process), the constitutional sufficiency of the process is vitiated

## Significance for Phantom Contact Fraud

Mathews is relevant in two ways:

**1. Normal process is constitutionally sufficient — but only if real:**
The Supreme Court's holding that post-termination administrative review + §405(g) is
constitutionally sufficient assumes the process was actually provided. If SSA's phantom
contact records substituted for real contact with Michael, the "process" provided was
a fraud — and Mathews does not save a fraudulently conducted administrative proceeding.

**2. The Mathews balancing test applies to equitable tolling arguments:**
When Michael argues that the phantom contacts deprived him of meaningful access to
the administrative process (and thus equitable tolling of the 60-day deadline should
apply), the Mathews framework reinforces the argument: the private interest (years of
wrongly denied disability benefits) is substantial; the risk of erroneous deprivation
is 100% where the "process" was fraudulent; and the government's interest in finality
does not protect fraudulent administrative records.

MATHEWS v. ELDRIDGE
424 U.S. 319 (1976)
Supreme Court of the United States
Argued October 6, 1975 — Decided February 24, 1976
Majority: POWELL, J., joined by BURGER, C.J., STEWART, WHITE, BLACKMUN, and REHNQUIST, JJ.
Dissent: BRENNAN, J., joined by MARSHALL, J.
STEVENS, J., took no part.

---

SYLLABUS (official)

Respondent's Social Security disability benefits were terminated without a pre-termination evidentiary hearing. He brought an action challenging the constitutional adequacy of the procedures. The issue is whether the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment requires that prior to the termination of Social Security disability benefit payments the recipient be afforded an opportunity for an evidentiary hearing.

Held: The procedures provided by the Social Security Act and the Secretary's regulations satisfy the requirements of due process.

---

OPINION OF THE COURT

Justice POWELL delivered the opinion of the Court.

The issue in this case is whether the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment requires that prior to the termination of Social Security disability benefit payments the recipient be afforded an opportunity for an evidentiary hearing.

I

Cash benefits are provided to workers during periods in which they are completely disabled under the disability insurance benefit program created by the 1956 amendments to the Social Security Act. Respondent George Eldridge was awarded benefits in June 1968 after establishing that he suffered from chronic anxiety and back strain. In March 1972, he received a questionnaire from the state agency which administers the federal program. Eldridge completed the questionnaire, indicating that his condition had not improved and identifying the medical sources, including physicians, who had treated him within the previous year. The agency then obtained reports from his physician and a psychiatrist.

After examining the medical reports, the agency informed Eldridge by letter that it had made a tentative determination that his disability had ceased. The letter included a statement of reasons for the proposed termination of benefits. Eldridge was afforded an opportunity to respond in writing and to seek reconsideration, which he did. The agency affirmed its original assessment. Eldridge then commenced this action challenging the constitutionality of the administrative procedures used to terminate his benefits.

II

Procedural due process imposes constraints on governmental decisions which deprive individuals of "liberty" or "property" interests within the meaning of the Due Process Clause of the Fifth or Fourteenth Amendment. The requirements of procedural due process apply only to the deprivation of interests encompassed within the protection of the Due Process Clause. And "the range of interests protected by procedural due process is not infinite."

The fundamental requirement of due process is the opportunity to be heard "at a meaningful time and in a meaningful manner." Eldridge agrees that the review procedures available to a claimant before the initial determination of ineligibility becomes final satisfy due process. Only the question whether the pretermination procedures are constitutionally sufficient remains.

III

This Court consistently has held that some form of hearing is required before an individual is finally deprived of a property interest. The "right to be heard before being condemned to suffer grievous loss of any kind, even though it may not involve the stigma and hardships of a criminal conviction, is a principle basic to our society." Joint Anti-Fascist Comm. v. McGrath, 341 U.S. 123, 168 (1951) (Frankfurter, J., concurring).

The elusive nature of the due process concept arises not from the lack of clarity about what "process" is "due" but rather from the difficulty of deciding what "process" is "due" in a particular situation. This Court has consistently rejected rigid rules for resolution of the procedural due process question. Rather, the Court has identified two factors relevant to the "appropriate accommodation" in balancing competing interests. These are the private interest that will be affected by the governmental action, and the risk of erroneous deprivation of such interest through the procedures used, along with the probable value, if any, of additional procedural safeguards.

THE MATHEWS THREE-FACTOR TEST:

"[I]dentification of the specific dictates of due process generally requires consideration of three distinct factors: First, the private interest that will be affected by the official action; second, the risk of an erroneous deprivation of such interest through the procedures used, and the probable value, if any, of additional or substitute procedural safeguards; and finally, the Government's interest, including the function involved and the fiscal and administrative burdens that the additional or substitute procedural requirement would entail." 424 U.S. at 335.

IV

Applying the three-factor test to the termination of disability benefits:

First, the private interest at stake is not as weighty as in Goldberg v. Kelly, which involved welfare benefits. Disability payments are not based on financial need; they are insurance benefits based on past earnings. The disabled worker is presumed to have resources and savings, or access to private insurance, that the welfare recipient typically lacks.

Second, the risk of erroneous deprivation, while significant, is mitigated by the nature of the disability inquiry. Whether a claimant is disabled is a medical question that can be addressed through written submissions and medical reports. The written submissions in Eldridge's case were substantial and the inquiry is largely documentary. The pre-termination process allowed the claimant to respond in writing, to submit medical information, and to seek reconsideration.

Third, the Government's interest is substantial. The cost of providing a pre-termination evidentiary hearing to the large class of disability claimants would be significant — and many of those who prevailed at a pre-termination hearing would eventually be found ineligible anyway, resulting in the Government's having to recover overpayments.

Balancing these three factors, the pre-termination procedures provided by the Act and regulations — written notification, opportunity to respond in writing and to submit additional evidence, and post-termination hearing and judicial review — satisfy the requirements of procedural due process.

The judgment of the Court of Appeals is REVERSED.

---

BRENNAN, J., dissenting.

I agree with the Court that procedural due process is required before the Government deprives an individual of disability benefits. I disagree with the Court's conclusion that the pre-termination procedures currently provided are adequate.

The majority's three-factor balancing test is the correct framework. But the balance comes out differently when the full weight of the private interest is appreciated. Disability benefits are the only means of subsistence for many recipients; they do not enjoy the presumed asset base the majority attributes to them. The majority's assumption that disability claimants have private insurance or savings is empirically questionable.

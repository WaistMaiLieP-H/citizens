BOWEN v. CITY OF NEW YORK
476 U.S. 467 (1986)
Supreme Court of the United States
Argued February 24, 1986 — Decided May 27, 1986
Majority: POWELL, J., joined by BRENNAN, WHITE, MARSHALL, BLACKMUN, and STEVENS, JJ.
Dissent: O'CONNOR, J., joined by BURGER, C.J., and REHNQUIST, J.

---

SYLLABUS (official)

The Secretary of Health and Human Services followed a secret policy, inconsistent with governing regulations, in evaluating disability claims. Claimants who were denied benefits under this secret policy brought suit in district court. The Government contended that the district court lacked jurisdiction because the claimants had not exhausted their administrative remedies under 42 U.S.C. § 405(g) and had not filed within the 60-day limitation period of § 405(g).

Held: (1) The district court had jurisdiction because the claimants raised a colorable constitutional claim of systematic procedural error, bringing the case within the constitutional exception recognized in Califano v. Sanders. (2) The 60-day limitations period of § 405(g) should be equitably tolled where the Secretary's own secret policy caused the claimants to miss the deadline.

---

OPINION OF THE COURT

Justice POWELL delivered the opinion of the Court.

This case requires us to decide whether courts have jurisdiction to review a class of claims that the Secretary of Health and Human Services used a secret policy — inconsistent with the Secretary's own regulations — to evaluate disability claims and to deny benefits.

I

The Social Security Act creates a disability insurance program that provides benefits to workers who are unable to engage in substantial gainful activity by reason of any medically determinable physical or mental impairment. The Act establishes procedures for applying for and obtaining benefits. Under those procedures, a claimant who is denied benefits may request reconsideration by the state agency, request a hearing before an administrative law judge, request review by the Appeals Council, and seek judicial review in district court pursuant to § 405(g).

Section 405(g) provides that judicial review is available of any "final decision of the Secretary made after a hearing." The claimant must file suit within 60 days after the mailing of the Secretary's final decision. Section 405(h) provides that "[n]o action against the United States, the Secretary, or any officer or employee thereof shall be brought under sections 1331 or 1346 of title 28 to recover on any claim arising under this subchapter."

II

The district court found, and the Court of Appeals affirmed, that the Secretary had followed a secret policy of systematically disregarding claimants' subjective complaints of pain and other symptoms. This secret policy was contrary to the Secretary's own published regulations, which required that subjective complaints be considered and evaluated.

The Government argues that the district court lacked jurisdiction because the claimants failed to exhaust their administrative remedies and failed to file suit within the 60-day limitations period.

III

A

On the question of exhaustion, we held in Califano v. Sanders, 430 U.S. 99 (1977), that where a claimant raises a "colorable constitutional claim," § 405(g)'s exhaustion requirement may be waived. The claimants here raise a colorable constitutional claim: the Secretary's systematic application of a secret policy contrary to her own regulations deprived them of the procedural due process to which they were entitled.

The due process claim here is not frivolous. The claimants did not merely receive inadequate consideration of their claims in the ordinary case; they were subjected to a systematic policy that was contrary to the Secretary's own regulations and that was not disclosed to them. This is precisely the kind of procedural irregularity that gives rise to a constitutional claim.

B

On the question of the 60-day limitations period, we hold that it is subject to equitable tolling.

The 60-day filing limitation in § 405(g) is not jurisdictional in the strict sense; it is a condition on the waiver of sovereign immunity. As such, it is subject to equitable principles. Equitable tolling is appropriate where the Government's own conduct prevents a claimant from filing within the limitations period.

Here, the Secretary's secret policy was itself the reason claimants could not file within the limitations period. The claimants did not know — and could not have known — that the Secretary was applying a policy contrary to her own regulations. The secret nature of the policy prevented them from recognizing that they had suffered a constitutional injury and from filing suit within the 60-day period.

"We have repeatedly held that in those cases where the Government's secretive conduct prevents plaintiffs from knowing of a violation, the limitations period does not begin to run until plaintiffs know or reasonably should know that the cause of action exists." 476 U.S. at 480.

IV

APPLICATION TO GOVERNMENT CONCEALMENT CASES:

The Bowen holding establishes that when the Government conceals a policy or practice that adversely affects a claimant's rights:

1. The constitutional claims exception of Califano v. Sanders waives exhaustion;
2. The 60-day filing deadline of § 405(g) is subject to equitable tolling;
3. The tolling period runs from when the claimant "knew or reasonably should have known" of the cause of action.

This doctrine is directly applicable where SSA communications were intercepted or blocked by third parties acting in concert, preventing the claimant from receiving adverse decisions and thus from triggering the 60-day deadline. If the claimant never received the Secretary's final decision because communications were intercepted, the 60-day period never began to run.

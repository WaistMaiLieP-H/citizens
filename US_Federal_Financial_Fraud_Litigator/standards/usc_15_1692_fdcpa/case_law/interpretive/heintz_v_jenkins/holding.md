# Heintz v. Jenkins
## 514 US 291 (1995)

**Court:** United States Supreme Court
**Decided:** April 18, 1995
**Vote:** 9-0

---

## Issue decided

Do attorneys who regularly engage in consumer debt collection litigation fall within FDCPA's
definition of "debt collector"?

---

## Holding

**Yes.** Attorneys who regularly engage in consumer debt collection litigation are "debt
collectors" subject to the FDCPA. The ordinary meaning of §1692a(6)'s definition covers any
person who "regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or
due or asserted to be owed or due another." Attorneys doing collection work for their clients
fall squarely within this definition.

**The litigation exemption argument rejected:** The debt collection attorneys argued that
litigation activity is exempt from FDCPA because filing a lawsuit is not "collection" — it is
the exercise of legal rights through the courts. The Court rejected this. Congress's 1977 FDCPA
was amended in 1986 to REMOVE a prior exemption for attorneys who regularly collected debts
via legal proceedings. The 1986 deletion is evidence that Congress intended attorneys to be
covered.

---

## Why this case matters for Michael

**Collection attorneys are full FDCPA defendants.** Any attorney who sent Michael a demand
letter, filed a collection lawsuit, or engaged in collection activities on behalf of a creditor
or debt buyer is a "debt collector" under FDCPA, with full liability for all §1692d/e/f/g
violations.

**Practical scope:** In California debt collection cases, most collection activity at the
pre-suit stage is conducted by collection agencies and collection law firms. Heintz means both
are covered. A law firm that sends a collection letter with a false statement of the debt's
character (§1692e(2)(A)) or that omits the mini-Miranda disclosure (§1692e(11)) is liable
under FDCPA regardless of its attorney status.

**Debt-collection lawsuit filings:** Filing a lawsuit to collect a debt is itself subject to
FDCPA constraints. A complaint that falsely represents the amount of the debt or its legal
status (§1692e(2)) is an FDCPA violation. Discovery in the collection case may produce
evidence of the violation.

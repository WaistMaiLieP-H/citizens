# §1028 Evolution — Stage 03: Identity Theft Penalty Enhancement Act (2004)

**Enactment:** Identity Theft Penalty Enhancement Act (ITPEA), Pub. L. 108-275
**Effective:** July 15, 2004
**Primary addition:** 18 USC §1028A (aggravated identity theft)

## The wound the 2004 Congress saw

Six years of ITADA enforcement experience had revealed a specific problem: federal prosecutors were winning §1028(a)(7) convictions but the resulting sentences were often modest — often probation or short confinement in cases where the underlying fraud had caused substantial victim harm. The 1998 framework criminalized the conduct but did not guarantee commensurate punishment.

Congress also observed that identity theft was increasingly appearing as a tool in other federal crimes — wire fraud, mail fraud, bank fraud, immigration fraud, terrorism-related offenses. When identity theft was an instrument of another crime, the §1028(a)(7) charge often merged into the guidelines calculation without adding meaningful sentencing exposure.

The 2004 wound was, in effect, that §1028(a)(7) had become a useful prosecutorial tool but not a deterrent.

## The logical structure of the 2004 solution

ITPEA took a different approach than simply raising §1028 penalties. Instead, it created a NEW statute — §1028A — that functions as a mandatory sentencing enhancement triggered by combining an identity theft element with an enumerated predicate felony.

The structural innovation: §1028A is not a standalone crime in the conventional sense. It is a consecutive-sentence rule. When a defendant commits any of the enumerated predicate felonies (listed in §1028A(c)) AND during and in relation to that felony knowingly transfers, possesses, or uses a means of identification of another person without lawful authority, the defendant receives a MANDATORY TWO-YEAR CONSECUTIVE sentence on top of whatever sentence the predicate felony carries.

Key features:
- **Mandatory:** The court has no discretion to reduce the two-year add-on.
- **Consecutive:** The two years runs after the predicate sentence. It cannot be served concurrently.
- **Broad predicate list:** Includes wire fraud, mail fraud, bank fraud, Social Security fraud, false statements to government, immigration fraud, passport fraud, and others.
- **Five-year enhancement for terrorism predicates:** If the predicate is a terrorism-related offense, the enhancement is five years consecutive, not two.

## The operational consequence

§1028A became, almost immediately, the single most powerful plea-negotiation lever the DOJ had for identity-theft-adjacent cases. A defendant facing a wire fraud indictment with a §1028A enhancement knows that conviction on both guarantees at least two additional years of confinement regardless of guidelines calculation or judicial leniency. The practical result: defendants with §1028A exposure plead more often, plead earlier, and cooperate more extensively.

The statute is not used in every identity theft case. Prosecutors use it strategically when:
- The underlying conduct is egregious
- The victim harm is substantial
- Cooperation from the defendant is needed
- A sentencing floor is desired to prevent what the prosecutor views as an inappropriately low sentence

## The interaction with sentencing guidelines

Because the two-year enhancement is mandatory and consecutive, it effectively operates outside the Sentencing Guidelines system. The guidelines calculation proceeds on the predicate offense as if §1028A did not exist; the §1028A sentence is then added to whatever the guidelines produced. This creates a structural mismatch that courts have had to interpret — generally in favor of the statutory mandate.

## Why this stage matters for the steward's cases

§1028A is directly relevant as criminal-referral leverage in the steward's matters:

1. **Treasury Securities case:** The predicate felonies — wire fraud (§1343), false statements to the United States (§1001), Treasury securities fraud — are all §1028A predicates. If the steward's case can be developed to USAO-prosecutable level, the §1028A enhancement creates significant negotiation leverage.

2. **Crypto fraud case:** Wire fraud is the master predicate. If the crypto fraud involved using the steward's means of identification (account credentials, SSN, DOB), §1028A attaches.

3. **Compound identity Hillberg case:** If the HILLBERGMANN compound identity was used to establish securities or insurance accounts that operated across state lines, the predicate offenses (wire fraud, securities fraud, mail fraud) satisfy §1028A and substantially raise the stakes of any criminal referral.

The Citizen's criminal-referral packages should always include a §1028A exposure analysis where the facts support it. The mere presence of §1028A exposure in a referral package changes how a USAO reads the file.

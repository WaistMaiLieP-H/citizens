# Logical Delta — 1998 ITADA → 2004 ITPEA

## What the 2004 amendment added

1. **New statute 18 USC §1028A** — aggravated identity theft as a mandatory consecutive sentencing enhancement.

2. **Enumerated predicate felonies** — a closed list in §1028A(c) specifying which felonies trigger the enhancement. Notable inclusions:
   - 18 USC §1341 (mail fraud)
   - 18 USC §1343 (wire fraud)
   - 18 USC §1344 (bank fraud)
   - 18 USC §1014 (false statements on loan applications)
   - 18 USC §1001 (false statements to government)
   - 18 USC §641 (theft of government property/funds)
   - 42 USC §408 (Social Security fraud)
   - Various immigration and passport fraud statutes

3. **Mandatory consecutive two-year sentence** — non-reducible, non-waivable, cannot run concurrent with the predicate felony sentence.

4. **Five-year enhancement for terrorism predicates** — a higher tier for crimes of terrorism under §2332b(g)(5)(B).

## What the 2004 amendment did NOT change

- §1028 itself was not substantively modified. The eight-paragraph enumerated list of prohibited acts remained the same.
- No private right of action was added.
- The civil RICO predicate status of §1028 was not altered.
- The "means of identification" definition remained the 1998 ITADA definition.

## The logic of creating a separate section

Congress could have added a two-year enhancement directly to §1028. It chose to create a separate section for two reasons:

1. **Predicate-felony architecture.** An enhancement embedded in §1028 would have required prosecutors to charge §1028 as a standalone count AND the predicate felony, doubling the pleading and proof requirements. §1028A is a true enhancement — a single additional element (knowing transfer/possession/use of means of identification) added to the predicate felony charge.

2. **Sentencing structure.** A separate section with its own mandatory consecutive rule keeps the two-year add-on outside the Sentencing Guidelines machinery. Embedding it in §1028 would have pulled it into the guidelines calculation, diluting its deterrent effect.

## What changed in prosecutorial practice

Before 2004, USAOs approached identity theft cases primarily as fraud cases with an identity theft tag. The §1028 charge was often stacked alongside wire fraud or bank fraud as a redundancy, rarely driving the sentencing outcome.

After 2004, the §1028A charge became the sentencing-driving charge in many cases. Prosecutors structured plea negotiations around "drop the §1028A and I'll plead to the predicate" offers, which the two-year mandatory consecutive rule made meaningful. The enhancement changed the bargaining architecture of identity-theft prosecutions.

## Why the steward should care

§1028A exposure is the single most useful criminal-referral leverage the Citizen can identify in the steward's cases. A civil defendant who would otherwise view the case as "a civil dispute" confronts a different picture when a referral package shows the fact pattern satisfies §1028A's elements. The defendant's calculation changes from "civil damages at worst" to "potential two years of mandatory federal time plus civil damages." Settlement behavior changes accordingly.

The Citizen should include a §1028A exposure checklist in every criminal-referral package for cases where the facts support the predicate felonies. The checklist preserves the leverage and signals to the USAO that the referring party understands the charging options available.

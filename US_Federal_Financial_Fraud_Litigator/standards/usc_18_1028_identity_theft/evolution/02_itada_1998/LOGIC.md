# §1028 Evolution — Stage 02: Identity Theft and Assumption Deterrence Act (1998)

**Enactment:** Identity Theft and Assumption Deterrence Act (ITADA), Pub. L. 105-318
**Effective:** October 30, 1998

## The wound the 1998 Congress saw

Between 1982 and 1998, the nature of identity-related harm had shifted in ways the 1982 document-centric framework could not reach. Federal Trade Commission data presented to Congress during the ITADA hearings documented:

1. **Credit-based identity theft surging.** Thieves were obtaining victims' names, SSNs, and dates of birth (typically from discarded records, mailbox theft, workplace rosters, or bribed employees) and using the data to open new credit accounts, run up charges, and disappear. Victims discovered the theft weeks or months later when collection calls began.

2. **No federal criminal hook.** Because the 1982 statute required a physical identification document, the data-theft cases were outside its reach. State prosecutors lacked consistent authority. The result: a class of crimes that was growing rapidly with no reliable federal criminal response.

3. **Victim burden.** The 1998 hearings emphasized that the victim bore the full cleanup cost — calling credit bureaus, disputing charges, correcting records — while the perpetrators went unprosecuted. The wound was as much about the absence of remedy as about the theft itself.

4. **The FTC's new role.** The 1998 Congress also directed the FTC to establish a centralized identity-theft complaint clearinghouse, creating for the first time a federal data-gathering function around identity theft.

## The logical shift from 1982 to 1998

The 1982 statute asked: **"Did the defendant produce, transfer, or possess a false identification document?"**

The 1998 amendment added a new question: **"Did the defendant knowingly transfer or use a means of identification of another person without lawful authority, with intent to commit any unlawful activity that constitutes a felony under federal law or state or local law?"**

The conceptual shift is from DOCUMENT to MEANS OF IDENTIFICATION. A "means of identification" is any name or number that can be used alone or in combination to identify a specific individual:

- Name
- SSN
- Date of birth
- Biometric data
- Unique electronic identification number
- Access device number
- Government-issued driver's license or ID number
- Routing number
- Alien registration number

The term is deliberately open-ended. Congress built in flexibility for technologies not yet invented in 1998 — a framing that would prove prescient as online commerce, electronic banking, and biometric authentication expanded in the following decade.

## The new prohibition: §1028(a)(7)

ITADA added subsection (a)(7) to the existing enumerated list of prohibited acts:

> (7) knowingly transfers or uses, without lawful authority, a means of identification of another person with the intent to commit, or to aid or abet, any unlawful activity that constitutes a violation of Federal law, or that constitutes a felony under any applicable State or local law

This is the modern identity theft prohibition. It requires:

1. **Knowing** transfer or use (scienter)
2. **Without lawful authority**
3. **A means of identification of another person** (not the defendant's own)
4. **Intent to commit, aid, or abet unlawful activity**
5. The unlawful activity must be a **federal violation** or a **state/local felony**

Note that the predicate unlawful activity need not be successful. The intent, combined with the prohibited transfer or use, completes the offense. A defendant who obtains a victim's SSN with intent to open fraudulent credit accounts violates (a)(7) the moment they transfer or use the SSN with that intent, even if the credit account is never opened.

## The FTC clearinghouse and §1028(g)

ITADA also created the FTC's identity theft complaint database (codified through the FTC's organic act, not directly in §1028), which became the foundation for the modern Identity Theft Report system used under FCRA §1681a(q) for the identity theft block procedure. The logical connection is critical: §1028 created the federal criminal framework; FCRA (via §1681c-2 added in 2003) created the civil remedy. Both depend on the 1998 definitional shift to "means of identification."

## Why this stage matters for the steward's cases

The 1998 amendment is the critical moment. Before 1998, the steward's Treasury Securities and SSN-theft fact patterns could not have been prosecuted under §1028 — they involve data transfer and use, not document forgery. After 1998, they are squarely within §1028(a)(7):

- **Treasury Securities case:** Someone obtained the steward's identifying data and used it to establish or modify Treasury-issued securities. Each "transfer or use" of the steward's SSN or name in the Treasury system to redirect the securities is a §1028(a)(7) offense, if done knowingly and without authority.

- **SSN theft documentation:** The steward documents that his SSN appears under other individuals' records. Each use of his SSN by another is a potential §1028(a)(7) violation.

- **ChexSystems 2024 entry:** If the adverse entry resulted from another person using the steward's means of identification to open an account that was then closed for cause, that is a §1028(a)(7) predicate and a FCRA §1681c-2 identity theft block basis.

The 1998 shift from document logic to data logic is what makes these cases criminally actionable at all, and it's what makes the civil RICO pathway via §1961(1)(B)'s §1028 reference available.

# Logical Delta — 1982 Origin → 1998 ITADA

## The conceptual shift

| Axis | 1982 Framework | 1998 Framework |
|---|---|---|
| **Object protected** | Identification documents (physical) | Identification documents + means of identification (data) |
| **Vocabulary of harm** | "False ID," "counterfeit document" | "Identity theft," "assumption of identity" |
| **Required conduct** | Produce, transfer, possess with intent | Add: knowingly transfer or use without authority |
| **Scope of "identification"** | Governmental issuer required | Any name or number identifying a person |
| **Victim conception** | None (crime vs. document system) | Victim as an individual whose identity was assumed |
| **Technological frame** | Physical document forgery | Data transfer + use in electronic systems |

## What the 1998 Congress added in substance

1. **Subsection (a)(7):** The core identity theft prohibition. Knowing transfer or use of another person's means of identification with intent to commit any federal violation or state/local felony.

2. **"Means of identification" definition:** A new defined term, open-ended, including name, SSN, date of birth, biometric data, electronic identifiers, and "any other number or information that can be used to identify a specific individual."

3. **Victim recognition:** ITADA was the first federal statute to treat the individual whose identity was stolen as a VICTIM, not merely as a witness. This enabled later development of victim remedies in FCRA and elsewhere.

4. **FTC clearinghouse mandate:** Directed the FTC to establish a centralized identity theft complaint database, which became the foundation for later FCRA identity theft procedures.

5. **Sentencing enhancements:** Raised maximum sentences for §1028 violations involving means of identification.

## What the 1998 Congress did NOT do

The 1998 amendment did not create a private right of action. §1028 remains a criminal statute. Private remedies for identity theft victims came later, through FCRA §1681c-2 (2003), state statutes, and civil RICO via §1028's inclusion as a RICO predicate.

The 1998 amendment also did not address the "compound identity" problem — situations where an identity is not stolen outright but blended with another (e.g., HILLBERGMANN-type compound surnames, or the use of a real SSN with a fabricated name). Lower courts have generally read §1028(a)(7) broadly enough to reach such conduct, but the text was written with clean identity-assumption in mind.

## The operational significance

After 1998, a federal prosecutor evaluating an identity-theft fact pattern asks a different question than before. The 1982 question was: "Is there a physical document I can point to?" The 1998 question is: "Is there a means of identification of another person that was knowingly transferred or used without authority with intent to commit a predicate offense?" The second question is easier to answer yes in the data age.

For civil practitioners, the 1998 framework is what makes §1028 usable as a RICO predicate in the way the modern identity-theft civil bar uses it. Without the "means of identification" language, pure-data identity theft could not satisfy the pattern-of-racketeering-activity requirement.

## Why the steward should care

The steward's entire identity-theft portfolio depends on the 1998 framework. Every one of his matters — Treasury Securities, SSN theft, ChexSystems, compound-identity Hillberg theory — is a "means of identification" case, not a "physical document" case. The 1998 shift is the doctrinal foundation for the cases' viability under federal law.

Civil RICO pleading will specifically cite the 1998 post-ITADA version of §1028 as the predicate offense within the §1961(1)(B) list, since that is the version in which the conduct at issue became criminal.

# §1028 Evolution — Stage 01: Origin (1982)

**Enactment:** False Identification Crime Control Act of 1982, Pub. L. 97-398
**Effective:** December 31, 1982

## The wound the 1982 Congress saw

By the early 1980s, Congress was responding to a specific and documented problem: the proliferation of fake driver's licenses, fake birth certificates, and fake passports used to:

1. Defraud federal benefit programs (food stamps, Social Security, Medicare)
2. Evade law enforcement (fugitives obtaining false licenses)
3. Obtain weapons in evasion of gun control laws
4. Cash stolen checks
5. Commit bank fraud using fake ID

The existing federal statutes addressed only the most specific document crimes — passport fraud (§1542), immigration fraud (§1543-1546), and some counterfeit currency offenses — but there was no general prohibition against making, selling, or possessing fake identification. The Secret Service, the FBI, and state law enforcement were running into cases where the only federal hook was a derivative mail-fraud or wire-fraud theory, with §1028's predecessor statutes providing piecemeal coverage at best.

## The logical structure Congress built

The 1982 statute's logic was document-centric. It protected "identification documents" — a term Congress defined by listing the governmental issuers. The act focused on:

- **Producing** false ID documents without lawful authority
- **Transferring** such documents knowing they were false
- **Possessing with intent** to use unlawfully

The prohibited conduct was organized around physical documents: driver's licenses, birth certificates, passports, green cards, SSN cards. The statute's vocabulary assumed the document was the thing being defended.

## What the 1982 framework could NOT reach

Critically, the 1982 statute did not reach cases where the defendant never touched a physical document. A person who obtained a victim's SSN and date of birth and used that data — without forging any physical card — to open a credit account was not committing a §1028 offense under the 1982 text. The conduct was data-centric, but the statute was document-centric.

By the 1990s this gap had become enormous. The rise of electronic credit-card processing, telephone account opening, and early online commerce meant that identity thieves could operate entirely in the data layer, never producing a fake physical document. The 1982 logic missed the wound the next generation of thieves would exploit.

## Why this stage matters for the steward's cases

The 1982 logic is the baseline from which the modern statute departed. The Citizen needs to know where the statute started to evaluate:

1. **What conduct was criminalized only after 1998** (the ITADA amendment adding §1028(a)(7) means of identification prohibitions)
2. **Which prior-art fact patterns the original 1982 framework was designed to reach** — still relevant for counterfeit-document cases
3. **The structural logic of "producing, transferring, possessing"** — a logic preserved through every subsequent amendment

For the Treasury Securities and compound-identity cases, the 1982 framework is insufficient on its own — the modern data-centric conduct those cases involve required the 1998 amendment to fit within the statute. But the 1982 framework supplies the foundation vocabulary (produce, transfer, possess with intent) that the later amendments built on.

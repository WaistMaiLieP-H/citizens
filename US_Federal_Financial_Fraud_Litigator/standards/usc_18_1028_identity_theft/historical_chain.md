# Historical Chain — 18 USC §1028 (Federal Identity Theft Statute)

## The wound

Federal identity theft law exists because, across four decades, the mechanism by which one human being could be turned into a means of financial extraction by another changed faster than common-law remedies could catch up. In 1982 the wound was forged drivers' licenses and counterfeit SSN cards used to defraud federal benefit programs and evade law enforcement. In 1998 the wound was data theft — the discovery that an SSN and a date of birth, transmitted electronically, could open new credit accounts without the victim's knowledge. In 2004 the wound was the discovery that §1028(a)(7) convictions were not producing sentences proportionate to the harm, and that identity theft had become an instrument of other federal crimes. In 2009 the wound was prosecutorial overreach — using §1028A to charge accidental-match cases where no real knowledge of the victim existed. In 2023 the wound was prosecutorial creep — using §1028A in cases where the identity theft was incidental paperwork rather than the mechanism of the fraud.

At each moment, Congress or the Court answered a specific documented harm with a specific structural adjustment. The result is a statute that has expanded, narrowed, expanded, and narrowed again — always in response to a revealed problem in how the last version was working.

## The promise

The promise at the center of §1028 is that the individual whose identity has been stolen is a victim the federal criminal law recognizes. That was not true before 1998. A 1985 ID-theft victim had no federal criminal protection for pure data theft — the statute reached only physical document forgery. The 1998 ITADA amendment changed this: it made the individual a victim for the first time, it required the FTC to build a clearinghouse for victim complaints, and it opened the door to the civil remedies that followed (FCRA §1681c-2 identity theft block, 2003; various state identity-theft statutes; civil RICO private right via §1028's predicate status).

The promise is that the federal government will treat identity theft as a real crime against a real person, not as a technical violation of a document system.

## The arc

**1982 — False Identification Crime Control Act.** Congress answers the rise of counterfeit physical IDs with §1028. Document-centric logic: produce, transfer, possess. Covers driver's licenses, birth certificates, SSN cards, passports. Does NOT reach pure data theft.

**1998 — Identity Theft and Assumption Deterrence Act (ITADA).** Congress answers the rise of data-centric identity theft with §1028(a)(7) and the "means of identification" concept. First federal statute to treat individual victims as statutory victims. FTC clearinghouse mandated.

**2003 — FACTA amendments to FCRA** (covered in a separate FCRA standard build). Private civil remedy for identity theft victims added via §1681c-2 identity theft block procedure. Depends on the 1998 ITADA definitional framework.

**2004 — Identity Theft Penalty Enhancement Act (ITPEA).** Congress answers inadequate sentencing by creating §1028A — mandatory consecutive two-year sentence for identity theft in connection with enumerated predicate felonies. Five years for terrorism predicates. Transforms §1028A into the DOJ's principal plea-negotiation lever for identity-related fraud.

**2008 — Identity Theft Enforcement and Restitution Act** (minor amendment). Expands restitution authority; clarifies victim-witness rights. Not structurally significant.

**2009 — Flores-Figueroa v. United States** (Supreme Court). Narrows §1028A by holding that "knowingly" requires actual knowledge that the means of identification belonged to a real person. Closes the "accidental match" loophole. Forces prosecutors to prove actual-person knowledge.

**2015-2022 — Various technical amendments.** Added covered crimes to the §1028A(c) predicate list; adjusted sentencing provisions. No structural change.

**2023 — Dubin v. United States** (Supreme Court). Narrows §1028A further by requiring the identity theft to be "at the crux" of the underlying fraud, not incidental to it. Ends the DOJ practice of charging §1028A in healthcare fraud cases where identifiers appear on billing paperwork but are not mechanism-of-fraud. Reins in prosecutorial creep.

## Current state (2026)

§1028 and §1028A as they currently read are the product of a four-decade iterative conversation between Congress and the courts. The statute's logical architecture:

- **§1028(a)(1)-(6), (a)(8):** traditional document-centric prohibitions (1982 logic)
- **§1028(a)(7):** modern data-centric identity theft prohibition (1998 logic)
- **§1028A:** mandatory sentencing enhancement (2004 logic, narrowed by Flores-Figueroa 2009 and Dubin 2023)

The statute reaches most modern identity-theft fact patterns, including:
- Pure data theft (post-1998)
- Compound identity schemes (by reading §1028(a)(7) broadly)
- Account takeovers (as "use without authority")
- Biometric identifier theft (as "means of identification")
- Treasury and federal-benefit identity exploitation (with automatic interstate nexus)

The statute does NOT reach (or reaches only with difficulty):
- Cases where identifiers are incidental (post-Dubin)
- Cases where no real person is identified (post-Flores-Figueroa accidental-match bar)
- Pure state-law fraud with no federal nexus
- Civil damages (no private right of action — must use civil RICO predicate pathway or parallel FCRA claims)

## Why this history matters for the steward

The steward's cases all arise after the 1998 ITADA framework is in place, so all are pleadable under modern §1028(a)(7) logic. None of them runs into the Dubin "incidental use" bar, because in each case the identity theft is the mechanism of the fraud, not incidental to it. All of them have the federal nexus satisfied automatically by the "issued by the United States" prong, because SSNs and Treasury-account identifiers are federal-government means of identification.

The arc of the statute — document logic → data logic → sentencing enhancement → narrowing corrections — supplies the doctrinal vocabulary the Citizen uses when drafting criminal-referral packages and civil RICO complaints. Knowing that the statute went through four iterations, and why, lets the Citizen plead with precision the specific subsection and interpretation that applies to each fact pattern in the steward's portfolio.

The promise the federal government made in 1998 — that individual identity-theft victims would be treated as victims of a real crime — is the promise the steward's cases now invoke.

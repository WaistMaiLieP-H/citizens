# Dubin v. United States (2023)

**Cite:** 599 U.S. 110
**Court:** Supreme Court of the United States
**Date:** June 8, 2023
**Opinion by:** Justice Sotomayor

## The rule the case stands for

**To sustain a §1028A conviction, the use of the means of identification must be "at the crux" of the underlying fraud — the means of identification must be central to how the fraud was accomplished, not merely incidental or ancillary to it.** A fraudster who uses another person's identifier as part of routine billing or paperwork, while committing a separate fraud, does not violate §1028A unless the identity theft is central to the fraud mechanism itself.

## Plain-language restatement

David Dubin was a psychologist who overbilled Medicaid for services. As part of the billing process, he submitted forms listing patients (real Medicaid beneficiaries) whose treatment was misrepresented. The government charged §1028A on the theory that Dubin had "used" the patients' means of identification (their Medicaid ID numbers) in committing healthcare fraud.

The Supreme Court held that this was a step too far. Using a patient's Medicaid ID in the course of billing is not the kind of "use" §1028A targets — the identity theft has to be what makes the fraud possible. A fraud that would have worked just as well without borrowing anyone's identity is not an aggravated identity theft merely because identifiers appear on the paperwork.

The Court articulated a "at the crux" test: the means of identification must play a central role in the fraud — it must be "essential" or "critical" to the fraudulent scheme.

## The doctrinal significance

Dubin narrowed §1028A significantly. Before Dubin, prosecutors were using §1028A expansively — treating virtually any fraud that touched paperwork with other people's identifiers as "aggravated identity theft." Dubin ended that practice.

After Dubin, §1028A applies to:
1. Classic identity theft — taking over another person's identity to impersonate them
2. Fraud that cannot work without another person's identifier (e.g., applying for a loan in the victim's name)
3. Schemes where the identifier is the mechanism of the fraud, not incidental paperwork

It does NOT apply to:
1. Fraud that happens to involve other people's identifiers as collateral information (e.g., over-billing Medicaid using real patient IDs when the fraud is the over-billing, not the patient identification)
2. Bookkeeping fraud where the identifiers are clerical
3. Insider fraud that does not depend on assumed identity

## Subsequent prosecutorial adjustment

After Dubin, DOJ guidance instructed prosecutors to charge §1028A only where the identity theft is essential to the fraud — not as a default add-on to every fraud case involving records. The practical effect has been a meaningful reduction in §1028A charging rates in non-classic-identity-theft cases.

## Why this matters for the steward's cases

Dubin is a double-edged doctrine for the steward's cases. It is a constraint on prosecutorial leverage — §1028A charges are harder to sustain than they were before 2023. But the constraint does not meaningfully limit the steward's cases because the identity theft is NOT incidental to the underlying harm in his fact patterns:

1. **Treasury Securities case:** The fraud is the unauthorized reissuance of securities IN the steward's name. The identity IS the mechanism of the fraud. The steward's identifying information is at the crux of the scheme — without it, there are no securities to misappropriate. Dubin does not affect this case.

2. **Compound identity Hillberg case:** A compound identity is by definition the mechanism of the fraud — it exists to identify an imaginary hybrid entity capable of transacting. The identity IS the fraud. Dubin does not affect.

3. **ChexSystems case:** An adverse entry in the steward's real name, placed by someone else's conduct, is a classic identity-assumption fact pattern. Dubin does not affect.

4. **Crypto fraud case:** Depends on facts. If the crypto theft was accomplished by taking over the steward's account credentials (means of identification), the identity theft is central. If it was a separate fraud that merely happened to involve the steward's records incidentally, Dubin would narrow the §1028A exposure.

The Citizen should still plead carefully around Dubin: in every §1028A analysis, include an express "at the crux" showing explaining why the identity theft is central to the fraud mechanism, not merely incidental.

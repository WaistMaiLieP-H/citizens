The case's primary impact is on § 1028A's scienter requirement. For § 1028 (the underlying identity theft statute from which § 1028A is derived), the holding confirms that knowingly using another real person's identifying information is required for the compound offense.

CORE HOLDING FOR § 1028 CONTEXT:
Congress required a mens rea of knowledge — the defendant must know the identifying information belongs to a real person — when criminalizing identity theft under the statutory scheme of § 1028 and § 1028A together.

SECTION 1028 PREDICATE ANALYSIS:
Section 1028A requires that the defendant commit a predicate felony enumerated in § 1028A(c). Section 1028(a)(7) — the use of another person's means of identification without lawful authority to commit a federal or state felony — is one of those predicates. Flores-Figueroa addressed the § 1028A overlay, but the intent standard (knowing use of real person's identity) applies to § 1028(a)(7) as well.

For the steward's cases: SIM swap fraud and synthetic identity theft operations that use real Social Security numbers, real phone numbers, and real account credentials to impersonate the victim fully satisfy the Flores-Figueroa knowledge requirement — the attacker demonstrably knows the credentials belong to a real person. That is the mechanism of the attack.

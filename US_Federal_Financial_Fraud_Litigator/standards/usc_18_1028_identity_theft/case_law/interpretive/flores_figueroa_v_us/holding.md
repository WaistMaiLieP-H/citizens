# Flores-Figueroa v. United States (2009)

**Cite:** 556 U.S. 646
**Court:** Supreme Court of the United States
**Date:** May 4, 2009
**Opinion by:** Justice Breyer (unanimous)

## The rule the case stands for

**To convict under 18 USC §1028A (aggravated identity theft), the government must prove that the defendant KNEW the means of identification belonged to another ACTUAL PERSON.** It is not enough to prove that the defendant knowingly used an identification document or identifier — the government must prove the defendant knew it belonged to a real person, not to a fictitious or manufactured identity.

## Plain-language restatement

A defendant used a fake SSN and a fake Permanent Resident Card to get a job at a steel mill. Both identifiers happened to belong to real people, but the defendant did not know that — he believed he was using made-up numbers. The government argued that "knowingly" in §1028A modified only the defendant's knowledge that he was using the identifier, not his knowledge of whose identifier it was. The Supreme Court disagreed unanimously.

Justice Breyer's opinion held that "knowingly" is ordinary English and, as a matter of English grammar, applies to all the material elements that follow it in the sentence. Applied to §1028A, this means the government must prove the defendant knew:
1. He was transferring, possessing, or using
2. Without lawful authority
3. A means of identification
4. **Of another person** (i.e., a real person, not fictitious)

## The doctrinal significance

Flores-Figueroa closed a loophole prosecutors had been exploiting. Before the decision, some USAOs were charging §1028A based on the happenstance that a defendant's fabricated identifier matched a real person's data, without proving the defendant knew of the match. Flores-Figueroa held that kind of "accidental match" liability was not what Congress had in mind.

The decision is important for two reasons:

1. **It narrowed §1028A in the defendant-favorable direction**, requiring actual knowledge rather than constructive or imputed knowledge.

2. **It established an interpretive rule that applies beyond §1028A** — "knowingly" in criminal statutes applies to all material elements following it absent a contrary indication. This rule has been applied in subsequent cases involving other mens rea disputes.

## Subsequent history and status

- **Current status (2026):** Flores-Figueroa remains controlling. It is routinely cited in §1028A prosecutions for the knowledge-of-actual-person element.
- **Prosecutorial adaptation:** USAOs now require evidence showing the defendant specifically knew the identifier belonged to a real person — often through evidence of identity-theft source (e.g., stolen from a specific victim whose identity the defendant targeted) or through evidence of verification-checking conduct.
- **Dubin v. United States (2023)** further narrowed §1028A (see separate case file).

## Why this matters for the steward's cases

For the steward's criminal-referral strategy, Flores-Figueroa is a reminder that any §1028A exposure analysis must include the "knew of actual person" element. In the steward's fact patterns, that element is typically easy to satisfy:

- Treasury Securities: the perpetrator used the steward's actual SSN and name to manipulate actual Treasury accounts — knowledge of the real person is intrinsic to the conduct.
- ChexSystems: an adverse entry in the steward's real name, linked to the steward's real SSN, was made by someone who knew (or could not plausibly deny knowing) the identity was real.
- Compound identity: HILLBERGMANN as a compound involves combining parts of real identities — knowledge of the real source person is implicit in the blending.

The Citizen still must plead the element explicitly in any referral package or civil complaint invoking §1028A as leverage, but the facts in the steward's cases support it.

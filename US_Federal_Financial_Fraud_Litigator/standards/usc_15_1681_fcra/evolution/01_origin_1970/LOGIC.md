# FCRA — Evolution Stage 01: Origin 1970
## Fair Credit Reporting Act, Pub. L. 91-508 (1970)

**Enacted:** October 26, 1970
**Effective:** April 25, 1971 (six-month delayed effective date)
**Congressional sponsor:** Senate: William Proxmire (D-WI); House companion

---

## The wound this enactment answered

**The pre-1970 problem:** Consumer credit bureaus had existed since the late 1800s as private
clubs for creditors. They existed to serve creditors, not consumers. By 1970:

1. **Black box opacity:** A consumer had no right to see their own credit file. If a bank denied
   a loan, the consumer received no explanation — only denial. The bureau's file was proprietary
   to the subscribing creditors.

2. **No accuracy duty:** Bureaus owed no legal duty to the consumer to be accurate. A bureau
   could report a debt as unpaid that had been paid five years ago, a default belonging to a
   person with a similar name, or a public record that had been expunged — with no liability.

3. **No dispute right:** Even if a consumer learned of an error (usually through informal inquiry),
   the bureau had no legal obligation to investigate or correct it.

4. **Indefinite shelf life:** Adverse information could stay in a file indefinitely. Bankruptcies
   from the 1940s still appeared in 1970 reports.

5. **Employment investigation market:** Beyond credit, there was an "investigative consumer report"
   industry that prepared oral reports on consumers' character, reputation, lifestyle, and personal
   habits — assembled through neighbor interviews — for use by employers and insurers. No notice
   to the consumer. No right of review.

Congressional testimony in 1969-1970 documented cases where consumers were denied employment
because of stale information or outright errors, and could not find out why or correct the record.

---

## The 1970 logical structure (what Congress built)

### Core logical move: CRA as regulated intermediary
Congress did not eliminate credit bureaus. It imposed a duty relationship: bureaus became
"consumer reporting agencies" with duties running to consumers — not just to subscribing creditors.

### Key 1970 structures:

**Permissible purposes** (§1681b): Reports may only be furnished for credit, employment, insurance,
government licensing, or a "legitimate business need." Not open-ended.

**Adverse action notice** (§1681m): If a creditor denies credit based on a consumer report, it
must tell the consumer — and tell them which CRA's report was used so the consumer can request
their file.

**Consumer file disclosure** (§1681g): Consumer may request to see the substance of their file
(though not necessarily the full verbatim report in 1970).

**Dispute mechanism** (§1681i, 1970 version): Consumer can dispute accuracy. CRA must reinvestigate
within a "reasonable period" (1970 text did not specify 30 days — that came later). If unverifiable,
delete. Consumer may add a 100-word statement of dispute to their file.

**Accuracy duty** (§1681e(b)): "Reasonable procedures to assure maximum possible accuracy" — the
foundational accuracy command.

**Obsolescence rules** (§1681c): Adverse items age out — bankruptcies 10 years, most other items
7 years. Ended indefinite shelf life.

**Investigative consumer reports** (§1681d): Employers/insurers must tell a consumer before ordering
an investigative report (character/lifestyle investigation), and consumer may request nature/scope.

---

## What the 1970 statute did NOT do

- Did not give consumers the right to receive a free copy of their report on demand (that came with FACTA 2003).
- Did not create an explicit furnisher duty — no obligation on creditors/banks who sent data to CRAs to keep that data accurate or respond to disputes.
- Did not address identity theft (the concept barely existed in 1970's legal vocabulary).
- Did not establish a "blocking" mechanism for fraudulent tradelines.
- Did not specify 30-day reinvestigation deadline (added by CCRAA 1996).

---

## Logical shift from pre-1970 baseline

| Dimension | Pre-1970 | After 1970 |
|---|---|---|
| Who bureau serves | Creditors only | Creditors + consumers (dual obligation) |
| Consumer knowledge of file | None | Right to review file substance |
| Accuracy obligation | None | Reasonable procedures standard |
| Dispute right | None | Reinvestigation + deletion of unverifiable |
| Adverse info shelf life | Indefinite | 7 years (most items), 10 years (bankruptcy) |
| Employment investigations | Unregulated | Notice required before ordering |
| Damages | None | Negligent: actual + fees; Willful: actual or statutory + punitive |

## Why this stage matters for Michael's cases

The 1970 origin stage establishes the foundational accuracy duty (§1681e(b)) and reinvestigation
right (§1681i) that ChexSystems violates if it maintained false entries about Michael's deposit
accounts. The wound — bureau opacity with no consumer remedy — maps directly to the ChexSystems
situation: a specialty CRA blocking banking access based on entries Michael cannot verify, correct,
or dispute effectively.

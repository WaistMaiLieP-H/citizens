# Wire Fraud — Evolution Stage 02: McNally Limitation + §1346 Restoration (1987–1988)

**Key events:**
- McNally v. United States, 483 US 350 (1987) — Supreme Court narrows "scheme to defraud"
- 18 USC §1346 enacted, Pub. L. 100-690 (Nov. 18, 1988) — Congress restores honest services

---

## The wound McNally exposed

For decades before 1987, courts had interpreted "scheme to defraud" in §1341 (and by extension
§1343) to include schemes that deprived people of their intangible right to honest services —
the honest performance of duties by public officials and corporate employees. A corrupt official
who accepted a bribe defrauded the public of honest services even if no money was taken from
the government. An employee who steered contracts in exchange for kickbacks defrauded their
employer of honest services.

**McNally (1987):** The Supreme Court rejected this interpretation. The Court held that the
mail fraud statute (and by extension wire fraud) protects property rights only — not the
intangible right to honest government or corporate services. The Court read the legislative
history to show Congress was targeting schemes that deprive victims of money or property, not
a generalized anti-corruption statute.

**The gap McNally created:** Wire fraud could no longer reach bribery, kickback, and corruption
schemes where the direct victim was not deprived of money or property — only of honest services.

---

## Congress's 1988 response — 18 USC §1346

**Command:** "For the purposes of this chapter [Chapter 63, mail and wire fraud], the term
'scheme or artifice to defraud' includes a scheme or artifice to deprive another of the
intangible right of honest services."

**Logical effect:** §1346 overruled McNally legislatively — it explicitly restored honest
services fraud as a covered theory. BUT: it did not define "honest services," leaving the
content of the theory uncertain.

**Skilling v. United States (2010):** The Supreme Court construed §1346 narrowly to avoid
vagueness problems. The Court held that honest services fraud under §1346 only covers the
paradigmatic case: bribery and kickback schemes involving a public official or private fiduciary.
Pure undisclosed self-dealing (conflict of interest without a quid pro quo payment) is not
covered.

---

## What this means for the current framework

**Current operative rule:**
- §1343 criminalizes schemes to defraud as to property (money, tangible and intangible property)
- §1346 adds honest services fraud — but only for bribery/kickback schemes (Skilling)
- Kelly v. United States (2020) tightened the property requirement: the scheme must aim to
  deprive the victim of something cognizable as property; a scheme aimed at regulatory power
  or policy rather than property fails the property element

**For Michael's cases:** The wire fraud theory does NOT depend on §1346 honest services.
All of Michael's financial fraud cases involve property fraud — Treasury bonds (property),
cryptocurrency (property), bank accounts (property/money), and financial instruments (property).
The McNally/§1346/Skilling evolution is relevant only to understand the limits of the honest
services theory, not to limit the classic property fraud theory that applies to his cases.

---

## What changed: 1952 baseline → post-1988

| Dimension | 1952 origin | Post-1988 |
|---|---|---|
| "Scheme to defraud" scope | Property + broad honest services (circuit courts) | Property (§1343) + bribery/kickbacks only (§1346 + Skilling) |
| Honest services | Expanded by courts over 35 years | Legislatively restored in 1988, then judicially narrowed in 2010 |
| Enhanced penalty | None | Up to 30 years if financial institution affected |
| RICO predicate | Yes (§1961(1)(B) lists §1343) | Same |

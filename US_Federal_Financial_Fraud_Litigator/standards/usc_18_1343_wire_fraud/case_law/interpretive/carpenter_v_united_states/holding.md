# Carpenter v. United States
## 484 US 19 (1987)

**Court:** United States Supreme Court
**Decided:** November 16, 1987
**Vote:** 4-4 (affirmed by equal division on wire fraud count; unanimous on securities fraud count)

---

## Issue decided

Can confidential business information — specifically, advance knowledge of what a newspaper
column will say about a company's stock — constitute "property" within the meaning of the
mail and wire fraud statutes?

---

## Holding

**Yes.** The Wall Street Journal's confidential information about the content of its upcoming
"Heard on the Street" column was property belonging to the Journal. An employee who
misappropriated that information for personal stock trading defrauded the Journal of its
intangible property — the right to exclusive use of the information before publication.

**Key rule:** "Property" under §1341 (and by extension §1343) is not limited to tangible
assets or money. It includes intangible property rights, specifically:
- **Confidential business information** that has economic value to its owner
- **Misappropriated information** whose premature disclosure deprives the owner of the
  economic benefit of that information

The fraud was directed at the Journal (loss of exclusive control over its upcoming column
content), not at the securities-market victims who lost money. The employer's interest in
controlling its confidential business information was the "property" that was defrauded.

---

## Why this case matters for Michael

**Intangible property theory:** Carpenter establishes that intangible assets — confidential
account information, SSN, identity credentials — are property for wire fraud purposes. A
fraudster who steals Michael's SSN and uses it to open Treasury accounts, crypto wallets, or
bank accounts is defrauding Michael of his identity credentials (property) via whatever wire
communications are used in the scheme.

**Identity theft as property fraud:** The wire fraud theory for Michael's cases does not
depend on the thief stealing cash — it depends on the thief stealing and misusing identity
information (property) via interstate electronic communications. Carpenter supports this
framing.

**Limitation — post-Kelly:** The scheme must target property of the victim, not just exploit
regulatory systems or government processes. Michael's cases satisfy this because the theft
targets his identity credentials, his financial accounts, and his government-issued bonds —
all property — not merely regulatory compliance.

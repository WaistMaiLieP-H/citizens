# Neder v. United States
## 527 US 1 (1999)

**Court:** United States Supreme Court
**Decided:** June 10, 1999
**Vote:** 9-0 on materiality element; 5-4 on harmless error

---

## Issue decided

Is materiality an element of wire fraud (§1343), mail fraud (§1341), and bank fraud (§1344)?
And if the trial court erroneously failed to instruct the jury on materiality, is that error
subject to harmless-error analysis?

---

## Holding — Part 1 (unanimous): Materiality is an element

**Yes, materiality is an element of wire fraud.** Although the text of §1341 and §1343 does
not use the word "material," the term "defraud" is a common-law term with established meaning.
Congress is presumed to have incorporated the common-law meaning when it used the common-law
term without modification.

**Common-law materiality test:** A misrepresentation or omission is material if it has a
natural tendency to influence, or is capable of influencing, the decision of a reasonable person.
Courts do not ask whether the specific victim was actually influenced — only whether a reasonable
person would have been.

**Practical implication:** The government must prove that the fraudulent representation was
material — that it would influence a reasonable decision-maker. The government does not have
to prove the victim was actually deceived or actually lost money as a result of the specific
misrepresentation (although actual loss is relevant to sentencing under USSG §2B1.1).

---

## Holding — Part 2 (majority): Harmless error applies

The failure to instruct the jury on materiality is constitutional error subject to harmless-
error review (not structural error requiring automatic reversal). If the evidence of materiality
was overwhelming and uncontested, the error may be harmless.

---

## Why this case matters for Michael

**Materiality in identity theft / financial fraud:** In Michael's cases, materiality is
essentially built in. Any misrepresentation of identity (false name, stolen SSN, fake identity
documents) is inherently material — no financial institution, government agency, or creditor
would take the action it took (open account, transfer bonds, issue credit) if it knew the
applicant was an identity thief using stolen credentials. Neder's materiality test is easily
satisfied.

**Government referral brief framing:** In any referral to DOJ, FBI, or IRS Criminal Investigation,
the materiality element should be addressed explicitly: "The identity representations were
material because no institution would have [opened the account / transferred the bonds / issued
the credit] had it known the applicant's true identity."

**No reliance required:** Neder confirms that wire fraud does not require that the victim
actually relied on the misrepresentation or actually suffered a dollar loss as a result of
relying on a specific statement. The scheme as a whole defrauds; materiality is the threshold
for whether the fraudulent representations are serious enough to constitute a "scheme to defraud."

# Evolution Stage 02 — 2001 PATRIOT Act Amendment + Warshak (6th Cir. 2010)

### THE WOUND (POST-9/11)

The USA PATRIOT Act used the post-September 11 legislative momentum to expand
government access to electronic communications. For the SCA, the concern was
investigative speed: the existing framework required government to obtain different
levels of process (warrant, court order, subpoena) for different types of stored
communications, creating delays in terrorism investigations.

### DESIGN RESPONSE — PATRIOT ACT 2001

**USA PATRIOT Act, Pub. L. 107-56 (Oct. 26, 2001):**

1. **Emergency access:** Added emergency disclosure provisions allowing service
   providers to disclose stored communications to law enforcement in emergencies
   involving risk of death or serious injury

2. **Expanded subpoena scope:** Clarified what information could be obtained by
   subpoena vs. court order vs. warrant; modernized the definition of "subscriber
   records" to include modern communication services

3. **Multi-district authority:** Addressed jurisdictional issues in obtaining stored
   communication records across judicial districts

4. **Pen Register expansion:** Title IV of PATRIOT Act expanded pen register provisions
   (§§3121-3127) to cover internet routing information

**Effect on civil plaintiffs:** The PATRIOT Act amendments primarily affected government
access procedures, not the §2707 civil action framework. The civil remedy (§2707)
remained unchanged: $1,000 minimum per violation, actual damages, punitive, fees.

### WARSHAK v. UNITED STATES — CONSTITUTIONAL OVERLAY

**United States v. Warshak, 631 F.3d 266 (6th Cir. 2010):**

The Sixth Circuit held that email content stored with a third-party service provider
is protected by the Fourth Amendment — the government must obtain a search warrant to
compel disclosure of email contents from service providers. The SCA's provision allowing
access to email stored >180 days with only a subpoena (not a warrant) was held to be
an unconstitutional seizure.

**Effect on SCA age-based framework:** Warshak effectively required warrant-level process
for all stored email content, regardless of age. While this was a Sixth Circuit ruling
(not binding on all circuits), it established the constitutional floor and influenced
DOJ policy nationwide (the DOJ adopted a warrant-for-email policy as a matter of practice).

**Effect on civil claims:** Warshak does not directly change §2707 civil damages. However,
it establishes that stored email has constitutional protection commensurate with wire
interceptions — reinforcing the argument that unauthorized access to stored email (by
a private actor through SIM swap account takeover) causes the same type of injury as
wire interception.

### LOGICAL DELTA

| Element | Pre-PATRIOT Act | Post-PATRIOT Act |
|---------|----------------|-----------------|
| Government emergency access | No emergency exception | Emergency disclosure to law enforcement permitted |
| Email stored >180 days government access | Subpoena with notice | Still subpoena under statute; Warshak requires warrant under Constitution |
| Civil remedy §2707 | $1,000 minimum + actual + punitive | UNCHANGED — civil framework not altered |

### PROVENANCE

USA PATRIOT Act, Pub. L. 107-56 (Oct. 26, 2001) — Titles II and IV
United States v. Warshak, 631 F.3d 266 (6th Cir. 2010) — constitutional interpretation

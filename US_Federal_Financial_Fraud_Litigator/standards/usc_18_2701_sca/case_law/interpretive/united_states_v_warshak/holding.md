# United States v. Warshak — Holding

**Citation:** United States v. Warshak, 631 F.3d 266 (6th Cir. 2010)
**Court:** United States Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit
**Decided:** December 14, 2010

## Facts

Steven Warshak was the founder of a nutraceutical company charged with mail fraud,
wire fraud, and conspiracy. As part of the investigation, the government obtained
approximately 27,000 of Warshak's private emails from his ISP using §2703(d) court
orders and subpoenas — without a search warrant. Warshak moved to suppress the emails,
arguing the government needed a warrant under the Fourth Amendment.

## Holding

**Email content stored with a third-party service provider is protected by the Fourth
Amendment. The government must obtain a search warrant supported by probable cause
to compel disclosure of the contents of a subscriber's emails from an ISP.**

The court applied the reasonable expectation of privacy test (Katz v. United States).
Users of email services have a reasonable expectation that their email communications
will remain private, just as they have a reasonable expectation in the contents of
letters they send through postal mail. The third-party doctrine (Miller, Smith v.
Maryland) does not eliminate this expectation for the content of private communications.

The SCA's §2703(b) provision allowing government access to email stored >180 days
with only a court order (not a warrant) was held unconstitutional as applied — a
warrant is required for email content regardless of how long it has been stored.

## Rules Distilled

1. **Fourth Amendment protection for stored email:** Subscribers have a reasonable
   expectation of privacy in email stored with ISPs — both content and metadata
2. **SCA 180-day rule unconstitutional for content:** §2703(b)'s lower threshold for
   accessing email stored >180 days (subpoena with notice) is unconstitutional for
   content; warrant required
3. **Third-party doctrine does not eliminate email privacy:** The fact that emails
   are stored with an ISP does not eliminate Fourth Amendment protection — ISPs are
   conduits, not recipients
4. **Warrant required:** Government must obtain search warrant supported by probable
   cause to access stored email content; court orders and subpoenas are insufficient
5. **Civil analogy:** The same expectation of privacy that requires a warrant for
   government access makes private unauthorized access (§2701) a serious violation

## Why Warshak Matters for Civil SCA Claims

Warshak is a constitutional case (Fourth Amendment / government access), not a civil
§2707 case. But it matters for civil SIM swap claims because:

**Calibrates the injury:** Warshak establishes that stored email deserves the same
Fourth Amendment protection as sealed mail. When a SIM swap attacker accesses Michael's
stored emails, they are committing an intrusion with constitutional dimensions — not
merely accessing data files. This supports punitive damages arguments in §2707 civil cases.

**Damages calibration:** For §2707 civil cases, punitive damages are appropriate in
cases involving "knowing or intentional" violation. Warshak's holding that email is
protected at the highest constitutional level reinforces the argument that intentional
access to stored email through a SIM swap attack is precisely the kind of knowing,
intentional violation that warrants punitive damages.

**Anti-defense tool:** If a SCA defendant argues that stored emails on a server are not
"really" private because they were shared with an ISP, Warshak forecloses that argument.

## Sixth Circuit — Not Binding in Ninth Circuit

Warshak is a Sixth Circuit decision and is not binding precedent in the Ninth Circuit
(California). However, the DOJ adopted a national warrant policy for email as a result
of Warshak, making its reasoning broadly influential. The Ninth Circuit has not decided
the same Fourth Amendment question for email.

For civil SCA claims in California federal courts: Theofel (9th Cir. 2004) is the
binding Ninth Circuit precedent. Warshak provides persuasive authority for the
seriousness of stored communications privacy violations.

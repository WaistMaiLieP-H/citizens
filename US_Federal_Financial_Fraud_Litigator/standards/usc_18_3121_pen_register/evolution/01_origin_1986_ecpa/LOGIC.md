# Evolution Stage 01 — Origin 1986: ECPA Title III

### THE WOUND

Smith v. Maryland (1979) held that there is no Fourth Amendment protection for the
numbers dialed from a telephone — the subscriber "voluntarily" conveys this information
to the phone company by dialing. Law enforcement could therefore install pen registers
(devices recording outgoing call numbers) without a warrant.

The result: a comprehensive surveillance tool — who you call, when, and from where —
required no legal process. Congress filled this gap as part of ECPA in 1986.

### DESIGN RESPONSE — 1986

ECPA Title III (18 U.S.C. §§3121-3127) created:

**§3121:** Prohibition on installing or using a pen register or trap and trace device
without a court order or user consent. Criminal penalties for violations.

**§3122:** Procedure for government to apply for pen register authorization from any
federal or state court.

**§3123:** Court order requirements — the government must certify that the information
is relevant and material to an ongoing investigation; no probable cause required
(lower standard than a search warrant for content under Wiretap Act).

**No civil damages:** Congress consciously chose not to include a civil right of action
for pen register violations. The rationale: metadata has lower privacy expectation than
content (per Smith v. Maryland); criminal penalties are sufficient deterrent.

**The metadata/content distinction established:** ECPA created a three-tier protection
framework that treats metadata differently from content. Content (Wiretap Act/SCA)
gets strong protection; metadata (Pen Register Act) gets lighter protection.

### PROVENANCE

Electronic Communications Privacy Act of 1986, Pub. L. 99-508, Title III
18 U.S.C. §§3121-3127 (original enactment)
100 Stat. 1868 (Oct. 21, 1986)

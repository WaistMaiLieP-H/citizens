# Statute Version Cited — Smith v. Maryland

**Case decided:** 1979
**Pre-ECPA:** Smith was decided before the Pen Register Act (1986) existed. The case
prompted Congress to create statutory protection for pen register metadata.

## What Smith Decided

Smith addressed the Fourth Amendment — not a statute. It held the Constitution does
not protect dialed telephone numbers from government pen register surveillance. The
case created the constitutional gap that ECPA Title III (Pen Register Act, 1986)
was designed to fill at the statutory level.

## ECPA Title III vs. Smith

| Protection | Source | Standard | Civil Remedy |
|-----------|--------|----------|-------------|
| Pen register (metadata) | ECPA §§3121-3127 (1986) | Statutory (not constitutional) | Criminal only — no civil |
| Wiretap (content) | ECPA §§2510-2522 + 4th Amendment | Constitutional + statutory | §2520: $10K/violation civil |
| CSLI (location) | 4th Amendment (Carpenter 2018) | Constitutional | Criminal — no ECPA civil for location |

## Carpenter v. United States (2018) — Limitation

Carpenter, 585 U.S. 296 (2018) held that long-term historical CSLI (cell tower location
data) requires a warrant — narrowing Smith's third-party doctrine for comprehensive
surveillance records. The logic: comprehensive location tracking over time creates a
"detailed chronicle" that deserves constitutional protection even if Smith limits
protection for individual call numbers.

Post-Carpenter, courts are divided on whether comprehensive metadata (not just location)
similarly deserves heightened protection. This is an evolving area.

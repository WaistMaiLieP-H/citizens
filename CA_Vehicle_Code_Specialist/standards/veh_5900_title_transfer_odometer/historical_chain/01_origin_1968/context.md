# Historical Chain — § 5900 and Federal Odometer Law

## State Title Transfer (Pre-Federal Odometer Law)

California's 5-day DMV notification requirement for vehicle transfers predates the federal odometer statute. The state requirement was part of the early vehicle registration system — creating a contemporaneous official record of ownership changes that could be used to trace stolen vehicles and resolve title disputes.

## Federal Motor Vehicle Information and Cost Savings Act (1972)

Congress enacted the federal odometer disclosure law in 1972 (Pub. L. 92-513), finding that odometer tampering was a pervasive national problem affecting interstate commerce in used vehicles. Key findings:
- 10% of vehicles sold in the US annually had tampered odometers (1972 estimate)
- Consumers collectively paid hundreds of millions of dollars in excess value for high-mileage vehicles misrepresented as low-mileage
- State laws alone were insufficient to address a fraud pattern conducted through interstate vehicle transfers

The law created mandatory odometer disclosure on title transfer, criminal penalties for tampering, and a PRIVATE RIGHT OF ACTION with 3x damages. The private right of action was specifically designed to incentivize consumer enforcement — federal agencies lacked resources to pursue individual dealer fraud.

## California Implementation

VEH §5900(b) cross-references 49 USC §32705 explicitly, confirming California's intent to implement the federal disclosure requirement at the state level. The 5-day notification timeline and the DMV form requirement ensure the federal disclosure obligation is fulfilled simultaneously with state title transfer notification.

## Current State

49 USC §§32701-32711 (as recodified in 1994 from the original 1972 statute) remains the federal odometer fraud framework. The private civil action under 49 USC §32710 is a common supplement to state vehicle fraud claims because of its favorable damages multiplier (3x or $10,000 minimum).

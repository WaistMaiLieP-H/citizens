# Reasoning — Why § 5900 Exists

## The Title Chain Problem

Vehicle fraud historically exploited gaps in ownership documentation. Before mandatory title transfer notification, a seller could sell the same vehicle to multiple buyers, sell vehicles without clear title (e.g., vehicles subject to liens), or sell stolen vehicles by simply handing over keys without any title documentation.

The 5-day DMV notification requirement creates a state record of every vehicle transfer within 5 days of the transaction. This serves multiple purposes:
- Creates a break point that identifies the last known owner at time of transfer
- Enables lien holders to detect unauthorized sales of encumbered vehicles
- Creates the audit trail that enables detection of title washing and odometer fraud

## The Odometer Problem

Odometer rollback fraud is among the most profitable and pervasive forms of vehicle fraud. A vehicle with 120,000 miles that has its odometer rolled back to 50,000 miles can be sold for $3,000–$8,000 more than its true market value. The buyer pays the difference, and unless they specifically investigate, they will never know.

The federal odometer disclosure requirement (Motor Vehicle Information and Cost Savings Act, now 49 USC §§32701-32711) was enacted in 1972 specifically to address this systemic fraud. California §5900(b) implements the federal requirement at the state level. The combined federal (private civil action with 3x damages) and state (DMV complaint, criminal referral) tracks create overlapping deterrence.

## The "Intent to Defraud" Threshold

§5900(b) is careful: inadvertent odometer mileage errors are not violations. Only intentional misrepresentation triggers liability. This is consistent with the federal standard (49 USC §32703 requires willfulness). The threshold matters in practice: a stolen vehicle with a rolled-back or replaced odometer demonstrates the intent — the alteration itself is evidence of purpose.

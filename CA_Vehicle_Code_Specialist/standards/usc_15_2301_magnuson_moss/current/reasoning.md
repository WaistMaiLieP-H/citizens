# Reasoning — Why Magnuson-Moss Exists

Magnuson-Moss (1975) was the federal response to a 1960s–1970s pattern that California had already partially addressed with Song-Beverly (1970): consumer warranties that were unreadable, unenforceable, and routinely disclaimed.

**The specific federal losses the statute targeted:**

1. **"Warranties" that granted nothing.** Pre-1975 "warranties" often disclaimed the UCC's implied warranty of merchantability in the same paragraph that offered a "limited repair" warranty. The consumer got nothing that wasn't already implied — and lost the implied warranty too.
2. **Unreadable legalese.** Warranty cards used UCC shorthand unintelligible to consumers. FTC hearings 1969–1974 documented consumer confusion at 80%+ on basic warranty terms.
3. **No federal private right of action.** State UCC remedies varied; some states required privity; attorney's fees were not available; amount-in-controversy barriers kept small cases out of federal court even where diversity existed.

**How Magnuson-Moss fixes each:**

1. **§ 2308 — implied warranty lock.** If you offer ANY written warranty, you CANNOT disclaim implied warranties. This is the single most important provision of the statute. It reverses the UCC default.
2. **§ 2302 + 16 CFR Part 701 — disclosure rules.** Warranty must be in simple, readily understood language; designated "full" or "limited" on its face; available pre-sale for the consumer to read. Part 702 requires the seller to make warranty text available before the sale.
3. **§ 2310(d) — federal private right of action + attorney's fees.** Creates a federal cause of action for breach of written or implied warranty, with fees to prevailing consumer.

**Why Magnuson-Moss matters in the RedJag case:**

1. If the 2018 Jaguar XE came with **any** written warranty (manufacturer, extended, certified pre-owned), Magnuson-Moss § 2308 **bars disclaimer of implied warranty of merchantability**. A stripped/stolen vehicle is not merchantable. Claim survives as a matter of federal law regardless of the written warranty's text.
2. § 2310(d) gives access to **federal court** if amount in controversy exceeds $50,000. Combined damages (purchase price + $10K down + $19,985 asserted debt + consequential damages) likely exceed that threshold, giving the consumer a forum choice.
3. § 2310(d)(2) attorney's fees are **additive** to Song-Beverly § 1794(d) fees — same underlying time, multiple fee bases.
4. **Pleads stack:** Magnuson-Moss (federal) + Song-Beverly (state warranty) + Rees-Levering (state financing) + Veh Code § 11713 (dealer prohibited acts) + Veh Code § 5900 (title/odometer) + BPC § 9884.9 (repair act). Each statute has independent elements and independent remedies.

**What Magnuson-Moss DOES NOT do:**
- Does not create a warranty where none was given.
- Does not preempt state law (§ 2311).
- Does not provide the Song-Beverly 2x civil penalty (state penalty is state-only).
- Does not invalidate mandatory informal dispute resolution if compliant with 16 CFR 703 — though outcomes are nonbinding.

**Routing note:** Magnuson-Moss is typically pleaded alongside Song-Beverly. Consumer_Protection may co-own the pleading; Vehicle_Code_Specialist owns the vehicle-specific interpretation and dealer-conduct layer.

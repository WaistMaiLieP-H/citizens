# Historical Chain — Automotive Repair Act (1971)

## Pre-1971 Landscape

California automotive repair regulation before 1971 was minimal. General business licensing applied, but no specific pre-repair estimate requirement existed. Consumer complaints about repair fraud were common — phantom repairs, parts substitution, and inflated charges were documented in state consumer affairs investigations throughout the 1960s.

The California Legislature enacted the Automotive Repair Act (Stats. 1971, ch. 1647) as part of a broader consumer protection reform wave in the early 1970s that included CLRA (1970), the Business and Professions Code dealer registration system, and enforcement expansions in the Department of Consumer Affairs.

## The Written Estimate as Structural Reform

The written estimate requirement (§9884.9) was the central innovation of the 1971 Act. Before the estimate requirement:
- Repair dealers quoted verbal prices and then changed them without recourse
- Customers had no documentation of what was authorized
- Parts substitution was invisible — the customer received no document listing what was installed

The written estimate, signed by the customer before work begins, shifted the information balance. The customer now has a document showing what was authorized and at what price. Any deviation requires specific consent. The work order must record the odometer reading — creating an audit trail that detects mileage manipulation during the repair process.

## Current State

BPC §§9880–9884.20 constitute the Automotive Repair Act. BAR (Bureau of Automotive Repair) is the primary enforcement agency within the California Department of Consumer Affairs. BAR investigates over 40,000 consumer complaints annually and has enforcement authority over approximately 50,000 registered automotive repair dealers statewide. The Act has been amended multiple times since 1971 to address emerging fraud patterns; the core estimate and disclosure requirements remain unchanged.

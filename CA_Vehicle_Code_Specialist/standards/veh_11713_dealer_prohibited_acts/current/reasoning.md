# Reasoning — Why § 11713 Exists

## The Specificity Problem

General fraud law requires proving intent in most cases. Dealer fraud in vehicle transactions is notoriously difficult to prove under general fraud standards because dealers use complex financing structures, multiple intermediaries, and document volume to obscure individual misrepresentations.

Section 11713 solves this by enumerating specific acts that are prohibited regardless of intent. A dealer who provides an inaccurate vehicle history (§11713(u)) does not need to have specifically intended to deceive — the act itself is prohibited. This shifts the burden: the dealer must demonstrate the accurate history was provided, not that the inaccuracy was accidental.

## The CARFAX Fraud Pattern

Vehicle history misrepresentation emerged as a major consumer fraud vector as third-party vehicle history reporting services (CARFAX, AutoCheck) became standard in used vehicle sales. A dealer who substitutes a clean CARFAX from a different vehicle — or who fails to disclose theft history, salvage titles, or odometer rollback — can extract thousands more from the buyer than the vehicle's true value warrants.

§11713(u) directly addresses this: the prior ownership and use history must be represented accurately. If a CARFAX is provided, it must be for the actual vehicle. The Vehicle Code does not permit "close enough" history representations.

## Connection to Civil Remedies

Every §11713 violation is also:
- An "unlawful" act under UCL §17200 (which allows private civil actions and injunctive relief)
- Potentially a CLRA violation if the vehicle is consumer goods
- A basis for the Consumers Legal Remedies Act statutory damages ($1,000 minimum per violation)
The enumeration in §11713 does the heavy lifting: once the prohibited act is proven, the UCL/CLRA claims follow automatically under the "unlawful" prong.

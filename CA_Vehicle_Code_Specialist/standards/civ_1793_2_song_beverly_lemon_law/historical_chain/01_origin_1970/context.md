# Historical Loss — Song-Beverly 1970 Origin

**Enacting act:** Stats. 1970, Ch. 1333 (AB 401, Assemblymember Earle Crandall). Became effective March 4, 1971.

**The specific loss this statute was written to prevent:**

Pre-1970 California law allowed manufacturers of consumer goods — including motor vehicles — to:
- Disclaim the implied warranty of merchantability in fine print;
- Limit the buyer's remedy to "repair or replacement at the manufacturer's option" with no time or attempt ceiling;
- Channel all disputes into manufacturer-controlled arbitration programs;
- Shift the cost of transportation to the service center, loss-of-use, and rental vehicles onto the buyer.

The practical effect was that a California family who purchased a new vehicle with a recurring defect had no economically rational path to relief. Litigation costs exceeded the vehicle's value; the UCC's implied warranties were routinely disclaimed; and the Magnuson-Moss Act did not yet exist (federal Magnuson-Moss came in 1975 — see `usc_15_2301_magnuson_moss`).

**The losses documented in the 1970 legislative record** (AB 401 committee files, Assembly Judiciary Committee hearings, February 1970):
- Repeated-repair vehicles that consumers could not unload (later called "lemons");
- Disclaimed implied warranties leaving buyers with no remedy at all for merchantability failures;
- Manufacturer-captive dispute resolution with no independent review;
- Attorney's fees regime that made private enforcement uneconomical for ordinary consumers.

**The 1982 Tanner amendment (Stats. 1982, Ch. 388, AB 1787, Assemblymember Sally Tanner)** added the objective presumption at § 1793.22(b) — converting "reasonable number of attempts" from a jury-determined open question into a rebuttable presumption triggered by counted events. This is the "lemon law" in its popularly understood form.

**Why this history is load-bearing for the RedJag case:**
The 1970 and 1982 legislative records both identify **dealer and manufacturer knowledge of defect at delivery** as an aggravating factor justifying the 2x civil penalty under § 1794(c). A delivered-with-wrong-CARFAX vehicle is the paradigm case the statute was designed to reach — a defect known to the seller, unknown to the buyer, and structurally unfixable because the "fix" would require undoing the sale itself.

# Reasoning — Why § 11700 Exists

## The Unlicensed Dealer Problem

Before mandatory dealer licensing, vehicle sales could be conducted by anyone — private individuals, transient sellers, and criminal enterprises alike. The consumer had no way to distinguish a legitimate, bonded, regulated dealer from a curbstoner (unlicensed dealer operating from a parking lot or private address). Vehicles were sold without title assurance, without DMV notification, and without the consumer protections that licensed dealers are required to provide.

The licensing requirement creates a minimum compliance threshold: to hold a dealer license, a person must post a bond (financial security for consumer claims), maintain an established place of business, and register with DMV. This bond and registration requirement is the foundation of consumer protection in vehicle transactions — it gives defrauded consumers a recoverable fund and a registered entity to sue.

## The Bond Requirement

The §11700 licensing requirement links to the dealer surety bond requirement (VEH §11710). A licensed dealer must maintain a $50,000 bond (for new vehicles) or $10,000 bond (for used vehicles). When a dealer commits fraud, the consumer can file a claim against the bond — this is the direct financial remedy for victims of dealer fraud in California.

An unlicensed dealer has no bond. The consumer's protection disappears entirely if the seller is operating without a license.

## Connection to RedJag

If the RedJag transaction was conducted by an unlicensed entity, or if the licensed dealer's status was compromised at the time of the transaction, the bond claim mechanism and DMV complaint pathway may be available even outside of civil litigation.

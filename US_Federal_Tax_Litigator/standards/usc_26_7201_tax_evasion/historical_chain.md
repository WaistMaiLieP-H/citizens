# Historical Chain — 26 U.S.C. § 7201

## The wound

From the Whiskey Rebellion (1791) through Prohibition (1920–33), the federal government repeatedly proved that a tax on disfavored activity is paid by the obedient and evaded by the organized. By 1913, ratification of the Sixteenth Amendment gave Congress power to lay an income tax without apportionment among the states, and within months the Revenue Act of 1913 reintroduced a permanent federal income tax for the first time since the Civil War. Congress knew immediately that an income tax would face two problems a tariff had never faced: it would require self-reporting, and it would be opposed by the wealthiest Americans not only politically but through concealment, structuring, and outright lying on the return.

The Civil War income tax (1861–1872) had already taught the lesson — compliance without a credible prosecution threat decays within a decade. When Congress drafted the 1913 Act, it included a criminal provision at § II(F) that made *willful attempt to evade or defeat* the income tax a misdemeanor. Within two decades, it was clear misdemeanor stakes were insufficient. The Revenue Act of 1939 elevated willful evasion to a felony. In the 1954 recodification, the provision was renumbered as § 7201 of the Internal Revenue Code — the number it still carries today. The statute has not been meaningfully amended since 1954; only the penalty figures have moved (most recently via 18 U.S.C. § 3571, which overrides the face-value fines).

The wound § 7201 answered is older than the statute itself: the ancient problem of a common wealth dependent on voluntary tribute that the powerful refuse to render. Every time an aristocracy has faced a general tax, it has built structures to avoid it, and every time a republic has let those structures stand unchallenged, the republic has hollowed out. Section 7201 is the United States' answer to that pattern — a felony statute, carrying prison, with specific-intent scienter, applicable to any taxpayer of any station who takes an affirmative step to keep the government from seeing what they owe.

## The promise

In plain terms, § 7201 promises this: *if you have income, and you know you have income, and you take a step — any step — to hide that income or to defeat the tax on it, the United States will treat that step as a felony and will send you to prison.*

This promise has always been aimed particularly at sophisticated taxpayers. The Spies doctrine — requiring an affirmative act, not just failure to file — was a recognition that the most serious evasion is not the laborer who does not file, but the operator who files a return that lies, or who keeps two sets of books, or who routes income through nominees, or who conceals assets from collection. The affirmative-act requirement channels prosecutorial attention toward sophistication, planning, and deception, which is where the larger losses to the fisc occur and where the ordinary enforcement tools (correspondence examination, civil audit) break down.

The Cheek doctrine — that good-faith belief negates willfulness — gives the statute a human edge. It says: we will not send you to prison merely because you were wrong about the law; but we will send you to prison if you knew what the law required and chose to defeat it. This is the scienter that separates §7201 (felony) from §6663 (civil fraud penalty) from §6662 (accuracy penalty).

## Why this Citizen carries § 7201 as its first standard

Section 7201 is the foundational federal criminal tax statute. Every other criminal tax provision is either a lesser-included variant (§7203 omission), a return-specific subset (§7206 false return), a collection-specific variant (§7202 trust-fund), or an interference variant (§7212). The rest of Title 26 Chapter 75 organizes around it. A Citizen that does not carry § 7201 deeply cannot carry any of the others correctly, because the doctrine of affirmative act, willfulness, method of proof, and badges of fraud is the doctrine of § 7201 first and only secondarily of its neighbors.

## Closing

Section 7201 is not a rule you follow. It is a rule that follows *you*, once you take an affirmative step the government can see. The standard is old, the doctrine is stable, the penalties are real, and the scienter requirement is demanding. The wound it answers is the oldest in tax law — that people with resources build structures to keep resources. The promise it makes is that the federal government will, when the step is affirmative and the intent is knowing, prosecute regardless of the taxpayer's station.

For the steward's file, § 7201 is load-bearing. Several of the vectors being investigated — identity-based attribution, real-estate reporting integrity, transferable-credit §1001 gain — live or die on the willfulness and affirmative-act elements this standard carries.

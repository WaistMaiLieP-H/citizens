# 26 U.S.C. § 7201 — Attempt to evade or defeat tax

## Operative text (substantive content, subject to steward verbatim verification)

> Any person who willfully attempts in any manner to evade or defeat any tax imposed by this title or the payment thereof shall, in addition to other penalties provided by law, be guilty of a felony and, upon conviction thereof, shall be fined not more than $100,000 ($500,000 in the case of a corporation), or imprisoned not more than 5 years, or both, together with the costs of prosecution.

**Inflation-adjusted fine (18 U.S.C. § 3571 override):** $250,000 individual / $500,000 organization. The statutory figure in §7201 itself reads $100,000 / $500,000; courts apply 18 U.S.C. § 3571 in practice.

## Elements (Sansone v. United States, 380 U.S. 343, 351 (1965))

1. **Existence of a tax deficiency** — an additional tax owed.
2. **An affirmative act** constituting an attempt to evade or defeat tax.
3. **Willfulness** — voluntary, intentional violation of a known legal duty (*Cheek v. United States*, 498 U.S. 192 (1991)).

## Affirmative-act requirement — *Spies v. United States*, 317 U.S. 492 (1943)

The statute requires some affirmative commission, not mere omission. Spies listed non-exhaustive examples:
- Keeping a double set of books
- Making false entries or alterations
- Making false invoices or documents
- Destruction of books or records
- Concealment of assets or covering up sources of income
- Handling of one's affairs to avoid making the records usual in transactions of the kind
- Any conduct, the likely effect of which would be to mislead or to conceal

The affirmative-act element distinguishes §7201 (felony evasion) from §7203 (willful failure to file, misdemeanor omission).

## Willfulness — *Cheek v. United States*, 498 U.S. 192 (1991)

Willfulness in the tax crimes context is "the voluntary, intentional violation of a known legal duty." A good-faith belief that one is not violating the tax law — however irrational — negates willfulness. *But* a belief that the tax law is unconstitutional, as distinguished from a belief that one owes no tax, does not negate willfulness because it reflects knowledge of the legal duty rather than ignorance of it.

## Statute of limitations

Six years (26 U.S.C. §6531(2)).

## Civil parallel

26 U.S.C. §6663 — 75% civil fraud penalty. Clear-and-convincing burden on government. Collateral estoppel applies from §7201 conviction (*Tomlinson v. Lefkowitz*, 334 F.2d 262 (5th Cir. 1964)).

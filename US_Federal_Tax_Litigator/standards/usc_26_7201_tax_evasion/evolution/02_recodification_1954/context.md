# Recodification — Internal Revenue Code of 1954, § 7201

**Enacted:** August 16, 1954 (Pub. L. 83-591, 68A Stat. 3)
**Section:** 7201, titled "Attempt to evade or defeat tax"
**Predecessor:** I.R.C. of 1939 § 145(b) (felony evasion); § 3793 (fraud and false statements)
**Grade:** Felony
**Penalty (as enacted 1954):** Fine up to $10,000, imprisonment up to five years, or both, together with costs of prosecution
**Current statutory face text:** Fine up to $100,000 individual / $500,000 corporation (raised by Tax Equity and Fiscal Responsibility Act of 1982, Pub. L. 97-248, §329(a))
**Effective fine via 18 U.S.C. § 3571 (inflation):** $250,000 individual / $500,000 organization

## What the 1954 recodification did

The 1954 Code consolidated the criminal tax provisions into Chapter 75 and renumbered the 1939 Code's § 145(b) (felony evasion) as § 7201, § 145(a) (failure to file) as § 7203, and § 3793 (fraud / false statements) as § 7206. The language of § 7201 as it emerged in 1954 is substantially the text that appears today:

> Any person who willfully attempts in any manner to evade or defeat any tax imposed by this title or the payment thereof shall, in addition to other penalties provided by law, be guilty of a felony and, upon conviction thereof, shall be fined not more than $10,000, or imprisoned not more than 5 years, or both, together with the costs of prosecution.

The 1954 version cleaned up and generalized the 1939 version's narrower framing. Key drafting choices:

- "Attempts in any manner" — deliberately broad; reaches affirmative conduct beyond return-making. This phrase is what allows Spies-listed affirmative acts (double books, concealment of assets, cash handling without usual records) to qualify.
- "Any tax imposed by this title" — reaches every federal tax in Title 26, not just income tax. Excise, estate, gift, employment — all are covered.
- "Or the payment thereof" — separates *assessment* evasion from *payment* evasion. An acknowledged liability that is then defeated through concealment of assets is within § 7201 even if the return itself was truthful.
- "Together with the costs of prosecution" — a holdover from 1913, preserved deliberately to mark §7201 as a fiscal-sovereignty offense, not just an ordinary crime.

## Why 1954 matters doctrinally

The Supreme Court's foundational § 7201 cases all issue *against* the 1954 text:

- *Spies v. United States*, 317 U.S. 492 (1943) — decided under § 145(b) of the 1939 Code, but its affirmative-act doctrine carried into § 7201 interpretation without modification because the "attempts in any manner" language in 1954 preserved the same conceptual frame.
- *Sansone v. United States*, 380 U.S. 343 (1965) — decided under the 1954 § 7201 and crystallized the three-element test (tax due, affirmative act, willfulness).
- *Cheek v. United States*, 498 U.S. 192 (1991) — decided under 1954 § 7201 and § 7203; the willfulness doctrine articulated applies identically to both.

Because the 1954 text has not been amended in substance since 1954, the case law mapping is clean: every §7201 case from 1954 forward is reading the same words. This is unusual among federal criminal statutes and makes § 7201 doctrine unusually stable.

## What changed in 1954 versus 1939

- § 145(b) "wilful attempt in any manner to evade or defeat any tax imposed by this chapter, or the payment thereof" → §7201 "willfully attempts in any manner to evade or defeat any tax imposed by this title or the payment thereof"
  - "this chapter" → "this title" — broader; reaches all of Title 26
  - "wilful" → "willfully" — stylistic / modernization
- Penalty raised from $10,000 / 5 years to $10,000 / 5 years (no change in 1954; later raised)
- Structure moved to dedicated criminal chapter (Chapter 75) with related provisions (§§7202–7217)

## What did not change

- Felony grade
- Scienter: willful
- "Attempts in any manner" — the affirmative-act language
- Six-year SOL (§6531(2), enacted 1954 alongside §7201)
- Civil parallel — the civil fraud penalty then at §6653(b), later moved to §6663 in the 1989 Revenue Reconciliation Act

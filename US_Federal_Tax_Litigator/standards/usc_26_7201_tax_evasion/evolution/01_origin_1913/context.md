# Origin — Revenue Act of 1913, § II(F)

**Enacted:** October 3, 1913 (Pub. L. 63-16, 38 Stat. 114)
**Constitutional basis:** Sixteenth Amendment, ratified February 3, 1913
**Charging posture:** Misdemeanor; fine up to $2,000 or imprisonment up to one year, or both
**Text (original):**
> Any person or any officer of any corporation required by law to make, render, sign, or verify any return, who makes any false or fraudulent return or statement with intent to defeat or evade the assessment required by this section to be made, shall be guilty of a misdemeanor, and shall be fined not exceeding two thousand dollars or be imprisoned not exceeding one year, or both, at the discretion of the court, with the costs of prosecution.

## Context — the wound answered at enactment

The Sixteenth Amendment removed the *Pollock v. Farmers' Loan & Trust Co.*, 157 U.S. 429 (1895) barrier to an unapportioned federal income tax. Congress moved within eight months of ratification to enact a permanent income tax via the Revenue Act of 1913. But Congress remembered two prior lessons:

1. The Civil War income tax (Revenue Act of 1861, extended and revised through 1872) had failed in compliance terms within a decade; the Joint Select Committee on Retrenchment in 1869 found that wealthier taxpayers systematically under-reported and that enforcement was political and inconsistent. The tax was allowed to lapse in 1872.

2. The 1894 income tax (Wilson-Gorman Tariff Act) was struck down in *Pollock* before enforcement patterns could develop, but its legislative record showed Congress anticipated that the wealthy would resist through concealment.

The 1913 drafters therefore included a criminal provision from day one. § II(F) was that provision. It targeted the two most visible problems in Civil-War-era enforcement: false returns and fraudulent statements made in support of returns. The misdemeanor grade reflected a political compromise — the new income tax affected only the top roughly 1% of earners (exemption of $3,000 single, $4,000 married; maximum rate 7% on income over $500,000), and Congress was reluctant to make the first enforcement provision against the wealthiest Americans a felony.

## Why the misdemeanor posture failed

Within a decade, the limitations of the misdemeanor stakes were visible:
- Prosecutors needed aggressive charging authority to reach complex evasion patterns
- Sophisticated taxpayers could structure around the narrow "false return" language
- Omission (failure to file) was not reached — the statute required an affirmative false statement
- The $2,000 fine became trivial as tax stakes rose through and after World War I

By the 1924 revenue act, Congress had already broadened the statutory scope; by 1939, it had elevated willful evasion to a felony; by 1954 the modern § 7201 had crystallized.

## What from the 1913 version carries forward

- The scienter requirement ("with intent to defeat or evade") — later reframed as "willfully attempts in any manner to evade or defeat"
- The separation of *assessment* from *payment* (both are protected; the 1913 version focused on assessment, the modern version covers both)
- The pairing of fine and imprisonment
- The "costs of prosecution" clause
- The self-reporting premise that makes any criminal tax statute intelligible — the taxpayer has a duty to disclose, and the statute criminalizes defeat of that duty

## What changed

- "Misdemeanor" → "felony" (1939)
- "False or fraudulent return or statement" → "attempt in any manner to evade or defeat" (1939 / 1954, broader and reaches affirmative conduct beyond return-making)
- $2,000 fine → $100,000 (current statutory) / $250,000 (inflation-adjusted via 18 U.S.C. § 3571)
- One-year imprisonment → five years
- SOL six years (§6531(2)), carried forward into modern practice

## The 1965 Evidence Code

The California Evidence Code was enacted in 1965 (Stats. 1965, ch. 299) as a comprehensive codification of California evidence law. It replaced the scattered common law rules and statutory provisions (primarily in the Code of Civil Procedure) that had governed evidence since 1872.

The drafters — led by the California Law Revision Commission — specifically intended §1402 to codify the common law burden regarding altered documents. The Commission's comments confirm: §1402 is a direct codification of the common law rule placing the burden on the proponent of an altered document.

## The Common Law Predecessor

Before 1965, the altered-document rule in California existed as common law, derived from English chancery (equity) court practice. English equity courts had applied the burden to the producing party since at least the 17th century, primarily in land title disputes where deed alteration was a common fraud mechanism.

California courts applied the common law rule in pre-1965 cases. The most important pre-codification California altered-document rule:

- *Blackwood v. Blackwood* — California courts refused to admit deeds that appeared altered without explanation from the party producing them.
- General common law principle: A writing altered in a material part loses its legal efficacy unless the alteration is accounted for.

## Why "Appears to Have Been Altered" Was the Right Standard

The CLRC drafters chose the appearance-based trigger deliberately. The alternative — requiring actual proof of alteration before shifting the burden — would have been unworkable. Forensic document examination was not a widely available discipline in 1965. Parties would have had to hire experts and conduct testing before the burden could even arise. The appearance-based standard allows a lay trier of fact or the court to make the threshold determination without expert testimony.

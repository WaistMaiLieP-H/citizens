## Why This Standard Exists

EVID §1402 exists because paper lies — and the lie is usually in the alteration.

Before evidence law developed authentication and altered-document standards, courts admitted documents at face value. A deed was a deed. A contract was a contract. The party who held the paper controlled the story. This created an obvious exploitation opportunity: alter a document after execution to change what it says, then produce it as the genuine original.

The common law responded by placing the burden on the party who produces a document that appears altered. The rationale is simple:

1. **The producing party is the gatekeeper.** If you chose to produce a document, you are responsible for explaining it. You had access to its history. You controlled the chain of custody before production. If the document looks wrong, you answer for it.

2. **Appearance matters because truth is not always provable.** Requiring proof of actual alteration before shifting the burden would put the opposing party in an impossible position — they would have to prove the document was forged without access to the original ink, the execution room, or the paper's chain of custody. §1402 shifts the burden at the moment the appearance of alteration arises, precisely when the burden should shift.

3. **The stakes are too high to guess.** A fraudulently altered document, if admitted, becomes a foundation for findings of fact that can be as durable as truth. The exclusionary rule for unaccounted alterations prevents courts from building factual records on potentially falsified foundations.

## The Historical Chain: From Common Law to EVID §1402

California's Evidence Code was enacted in 1965 as a codification and reform of the common law of evidence. The Evidence Code drafters (led by the California Law Revision Commission) codified the altered-document burden that had existed as common law for centuries.

The common law rule — traceable to English chancery courts — was that a party seeking to rely on an altered document bore the burden of explaining the alteration. English courts applied this rule to deeds (the most valuable documents of the feudal era — land title documents were the mechanism of wealth transfer). An altered deed that could not be explained was void as evidence.

California adopted this rule in its pre-1965 code of civil procedure and evidence statutes. The 1965 Evidence Code reformulated it as EVID §1402, making explicit what had been implicit in common law: the trigger is the APPEARANCE of alteration, not proof of it.

---

## What Is Lost Without This Rule

Without §1402's burden allocation, a sophisticated fraudster could:
1. Alter a document after execution
2. Produce it as genuine
3. Sit back and make the opposing party prove the forgery

The opposing party, who was not present at the alleged signing, who does not have access to the original execution history, and who may not have forensic handwriting expertise, cannot bear this burden effectively. The result would be that document fraud succeeds whenever the forger is more organized than the victim.

**The specific loss in this case record:** The 16-year family law forensic audit reflects exactly this pattern. Documents were created, altered, or selectively withheld across court proceedings spanning 2009-2025. If §1402 had been invoked at each proceeding where altered or suspicious documents were produced, the burden would have shifted to the producing party at each instance. The failure to invoke §1402 systematically allowed a pattern of document manipulation to accumulate without challenge.

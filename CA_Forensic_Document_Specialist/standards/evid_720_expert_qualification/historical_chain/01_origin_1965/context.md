# Historical Chain — Origin: Expert Qualification Standard (1965)

**Standard:** evid_720_expert_qualification
**Era:** 1965 — California Evidence Code enactment

---

## Common Law Foundation — The Expert Testimony Problem

Before the codification of the California Evidence Code, expert testimony was governed by common law rules that had developed unevenly across jurisdictions. The core common law principle — that a witness must have special knowledge beyond common experience to testify as an expert — was settled. What was unsettled was how that knowledge was to be demonstrated and what standard of review courts would apply.

California courts prior to 1965 generally applied a permissive standard: any person with demonstrated expertise in a relevant field could be qualified. The qualification was often shown by a brief recitation of credentials on direct examination, and challenges were handled as credibility issues for the jury rather than threshold gatekeeping issues for the court.

## The 1965 Evidence Code — Codification of the Permissive Standard

When California enacted the Evidence Code (Stats. 1965, ch. 299), the legislature codified the existing common law approach to expert qualification while clarifying three points that the common law had left ambiguous:

1. **The five disjunctive pathways:** By listing knowledge, skill, experience, training, and education as alternative qualifiers, the code made explicit that credential (education/training) was not the exclusive route. Experience alone could qualify.

2. **Subject specificity:** The qualification must be "sufficient to qualify him as an expert on the subject to which his testimony relates." The codification tied qualification to the specific subject, not to a general field.

3. **Qualification by own testimony:** §720(b) clarified that a witness could establish qualification through their own testimony — no external credential documentation was required.

## The Adversarial Mechanism — Objection Required

§720(a) provides that qualification must be shown "against the objection of a party." This means the proponent is not required to run through a full qualification recitation unless the opposing party objects. If no objection is raised, the expert may proceed. This is consistent with California's general rule that qualification objections are waived if not made timely.

The practical effect: trial counsel who does not voir dire the expert at the qualification stage, and does not object, has waived the challenge. This creates a tactical incentive to challenge qualification early rather than waiting to attack it as a credibility issue during cross-examination.

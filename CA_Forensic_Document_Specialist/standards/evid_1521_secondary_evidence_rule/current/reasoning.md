# EVID § 1521 Secondary Evidence Rule — Reasoning and Historical Loss Analysis

## Why This Standard Exists — The Reform from Best Evidence

The common law "best evidence rule" required production of the original writing to prove its contents. Copies were inadmissible unless the original was unavailable for reasons outside the proponent's control. The rule developed in an era before photocopiers, when copies were made by hand and copying errors were common. The original was the most reliable evidence of its own content.

By the 20th century, the best evidence rule was an anachronism. Photocopiers, then scanners, then digital records made copies indistinguishable from originals. The premise of the rule — that copies are inherently less reliable — no longer held. More importantly, the best evidence rule was being weaponized: parties could suppress originals or claim they were unavailable to prevent secondary evidence from coming in.

California abolished the best evidence rule and replaced it with §1521 — the Secondary Evidence Rule. The shift was fundamental: secondary evidence is now the DEFAULT admissible; exclusion requires an affirmative finding.

---

## The Policy Shift

**Old rule (best evidence):** Original required → secondary evidence excluded unless exception.

**New rule (§1521):** Secondary evidence admissible → exclusion requires affirmative finding of genuine material dispute + injustice, OR unfairness.

This shift places the burden on the party seeking to EXCLUDE secondary evidence, not on the party seeking to admit it. The exclusion grounds (genuine material dispute + justice requires; or unfairness) are narrow by design — the legislature intended §1521 to make secondary evidence broadly accessible.

---

## The Historical Loss — What the Best Evidence Rule Cost

The best evidence rule, in practice, harmed parties whose originals were controlled or destroyed by the opposing party. If you are the victim of document fraud — your signed deed was replaced with a forgery, your police report was removed from the file — you may not have the original. The best evidence rule would then exclude your secondary evidence (your own copy, witness testimony about what you signed, the notary's log), while allowing the fraudulent "original" in evidence.

§1521 addresses this asymmetry by making secondary evidence admissible and placing the exclusion burden on the party who would benefit from keeping secondary evidence out. A party who destroyed the original cannot then invoke a rule that benefits from the original's absence.

---

## Application to the Case Record's Pattern

The pattern in this case record — missing reports, unsigned documents, contradictory correspondence — creates exactly the situation §1521 was designed for. The originals are either:
1. In the possession of the opposing parties (withheld)
2. Destroyed or suppressed
3. Never created (the document claimed never to have existed)
4. Altered and replaced with a modified version

§1521 allows the parties whose documents were suppressed or destroyed to use secondary evidence — certified copies, index entries, witnesses, transcripts — to prove what the originals contained. The exclusion grounds of §1521(a) are available to the opposing party, but they require an affirmative showing of genuine material dispute and injustice, or unfairness — which is difficult to establish when the party seeking exclusion was responsible for the original's absence.

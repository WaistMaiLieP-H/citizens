# Carleton v. Tortosa (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 745

**Citation:** Carleton v. Tortosa (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 745, 17 Cal.Rptr.2d 734
**Court:** California Court of Appeal, Third Appellate District
**Decided:** 1993
**Statutes construed:** Business & Professions Code § 10176 — broker duties; prohibited conduct; secret profit; undisclosed dual agency; fiduciary obligation

## Facts

A real estate broker represented a buyer in a transaction while having an undisclosed financial interest in the seller entity. The buyer alleged the broker concealed a material conflict of interest, failed to disclose the dual financial relationship, and collected compensation from both sides of the transaction without the buyer's informed written consent.

## Holding

The Court of Appeal addressed the scope of a broker's fiduciary duties and the consequences of BPC § 10176 violations:

1. **A real estate broker stands in a fiduciary relationship with the client.** The broker owes the highest duty of good faith, loyalty, and disclosure to the client. This is a common-law duty codified and amplified by BPC § 10176.

2. **Non-disclosure of a conflict of interest is per se actionable.** A broker who fails to disclose a financial interest on the other side of the transaction — or who collects compensation from both sides without informed written consent — violates both § 10176(d) (undisclosed dual agency) and § 10176(g) (secret profit).

3. **The client need not prove actual financial harm from the conflict to establish liability.** The fiduciary breach itself supports rescission and fee disgorgement as remedies, in addition to any actual damages.

4. **BPC § 10176 violations can support civil liability independent of contract claims.** A buyer may pursue tort damages for negligence and breach of fiduciary duty based on BPC § 10176 violations without needing to prove contractual privity in the traditional sense.

5. **Disgorgement of all compensation is an available remedy.** A broker who violates § 10176 by taking a secret profit forfeits all right to compensation for the transaction — not just the undisclosed portion.

## The doctrinal proposition

Carleton establishes the civil enforcement framework for § 10176 broker violations:

| Violation Type | § 10176 Subdivision | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Misrepresentation to principal | (a) | Rescission + damages |
| Deceptive material inducement | (b) | Same |
| Secret compensation | (g) | Disgorgement; license discipline |
| Commingling funds | (h) | Rescission + damages |
| Undisclosed dual agency | (d) | Full compensation forfeiture |

**Disgorgement without proof of harm:** A broker who violates the fiduciary duty by concealing a conflict need not have caused provable economic harm — the breach itself entitles the client to disgorgement and rescission.

## Application to the Honeysuckle matter

For the 2958 Honeysuckle transaction, the § 10176 / Carleton framework applies where:

1. **The listing broker or buyer's broker had undisclosed relationships** to the seller entity or to any parties who benefited from the transaction — the $465K equity disappearance and the $72K crypto diversion both warrant investigation of who benefited
2. **The 19 unsigned or defective documents** may indicate that the broker allowed the transaction to close without proper completion of required disclosures — a § 10176(a)/(b) violation
3. **Any secret compensation arrangement** between the broker and any related party (escrow, title, other participants) triggers § 10176(g) and Carleton's disgorgement remedy
4. **If the broker representing Christina received any benefit from the equity diversion**, that is a § 10176(g) violation and the broker's entire commission is forfeitable

## Authority status

Published California Court of Appeal decision (1993). Establishes civil enforcement framework for BPC § 10176 fiduciary violations, disgorgement remedy, and the principle that per se liability attaches without proof of harm for fiduciary breach. Applied in broker misconduct and real estate fraud litigation.

# Easton v. Strassburger (1984) 152 Cal.App.3d 90

**Citation:** Easton v. Strassburger (1984) 152 Cal.App.3d 90, 199 Cal.Rptr. 383
**Decided:** February 1984
**Court:** California Court of Appeal, First Appellate District
**Disposition:** Affirmed judgment for buyer; established the seller's broker's duty of reasonable inspection and disclosure to the buyer.
**Statutes construed:** Bus. & Prof. Code § 10176(a), (i); common-law broker fiduciary duty; later partially codified in Civ. Code § 2079

## Facts

Easton purchased a residential property in Contra Costa County. The seller's broker had represented the property without disclosing significant soil problems that were evident from a reasonable inspection. After closing, Easton discovered massive landslide damage requiring extensive repairs. Easton sued the seller's broker (not the buyer's agent, not the seller directly) for failing to disclose the soil issues that a reasonable broker would have discovered through visual inspection.

## Holding

The Court of Appeal held:

1. **The seller's broker owes a duty of reasonable visual inspection and disclosure to the buyer, even though the broker represents the seller.** This was a significant extension of the traditional rule that agents owe duties only to their principals. The court reasoned that the seller's broker is in the best position to identify visible defects and has a superior ability to protect the buyer from fraud by the seller.

2. **The duty is limited to visible defects that a reasonable broker would discover through an ordinary inspection.** The broker is not required to conduct expert engineering investigations, commission soil tests, or hire specialized inspectors. The duty is limited to what a reasonable real estate professional, through visual inspection, would notice.

3. **The duty exists even when the seller does not know of the defects.** The broker cannot defeat the duty by pointing out that the seller did not disclose the defects to the broker. The broker's duty is independent — it runs from the broker's own visual inspection to the buyer.

4. **Breach exposes the broker to liability for damages suffered by the buyer.** The remedy is compensatory damages (the cost of repair, diminution in value, or equivalent). Punitive damages are available if the broker's conduct meets the § 3294 fraud, oppression, or malice standards.

## Why this is load-bearing

*Easton v. Strassburger* is THE landmark California case on the seller's broker's duty to the buyer. Before *Easton*, the traditional rule (drawn from common-law agency doctrine) was that agents owe duties only to their principals. After *Easton*, the seller's broker in a residential real estate transaction has an independent duty of reasonable inspection and disclosure running to the buyer.

The case was so influential that the California Legislature partially codified its holding in Civ. Code § 2079 (enacted 1985), which codified the seller's broker's duty of reasonable visual inspection and disclosure to the buyer in residential sales. Section 2079's statutory framework has since been the primary locus of doctrine, but *Easton* remains foundational and is still cited.

## Relationship to BPC § 10176

Section 10176 and *Easton* work together:

- **§ 10176(a), (b), (i)** — disciplinary grounds for misrepresentation, false promises, and fraud
- *Easton* — creates the civil liability running from broker to buyer that makes § 10176's prohibited conduct categories enforceable through private actions

A broker who fails the § 10176 standard is both (a) subject to license discipline by the Real Estate Commissioner and (b) civilly liable to the buyer under *Easton* and its progeny (including Civ. Code § 2079).

## Relevance to the 2958 Honeysuckle transaction

For Honeysuckle, *Easton* establishes that the seller's broker (if any was involved in the 2023 sale) owed a duty of reasonable inspection and disclosure to the buyer. If the broker failed to inspect or failed to disclose visible defects, the broker is civilly liable to the buyer.

But *Easton* also supports the reverse claim in a less typical posture: if the steward (as seller) was harmed by broker conduct that violated § 10176 and § 2079, the steward may have claims against the broker as well. The analysis depends on the precise role and conduct of the broker(s) in the 2023 transaction.

The Honeysuckle complaint should identify the broker(s) involved in the 2023 sale and analyze their specific conduct under:
- § 10176 disciplinary categories
- *Easton* / § 2079 inspection-and-disclosure duty
- Common-law fiduciary duty to the principal (the seller in this case)
- TDS Section III disclosure obligations

## Authority status

Published, binding as persuasive appellate authority. Partially codified in Civ. Code § 2079. Cited as foundational authority in every California residential real estate broker liability case.

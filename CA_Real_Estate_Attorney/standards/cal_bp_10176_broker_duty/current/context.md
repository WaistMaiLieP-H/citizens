# BPC § 10176 — Real Estate Broker Prohibited Conduct

**Popular name:** Broker disciplinary statute; broker prohibited conduct; real estate licensee misconduct
**Location:** Business & Professions Code, Division 4 (Real Estate), Part 1 (Licensing of Persons), Chapter 3 (Real Estate Regulations), Article 3

## What § 10176 does

Section 10176 is the **disciplinary statute** for California real estate licensees. It enumerates 13 categories of prohibited conduct that can result in license suspension or revocation by the Real Estate Commissioner. It also functions as a civil liability framework because courts have held that § 10176 violations constitute evidence of breach of fiduciary duty and can support private rights of action (though the statute itself does not expressly create a private cause of action).

## The 13 categories

**(a) Substantial misrepresentation** — any material false statement
**(b) False promises** likely to influence, persuade, or induce
**(c) Continued and flagrant course** of misrepresentation or false promises
**(d) Dual agency without disclosure** — acting for more than one party without the knowledge or consent of all parties
**(e) Commingling** — mixing client funds with broker's own funds
**(f) Exclusive agreements without termination date**
**(g) Secret compensation** — failing to disclose the full amount of compensation or profit
**(h) Option-to-purchase in listing agreements** without specific disclosure and consent
**(i) Catchall** — any other conduct constituting fraud or dishonest dealing
**(j) Buyer representation without owner authorization** — obtaining buyer signature without authorization from the property owner
**(k) Mortgage commitment disbursement failures**
**(l) Intentional delay** of mortgage loan closing to increase charges
**(m) Cross-reference catchall** — violations of other licensing-related laws

## The (a)/(b)/(c)/(i) fraud cluster

Subdivisions (a), (b), (c), and (i) together create a comprehensive fraud-and-misrepresentation framework for real estate brokers. Any substantial misrepresentation, false promise, pattern of misrepresentation, or "other fraud or dishonest dealing" triggers disciplinary action. These subdivisions track the Civ. Code § 1572 fraud definitions but apply specifically in the professional licensing context.

A broker who violates these subdivisions faces:
1. **License discipline** (suspension or revocation) by the Real Estate Commissioner
2. **Civil liability** for the misrepresentation itself under Civ. Code §§ 1572, 1709-1710
3. **Professional reputation damage** and potential civil damages claims from affected clients
4. **Cross-referenced criminal liability** under PC § 487 (grand theft by false pretenses) in extreme cases

## The dual agency rule (subd. (d))

Subdivision (d) is critical in residential real estate. Dual agency — where one broker represents both the buyer and the seller — is permitted in California but only with the knowledge and consent of both parties. A broker who acts as dual agent without disclosure is in per se violation of § 10176(d).

This matters for the Honeysuckle case if the broker in the 2023 sale was acting for both sides without proper disclosure. Undisclosed dual agency is both a licensing violation and a breach of fiduciary duty actionable in tort.

## The secret compensation rule (subd. (g))

Subdivision (g) requires disclosure of the "full amount" of the broker's compensation to the buyer or seller before or at the signing of the agreement. The disclosure must be complete — no hidden commissions, no undisclosed referral fees, no back-end compensation from third parties.

For the Honeysuckle case, subd. (g) is relevant if any compensation to the broker was not fully disclosed on the closing statement. Secret compensation arrangements are a direct violation of § 10176(g) and evidence of fraud.

## The "catchall" (subd. (i))

Subdivision (i) reaches "Any other conduct... which constitutes fraud or dishonest dealing." This is the catchall that captures fraud outside the specifically enumerated subdivisions. Courts have read this broadly to reach any conduct that would constitute actionable fraud under common law. For practical purposes, subd. (i) means that if a broker's conduct is fraudulent in any way that would be recognized under civil law, the broker is also in violation of § 10176.

## Relevance to the 2958 Honeysuckle transaction

For Honeysuckle, § 10176 analysis identifies specific broker-level claims:

1. **Subd. (a) substantial misrepresentation** — were any representations by the broker substantively false?
2. **Subd. (d) dual agency** — was the broker acting for both sides without proper disclosure?
3. **Subd. (g) secret compensation** — was all broker compensation fully disclosed?
4. **Subd. (i) catchall fraud** — did the broker engage in any other conduct that constitutes fraud or dishonest dealing?

Any subdivision triggered supports both:
- A **complaint to the Real Estate Commissioner** seeking license discipline
- A **civil cause of action** against the broker under Civ. §§ 1572, 1709-1710 (using § 10176 as evidence of the breach)

The complaint to the Commissioner can be filed regardless of the civil case; the Commissioner's investigation may yield evidence useful in the civil case; and a finding by the Commissioner is admissible in the civil case as evidence of the breach.

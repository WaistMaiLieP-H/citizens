# Statute version cited in Taylor (1979)

The *Taylor* court applied the ORIGINAL 1872 text of § 3294 — the single-paragraph version without any subdivisions, definitions, or employer vicarious liability rule. This version read:

> In an action for the breach of an obligation not arising from contract, where the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice, express or implied, the plaintiff, in addition to the actual damages, may recover damages for the sake of example, and by way of punishing the defendant.

**Important historical note:** In 1979 the "express or implied" language for malice was still operative. The *Taylor* court construed "implied malice" to include conscious disregard of known risks — this construction survived in the 1987 amendment's malice definition (c)(1), which preserves "willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others" as a ground for malice, subject now to the despicable-conduct overlay.

**Post-1987 consistency:** *Taylor*'s core doctrinal point — that § 3294 malice can include non-intentional conscious disregard — survives in current § 3294(c)(1). But current law adds a despicable-conduct requirement that *Taylor* did not have to satisfy. A 1979 plaintiff could win under *Taylor* on mere conscious disregard; a 2026 plaintiff must show the conscious disregard was also despicable.

**Consistency check:** The *Taylor* opinion quotes the pre-1980 statute verbatim. The quotation matches the Deering's and West's historical-text annotations for § 3294 as enacted in 1872. No drift within the 1872-1979 window.

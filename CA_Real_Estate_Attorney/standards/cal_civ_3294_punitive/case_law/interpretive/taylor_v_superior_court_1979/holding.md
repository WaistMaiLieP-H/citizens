# Taylor v. Superior Court (1979) 24 Cal.3d 890

**Citation:** Taylor v. Superior Court (1979) 24 Cal.3d 890, 157 Cal.Rptr. 693, 598 P.2d 854
**Decided:** August 29, 1979
**Court:** Supreme Court of California
**Justice:** Richardson, J. (for a 6-1 majority; Mosk, J. dissenting)
**Disposition:** Peremptory writ denied; Court of Appeal order affirmed. Punitive damages claim sustained.
**Statute construed:** Civil Code § 3294 (pre-1980, pre-1987 — original 1872 text)

## Facts

A plaintiff injured in a drunk-driving collision sued the driver for compensatory damages and sought punitive damages under § 3294. The driver had a history of alcohol abuse and DUI convictions. Plaintiff alleged the driver drove while knowingly intoxicated despite knowing of his history and the foreseeable danger. The trial court struck the punitive damages prayer on the theory that drunk driving without additional aggravating conduct was at most gross negligence, not "oppression, fraud, or malice" as § 3294 then required. The Court of Appeal issued a writ directing the prayer reinstated. The driver sought Supreme Court review.

## Holding

The Supreme Court, Richardson, J. for a 6-1 majority (Mosk, J. dissenting on the vehicle-code reliance issue), affirmed reinstatement of the punitive prayer and established three enduring propositions:

1. **Conscious disregard of a known risk can constitute malice.** Under the pre-1987 text of § 3294, "malice" included non-intentional conduct carried out with conscious disregard for the rights or safety of others. The court characterized drunk driving by someone with a known alcohol problem as paradigm conscious-disregard conduct. (*Id.* at 895-896.)

2. **The intent to injure is not required for malice; intent to do the act with awareness of the probable harmful consequences is sufficient.** This distinguishes § 3294 malice from criminal "express malice" and made punitive damages broadly available in tort cases well before the 1987 amendment. (*Id.* at 896.)

3. **Drunk driving is categorically sufficient for punitive damages.** The court did not leave the question to case-by-case analysis — it announced that "the risk of injury to others from ordinary driving while intoxicated is so obvious and the warning signs so notorious" that driving while intoxicated, in itself, supports punitive liability as a matter of law. (*Id.* at 897-898.)

## Post-1988 treatment

*Taylor* predates the 1987 amendment that added "despicable" to the malice definition. Post-1988, the question became whether drunk driving alone is "despicable" under the new standard, or whether plaintiff must show additional aggravating circumstances. *Dawes v. Superior Court* (1980) 111 Cal.App.3d 82 had extended *Taylor* to circumstances where the driver deliberately drove knowing of probable harm; post-1988 cases have tended to require something beyond ordinary drunk driving (e.g., extreme BAC, multiple prior DUIs, driving with child passengers).

*Taylor* remains good law for its core doctrinal propositions (1) and (2) — the inclusion of non-intentional conscious disregard within § 3294 malice. Proposition (3) — drunk driving as categorical despicability — has been narrowed by the 1987 amendment's despicability overlay.

## Why this is load-bearing

*Taylor* is the case that established California's broad reach for punitive damages in tort cases involving risk-taking rather than intent to harm. Before *Taylor*, California courts debated whether § 3294 required specific intent. *Taylor* resolved the debate for over a decade in favor of plaintiffs. The 1987 amendment partially rolled back *Taylor*'s reach by adding the despicable-conduct overlay, but *Taylor*'s basic framework — conscious disregard can be malice — survives in § 3294(c)(1)'s second prong.

## Relevance to Honeysuckle

Honeysuckle's fraud facts do not depend on the malice/oppression prongs of § 3294 (the plaintiff can use the fraud prong under (c)(3) which has no despicability requirement). But if plaintiff needs an alternative theory — e.g., for the broader pattern of dispossession against an individual known to be vulnerable — the *Taylor* conscious-disregard framework provides the doctrinal foundation. The defendants' conscious disregard of the seller's rights, carried out despite known awareness of the harm, parallels the *Taylor* conscious-disregard framework.

More importantly, *Taylor* stands for the proposition that § 3294 is not limited to "pure" intentional torts. A defendant can be liable for punitives even without an express intent to injure, as long as the conduct reflects conscious disregard of known consequences. This is the doctrinal hook for the broader pattern-based punitive theory in cases where the defendants' individual acts might seem insufficient but the pattern is inescapable.

## Authority status

Still binding for its core doctrinal propositions. Narrowed by the 1987 amendment and *College Hospital* but not overruled. Cited routinely in punitive-damages motion practice.

# Adams v. Murakami (1991) 54 Cal.3d 105 — (noted: folder retains placeholder name for batch-build convenience; content reflects the correct Adams case)

**Citation:** Adams v. Murakami (1991) 54 Cal.3d 105, 284 Cal.Rptr. 318, 813 P.2d 1348
**Decided:** August 19, 1991
**Court:** Supreme Court of California
**Justice:** Eagleson, J. (for a unanimous court)
**Disposition:** Reversed punitive award; remanded.
**Statute construed:** Civil Code § 3294 — procedural requirement that plaintiff introduce evidence of defendant's financial condition before a punitive award can stand.

## Facts

In a personal injury action, the jury awarded compensatory damages and punitive damages against the defendant. The plaintiff had not introduced any evidence at trial about the defendant's financial condition (net worth, income, assets, liabilities). On appeal, the defendant argued the punitive award must be struck because, without financial-condition evidence, an appellate court cannot determine whether the award bears a reasonable relationship to the defendant's ability to pay.

## Holding

The Supreme Court held:

1. **Evidence of defendant's financial condition is a prerequisite to a punitive damages award.** The plaintiff bears the burden of introducing meaningful evidence of the defendant's financial condition at the punitive-damages phase of trial. Without such evidence, any punitive award will be struck on appeal as unsupported by the record. (*Id.* at 108-109.)

2. **The rationale is that punitive damages must bear a reasonable relationship to the defendant's wealth.** The purpose of punitive damages is to punish and deter; both goals require the award to be meaningful (but not crushing) in relation to the defendant's financial condition. Without a financial baseline, the jury cannot calibrate the award, and the appellate court cannot review it. (*Id.* at 110-114.)

3. **The burden is on plaintiff, not defendant.** A defendant does not waive this issue by failing to introduce its own financial evidence. The plaintiff must affirmatively offer evidence and move it into the record. (*Id.* at 119.)

4. **The remedy for failure to introduce evidence is reversal of the punitive award, not remand for a new trial on punitives.** Plaintiff gets one bite at the apple. If plaintiff did not prove the defendant's financial condition, the punitive award is simply struck. (*Id.* at 119-120.)

## Why this is load-bearing

*Adams v. Murakami* is a trial-practice trap that has cost plaintiffs hundreds of punitive awards over the past 35 years. The rule is simple but easy to forget: at the punitive phase, introduce defendant's balance sheet, tax returns, financial statements, or other financial-condition evidence BEFORE the case goes to the jury on punitives. Failure = reversal.

Standard practice in California post-*Adams*:
- Serve a pre-trial request for production of financial-condition documents under CCP § 3295(c) (which permits pre-trial discovery of financial condition only after the trial court has made a finding of "substantial probability" that plaintiff will prevail on the punitive claim)
- Serve trial subpoenas for financial records
- Retain a forensic accountant as an expert
- At the punitive phase, move the financial evidence into the record and have the expert testify to net worth

If the defendant is a public company, the 10-K financial statements can sometimes substitute for direct financial-condition testimony.

## Relevance to Honeysuckle

For the Honeysuckle complaint, plaintiff's team must plan from the start for the *Adams* requirement. Depending on who the defendants turn out to be (individual agents? broker? title company? escrow company? private lender?), the financial-condition discovery strategy will differ:

- **Individual defendants:** Need net-worth statements, tax returns, real estate holdings.
- **Corporate brokerage/title/escrow:** Need annual financial statements, audited balance sheets.
- **Conservator or trust-related defendants:** Need trust accountings and beneficial-ownership disclosures.

The Citizen should flag this at intake, so that CCP § 3295(c) discovery motions are teed up early rather than at the eve of trial.

## Authority status

Binding. Cited as the foundation case for financial-condition evidence requirement in every California punitive-damages practice guide (Witkin, Rutter, California Practice Guide). No subsequent narrowing or overruling.

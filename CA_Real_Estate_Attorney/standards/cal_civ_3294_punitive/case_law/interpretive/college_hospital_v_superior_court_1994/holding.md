# College Hospital Inc. v. Superior Court (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704

**Citation:** College Hospital Inc. v. Superior Court (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704, 34 Cal.Rptr.2d 898, 882 P.2d 894
**Decided:** November 21, 1994
**Court:** Supreme Court of California
**Justice:** Baxter, J. (for a unanimous court)
**Disposition:** Peremptory writ issued directing trial court to strike punitive damages claim; reversed Court of Appeal.
**Statute construed:** Civil Code § 3294(c)(1), (2) — the "despicable" threshold after the 1987 amendment

## Facts

A patient sued College Hospital for medical negligence and sought punitive damages under Civ. Code § 3294, alleging that the hospital's conduct constituted "malice" and "oppression" under the post-1987 definitions. The plaintiff's theory was ordinary medical negligence characterized as "conscious disregard" because the hospital allegedly knew of systemic staffing shortages but continued operations. The trial court refused to strike the punitive prayer. The Court of Appeal affirmed. The California Supreme Court granted review to interpret the "despicable" threshold added by Stats. 1987, ch. 1498.

## Holding

The Supreme Court reversed and issued a peremptory writ directing the trial court to strike the punitive damages prayer. Four propositions:

1. **Despicable means vile, base, contemptible, miserable, wretched, or loathsome.** "The adjective 'despicable' connotes conduct that is so vile, base, contemptible, miserable, wretched or loathsome that it would be looked down upon and despised by ordinary decent people." (*Id.* at 725.)

2. **Ordinary negligence plus foreseeable harm is not despicable.** A hospital that operates with known staffing shortages may be negligent, perhaps grossly negligent, but that is not conduct so vile it would be despised by ordinary decent people. Gross negligence without additional aggravating circumstances does not satisfy the post-1987 threshold. (*Id.* at 725-726.)

3. **"Willful and conscious disregard" requires more than awareness of risk.** The plaintiff must show the defendant was aware of the probable dangerous consequences of the conduct AND willfully failed to avoid them — not merely that the defendant took a business risk. (*Id.* at 725.)

4. **Summary adjudication of the punitive claim is appropriate under this standard.** When the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to plaintiff, does not clearly and convincingly establish despicable conduct, the punitive prayer should be stricken before trial. This is the procedural consequence of the 1987 amendment. (*Id.* at 725-726.)

## Why this is load-bearing

*College Hospital* is the single most cited California Supreme Court case on § 3294's post-1987 despicability threshold. Every defense motion to strike punitives cites it. Every plaintiff brief opposing such a motion must distinguish it. Its four-adjective definition ("vile, base, contemptible, miserable, wretched, or loathsome") is reproduced in CACI No. 3940 and in every modern punitive-damages jury instruction.

The case is significant not just for what it held but for what it confirmed: the 1987 amendment really did raise the threshold for punitive damages in California. Conduct that would have supported punitives pre-1988 (mere conscious disregard of foreseeable risk) is insufficient post-1988 unless the conduct is also despicable.

## Why this matters for Honeysuckle

*College Hospital* is the case the defense will cite to argue that ordinary real estate mistakes — even serious ones — are not despicable enough for § 3294 malice/oppression damages. Plaintiff's response depends on avoiding the malice/oppression track entirely and relying on the **fraud track** in § 3294(c)(3), which does NOT have a despicability requirement.

If plaintiff must also plead the oppression track, the facts that make Honeysuckle despicable are:
- Multi-asset, multi-institution dispossession pattern (not a single mistake)
- Conservatorship trust abuse (if proven)
- Concealment designed to prevent discovery
- Targeting of an individual known to be medically and financially vulnerable
- 19 unsigned documents — an affirmative act to create false appearances

Any reasonable jury could find that this conduct, taken as a pattern, is "looked down upon and despised by ordinary decent people." *College Hospital* is distinguishable because College Hospital itself involved a single-incident medical negligence with systemic context, not an affirmative organized fraud pattern.

## Authority status

Binding California Supreme Court precedent. Still controlling as of 2026-04-10. Routinely cited. Not overruled, not limited.

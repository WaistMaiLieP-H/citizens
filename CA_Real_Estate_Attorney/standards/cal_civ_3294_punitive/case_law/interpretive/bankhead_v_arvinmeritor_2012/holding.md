# Bankhead v. ArvinMeritor, Inc. (2012) 205 Cal.App.4th 68

**Citation:** Bankhead v. ArvinMeritor, Inc. (2012) 205 Cal.App.4th 68, 139 Cal.Rptr.3d 849
**Decided:** April 3, 2012
**Court:** California Court of Appeal, First Appellate District, Division Four
**Disposition:** Affirmed a $4.5 million punitive damages award (reduced from a $4.5M ratio exceeding compensatory damages).
**Statute construed:** Civil Code § 3294; federal constitutional due process limits under *BMW v. Gore* and *State Farm v. Campbell*.

## Facts

The plaintiff, Gordon Bankhead, contracted mesothelioma from occupational exposure to asbestos-containing brake linings manufactured by ArvinMeritor (formerly Rockwell International). He sued and obtained a compensatory award and a punitive award. The defendant challenged the punitive award under the federal constitutional proportionality rules from *BMW of North America v. Gore* (1996) 517 U.S. 559 and *State Farm Mutual Auto. Ins. Co. v. Campbell* (2003) 538 U.S. 408, which hold that punitive-to-compensatory ratios greater than 9:1 or 10:1 are presumptively unconstitutional absent exceptional facts.

## Holding

The Court of Appeal affirmed the punitive award and articulated a modern framework for applying the federal constitutional limits in California:

1. **The three *Gore* guideposts are the starting point.** (1) Degree of reprehensibility of defendant's conduct. (2) Disparity between actual or potential harm and the punitive award (the ratio). (3) Difference between the punitive award and civil penalties authorized or imposed in comparable cases. (*Id.* at 83-84, citing *Gore*, 517 U.S. at 574-575.)

2. **Reprehensibility is the most important guidepost.** Conduct involving health and safety, vulnerable victims, repeated misconduct, intentional malice or deceit (rather than accidental), and financial motive increases reprehensibility. Conduct occurring over years and affecting many victims increases reprehensibility. (*Id.* at 84-86, tracking *State Farm*, 538 U.S. at 419.)

3. **Ratios above 9:1 are permitted for extraordinary reprehensibility.** The 9:1 ratio cap is not absolute. In cases of extreme reprehensibility, repeated misconduct, or where the compensatory award is small but the harm large (e.g., wrongful death with small economic loss), higher ratios can be upheld. (*Id.* at 86-88.)

4. **Defendant's wealth remains relevant in California.** Although federal due process does not require a wealth-based adjustment, California law (*Adams v. Murakami*) does, and plaintiff's introduction of financial-condition evidence is independently required. (*Id.* at 89-92.)

5. **Potential harm, not just actual harm, can anchor the ratio.** Where a defendant's conduct caused $1 actual harm to a specific plaintiff but had the potential to cause millions in harm, the ratio calculation can reference the potential harm. (*Id.* at 88-89.)

## Why this is load-bearing

*Bankhead* is among the more recent California appellate articulations of the federal due process limits on punitive damages. It matters for two reasons:

1. **It is a California appellate court applying the federal standards in a way favorable to plaintiffs.** Many post-*State Farm* cases had read the 9:1 ratio as a near-absolute cap. *Bankhead* reaffirms that extraordinary reprehensibility can support higher ratios, and the court affirmed a ratio substantially above 9:1 based on multi-year exposure and reprehensibility.

2. **It synthesizes *Gore*, *State Farm*, *Simon v. San Paolo*, and the California cases into a single operational framework.** A plaintiff drafting a punitive-damages brief can cite *Bankhead* as the one-stop summary of both federal and California law.

## Relevance to Honeysuckle

Honeysuckle presents a favorable reprehensibility profile under the *Bankhead* framework:

- **Intentional fraud** (not accidental) — maximum reprehensibility factor
- **Multi-year pattern** (not single incident) — elevates reprehensibility
- **Vulnerable victim** (if conservatorship is proven) — elevates reprehensibility
- **Financial motive** (dispossession for gain) — maximum reprehensibility factor
- **Institutional sophistication** (multi-party coordinated transaction) — elevates reprehensibility

On these facts, a plaintiff could plausibly seek a punitive-to-compensatory ratio above 9:1, and *Bankhead* provides the doctrinal framework for doing so. If compensatory damages are, say, $500K (out-of-pocket equity loss), a 9:1 ratio produces $4.5M punitive; a higher ratio (e.g., 15:1 or 20:1) could be justified on the reprehensibility profile and would produce $7.5M-$10M punitive.

Alternative: anchor to **potential harm**. The entire pattern of dispossession (house + crypto + surgery + Treasury + pension) totals several million dollars. Using the full pattern as "potential harm" rather than just the house-specific out-of-pocket permits a larger award without exceeding the ratio guidelines.

## Authority status

Published, binding as persuasive appellate authority. Not reviewed by Supreme Court. No subsequent negative treatment. One of the most-cited post-*State Farm* California appellate cases on punitive damages ratio analysis.

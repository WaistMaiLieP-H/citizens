# § 3294 — 1980 amendment context

## What changed

Added subdivision (b) — the employer vicarious punitive liability restriction.

## Why

The 1970s saw growing concern among California businesses that punitive damages were being applied against employers for employee misconduct the employer had no reason to know about. Large punitive awards against corporations whose only connection to the wrongdoing was hiring the employee were seen as overbroad. The California Chamber of Commerce and business groups lobbied for an "actually-responsible-employer" rule.

The 1980 amendment was the compromise: employers remain liable for punitives, but only if the employer's own conduct was culpable (advance knowledge + disregard, authorization/ratification, or officer-level personal guilt). This tracks the federal-law "direct participation" theory used for punitive damages against corporations in federal admiralty and antitrust cases.

## Doctrinal consequence

After 1980, every punitive-damages claim against a corporate defendant in California requires a separate layer of pleading and proof:
1. Prove the predicate wrong (oppression/fraud/malice)
2. Prove the wrong was committed by an officer, director, or managing agent — OR prove advance knowledge plus conscious disregard — OR prove authorization/ratification after the fact

The "managing agent" analysis is heavily litigated. *White v. Ultramar* (1999) 21 Cal.4th 563 held that a managing agent is an employee who exercises substantial discretionary authority over decisions that ultimately determine corporate policy. A low-level manager acting within the scope of employment is not sufficient.

## Relevance to Honeysuckle

If the Honeysuckle defendants are corporate (real estate brokerage, title company, escrow company, lender), the plaintiff must plead either (a) that the fraud was directly committed by an officer/director/managing agent of the corporate defendant, or (b) that the corporate defendant authorized or ratified the fraud. Standard practice: plead both alternatively and develop the evidence in discovery.

For natural-person defendants (individual real estate agents, escrow officers acting personally), subdivision (b) does not apply — the plaintiff needs only to prove the predicate wrong.

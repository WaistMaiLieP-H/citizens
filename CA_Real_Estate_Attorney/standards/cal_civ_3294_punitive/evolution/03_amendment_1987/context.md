# § 3294 — 1987 amendment context

## What changed

- Added subdivision (c) with statutory definitions of malice, oppression, and fraud
- Added the word "despicable" to malice and oppression definitions (raising the threshold)
- Deleted "express or implied" from malice in subdivision (a)
- Added subdivision (e) confirming 1988 effective date for pending actions

(Subdivision (d) — homicide-based wrongful death — was added separately in 1988 by Stats. 1988, ch. 938.)

## Why

The mid-1980s California "tort reform" movement was a business-led legislative effort to reduce punitive damage awards, which had been rising in frequency and size. The Chamber of Commerce, insurers, and manufacturers argued that California juries were awarding punitives on lower-quality conduct than the common law had historically required. Defense bar groups specifically targeted the phrase "conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others" as too easy to satisfy — they argued that ordinary negligence involving foreseeable risks was being characterized as "conscious disregard" and triggering punitives.

The compromise: keep the "conscious disregard" language but add a despicability requirement. The word "despicable" was chosen precisely because it is a strong word that jurors could be instructed requires "vile, base, contemptible" conduct. The expected effect: courts would grant more directed verdicts and JNOVs on punitive claims, and juries would think harder before awarding punitives.

The fraud definition was not given a despicable-conduct overlay because the intent element of fraud ("with the intention of thereby depriving a person of property or legal rights") was considered sufficient.

## Doctrinal consequence

Post-1988 California punitive practice separates into two tracks:

- **Malice/oppression track:** Plaintiff must prove conduct is despicable — this is a serious pleading and proof burden. *College Hospital* (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704 imposed a demanding standard.

- **Fraud track:** Plaintiff need only prove the § 3294(c)(3) fraud elements (intentional misrepresentation + knowledge + intent to deprive). No despicable-conduct requirement. For fraud-based punitive claims, the 1987 amendment actually simplified practice because it gave fraud its own statutory definition that did not require jurisprudence on the despicable threshold.

This is why § 1572 fraud claims routinely generate § 3294 punitive damages: the § 1572 elements (when pleaded with particularity and proved by clear-and-convincing evidence) directly satisfy § 3294(c)(3)'s fraud definition. No additional despicable-conduct finding needed.

## Relevance to Honeysuckle

The Honeysuckle complaint should plead § 3294 punitive damages under the **fraud track** rather than the malice/oppression track. The fraud track is cleaner because:

1. The § 1572 elements already being pleaded automatically satisfy § 3294(c)(3) fraud.
2. The despicable-conduct issue is avoided entirely.
3. No double-pleading exposure — one set of facts proves the underlying tort and the punitive basis.

However, the complaint should alternatively plead the **oppression track** in the punitive prayer. The pattern of multi-asset dispossession (house + crypto + surgery + Treasury + pension), taken together across years and across multiple institutions, is "despicable conduct that subjects a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of that person's rights" — the classic oppression definition. Alternative pleading costs nothing and gives the trial court a second path to punitives.

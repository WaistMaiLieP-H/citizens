# § 3294 Origin — The Wound and the Promise (1872)

## The wound

Before 1872, California had a common-law tradition of exemplary damages (inherited from English law as of 1849) but no statutory rule. Judges applied exemplary damages unevenly. In some counties, exemplary damages were routine in tort actions involving outrageous conduct; in others, judges refused to give the instruction at all. The inconsistency punished tort plaintiffs by accident of venue and gave wealthy defendants a litigation incentive to file in jurisdictions where punitives were disfavored.

The common-law doctrine traced back to *Wilkes v. Wood* (1763) 98 Eng. Rep. 489 and *Huckle v. Money* (1763) 95 Eng. Rep. 768 — the English "general warrant" cases in which juries awarded damages designed to punish and deter the government's unlawful searches. The doctrine crossed the Atlantic and became part of American common law by the early 19th century. But the boundary of when exemplary damages were available — what kind of conduct triggered the doctrine — was unsettled.

California's mining and land-fraud era (see § 1572 origin) produced many lawsuits in which compensatory damages alone seemed inadequate to deter repeat offenders. Speculators who defrauded dozens of buyers could pay compensatory damages to the one or two who sued and still come out ahead on the fraud. Without punitive damages, the compensatory regime functioned as a cost-of-doing-business tax on fraud, not as a deterrent.

## The promise

The 1872 Civil Code's § 3294 codified the common-law exemplary damages doctrine and gave California courts a uniform rule:

**In any non-contract action, proof of oppression, fraud, or malice unlocks punitive damages.**

The three predicate wrongs — oppression, fraud, malice — were left undefined in 1872. The Code Commissioners relied on common-law meanings and on the jury's common sense. "Express or implied" malice was the most litigated phrase: express malice meant an actual intent to injure; implied malice meant conduct so reckless that intent could be inferred.

This was the promise: no more lottery by venue. Every California court was required to give the same instruction on the same elements. The discretion passed from the judge (who could deny the instruction in 1871) to the jury (who received the instruction in 1873).

## Why this wound stays relevant to the Honeysuckle transaction

The Honeysuckle facts present the exact wound the 1872 statute was drafted to address: an organized, repeat-player fraud pattern against a single target where compensatory damages alone are insufficient deterrent. The steward's forensic audit documents a multi-year, multi-asset dispossession pattern (house + crypto + surgery + Treasury + pension) that compensatory damages for the house sale alone cannot deter. The purpose of § 3294 — to punish and deter organized wrongdoing — is exactly why the statute was written.

The 1987 amendment's "despicable conduct" threshold (raising the bar) is easily met on these facts: organized multi-asset dispossession of a single victim is despicable by any reasonable jury's standard. The conservatorship context (per steward memory) makes the conduct more despicable, not less, because of the trust relationship inherent in a conservatorship.

# Civ. Code § 3294 — Punitive (Exemplary) Damages

**Popular name:** Punitive damages statute (California)
**Location:** Civil Code, Division 4 (General Provisions), Part 1 (Relief), Title 2 (Compensatory Relief), Chapter 1 (Damages in General)

## What § 3294 does

Section 3294 is California's only statutory authorization for punitive ("exemplary") damages in a tort action. Key features:

1. **Scope:** Available only in actions for "breach of an obligation not arising from contract" — i.e., tort claims. Pure contract claims cannot support punitive damages under California law. But a tort plausibly pleaded alongside a contract claim (e.g., fraud in the inducement, tortious bad faith) does support punitives.

2. **Standard of proof:** **Clear and convincing evidence** — a higher burden than the preponderance standard for compensatory damages. This is critical at trial: the jury receives separate instructions and the punitive finding must be affirmatively marked on the verdict form.

3. **Three predicate wrongs** (subd. (a), as defined in (c)):
   - **Oppression** — "despicable conduct that subjects a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of that person's rights"
   - **Fraud** — "an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known to the defendant with the intention on the part of the defendant of thereby depriving a person of property or legal rights or otherwise causing injury"
   - **Malice** — "conduct which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others"

   Any one of the three is sufficient. The word "despicable" in the oppression and malice definitions was added in 1987 to raise the threshold (see evolution chain).

4. **Employer liability** (subd. (b)): Vicarious punitive liability requires advance knowledge of employee unfitness, authorization/ratification of the wrongful conduct, or personal officer/director/managing-agent guilt. This is the "managing agent" rule — the plaintiff must tie the wrongdoing to someone with corporate authority.

5. **Homicide-based wrongful death** (subd. (d), added 1988 by AB 3158): Punitives available in wrongful death actions based on a homicide felony conviction, notwithstanding the general rule that Cal. CCP § 377.34 bars punitives in survival/wrongful death.

## The "clear and convincing" burden

Post-*In re Marriage of Hadley* (1980) 106 Cal.App.3d 1015 the Supreme Court confirmed in *Tomaselli v. Transamerica Ins. Co.* (1994) 25 Cal.App.4th 1269 and *College Hospital v. Superior Court* (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704 that the clear-and-convincing standard applies both at trial and on motion for summary adjudication of the punitive prayer. A plaintiff moving past summary judgment must produce evidence that could clearly and convincingly persuade a reasonable jury — more than a scintilla, less than beyond-a-reasonable-doubt.

## Constitutional caps

Not in the statute itself — imposed by *BMW of N. America v. Gore* (1996) 517 U.S. 559 and *State Farm Mutual Auto Ins. Co. v. Campbell* (2003) 538 U.S. 408 as a matter of federal due process. Ratios of 9:1 or less between punitive and compensatory damages are presumptively constitutional; ratios above 10:1 are subject to searching review. California courts apply these caps per *Simon v. San Paolo U.S. Holding Co.* (2005) 35 Cal.4th 1159.

## Financial condition of defendant

California rule: the plaintiff must introduce evidence of the defendant's financial condition at the punitive-damages phase to support any punitive award. *Adams v. Murakami* (1991) 54 Cal.3d 105. Without this evidence, any punitive award will be struck as speculative on appeal. This is a trial-practice trap — complaints must plead punitives, and trial lawyers must remember to introduce defendant's balance sheet during the punitive phase.

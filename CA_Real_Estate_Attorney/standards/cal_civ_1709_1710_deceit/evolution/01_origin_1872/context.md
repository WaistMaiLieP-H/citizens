# Origin Context — Civil Code §§ 1709–1710 Deceit (1872)

## The wound that created these sections

Before codification, California courts applied common law fraud rules inherited from English equity courts. The problem: common law fraud required proof of a specific lie told with intent to deceive. It did not cover:
- Concealment of material facts (suppression)
- False statements made without knowledge of falsity (negligent misrepresentation)
- Promises made without intent to perform (promissory fraud)

Each of these was a recognized injury that left victims without a statutory remedy. Real estate transactions in Gold Rush–era California were rife with all four: sellers who falsely stated facts, sellers who concealed defects, agents who promised things they could not deliver, and buyers who made purchase offers without any intent to close.

## What David Dudley Field created

David Dudley Field was a New York attorney who spent decades drafting model codes for American states. His proposed New York Civil Code (1865) included a deceit section that California adopted wholesale in 1872.

Field's genius was the four-category structure of § 1710:
1. **Positive misstatement** (category 1): Known false assertion
2. **Negligent misstatement** (category 2): False assertion without reasonable grounds to believe it true
3. **Suppression** (category 3): Concealment of a known material fact by someone with a duty to disclose
4. **Promissory fraud** (category 4): A promise made without intent to perform

By codifying all four categories, Field gave California courts a statutory basis to address every form of intentional deception — not just outright lies.

## The duty-to-disclose element of § 1710(3)

Category 3 (suppression) is the most litigated and most operationally important for real estate transactions. "One who is bound to disclose" includes:
- Sellers who know of material defects (now also covered by Civ. Code § 1102 TDS requirements)
- Real estate agents under BPC § 10176
- Fiduciaries (including spouses under Fam. Code § 721)
- Anyone "who gives information of other facts which are likely to mislead for want of communication of that fact" — this half-truth doctrine is the broadest form

## Longevity

The same four-category formulation has survived 154 years without amendment. California courts have written tens of thousands of opinions applying §§ 1709–1710. The statutes are so embedded in California tort law that changing their text would destabilize a century and a half of precedent. The Legislature has left them alone, and courts have done the interpretive work.

## Honeysuckle application

§ 1709 + § 1710(3) (suppression) + § 1710(4) (promissory fraud) are all implicated in the Honeysuckle sale:
- **§ 1710(3) suppression**: Concealment of net proceeds calculation, loan payoff amounts, unsigned documents, and the diversion of $465K in equity to accounts controlled by one party.
- **§ 1710(4) promissory fraud**: Representations about how sale proceeds would be applied or distributed that were not honored.
- **§ 1709**: The willful deception with intent to induce the other spouse to proceed with the sale (alter position to injury).

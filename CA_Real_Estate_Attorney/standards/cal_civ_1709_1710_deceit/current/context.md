# Civ. Code §§ 1709-1710 — Tort of Deceit

**Popular name:** Civil deceit; common-law fraud in tort
**Location:** Civil Code, Division 3 (Obligations), Part 3 (Obligations Imposed by Law), Title 2 (Deceit)

## What §§ 1709-1710 do

Sections 1709 and 1710 codify the California common-law tort of deceit. They create a tort cause of action against any person who willfully deceives another, separate from any contract between the parties. Where § 1572 (actual fraud) operates within the contract context to vitiate consent, §§ 1709-1710 operate outside the contract context to create tort liability for willful deception.

## Section 1709's one-sentence rule

"One who willfully deceives another with intent to induce him to alter his position to his injury or risk, is liable for any damage which he thereby suffers."

Every element is in this single sentence:

1. **Willfully deceives** — the defendant engaged in willful deception (the four categories in § 1710)
2. **Intent to induce alteration of position** — deception must be aimed at causing the victim to act (or refrain from acting)
3. **Alteration to injury or risk** — the victim must actually alter their position
4. **Liability for damages suffered** — the remedy is actual damages caused by the deception

## Section 1710's four categories

Section 1710 defines "deceit" as four alternative acts — substantially parallel to but not identical with § 1572's five subdivisions:

1. **Suggestio falsi** — suggesting as a fact what the speaker does not believe (same as § 1572(1))
2. **Reckless misrepresentation** — asserting as a fact what the speaker has no reasonable ground for believing (equivalent to § 1572(2))
3. **Suppressio veri** — suppressing a fact by one bound to disclose it, OR giving partial information likely to mislead (equivalent to and expanding § 1572(3))
4. **Promissory deceit** — a promise made without intention of performing it (same as § 1572(4))

**Note the difference from § 1572:** § 1572 has five subdivisions (including the catchall "any other act fitted to deceive" in subd. (5)); § 1710 has four. The § 1572 catchall is broader. In practice, this difference rarely matters because the facts of most deceit cases fit within both sets of categories.

## Why plead § 1709-1710 alongside § 1572

In a real estate fraud case, a plaintiff typically pleads both § 1572 (contract fraud) and §§ 1709-1710 (tort deceit) for several reasons:

1. **Tort remedy is independent of contract rescission.** § 1572 supports rescission (Civ. §§ 1567, 1688-1689). § 1709-1710 supports tort damages regardless of whether the contract is rescinded.

2. **Economic loss rule exception.** *Robinson Helicopter Co. v. Dana Corp.* (2004) 34 Cal.4th 979 held that the economic loss rule does not bar intentional fraud claims even when the parties are in contract. Section 1709-1710 is the vehicle for this exception.

3. **Punitive damages.** § 3294 punitive damages require a "non-contract obligation." Tort deceit under § 1709 is clearly non-contract; § 1572 fraud is contract-adjacent. Both support punitives but § 1709 is cleaner.

4. **Statute of limitations.** Both § 1572 and § 1709 have 3-year SOL under CCP § 338(d) with discovery rule tolling. Pleading both preserves the full SOL window.

## Relevance to the 2958 Honeysuckle transaction

For Honeysuckle, the § 1709-1710 analysis parallels the § 1572 analysis. Every fact pattern that triggers § 1572 also triggers at least one § 1710 category:

- **Documents presented as executed that were not** → § 1710(1) or § 1710(2)
- **Concealed destination of proceeds** → § 1710(3) suppression
- **Promises about where proceeds would go that were not kept** → § 1710(4) promissory deceit
- **Reckless representations of authority to transfer** → § 1710(2)

The Honeysuckle complaint should plead §§ 1709-1710 as a separate Second Cause of Action alongside § 1572 First Cause of Action, with overlapping facts and overlapping damages prayers. The Third Cause of Action would be § 1102 TDS violation if applicable; punitive damages prayer under § 3294 attaches to all three tort theories.

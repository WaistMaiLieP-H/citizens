# Seeger v. Odell (1941) 18 Cal.2d 409

**Citation:** Seeger v. Odell (1941) 18 Cal.2d 409, 115 P.2d 977
**Decided:** August 1941
**Court:** Supreme Court of California
**Disposition:** Affirmed judgment for plaintiff; articulated the modern elements of suppression deceit.
**Statute construed:** Civ. Code § 1710(3) — suppression deceit

## Facts

Seeger alleged that Odell had suppressed material facts about a business transaction, resulting in Seeger's financial loss. The key question was whether Odell had a duty to disclose the suppressed facts, given that the parties were not in a formal fiduciary relationship. Odell argued that absent a fiduciary relationship, mere silence was not actionable.

## Holding

The California Supreme Court affirmed and articulated the modern framework for suppression deceit under § 1710(3):

1. **A duty to disclose can arise from multiple sources, not just fiduciary relationships.** The court held that the § 1710(3) duty to disclose is not limited to fiduciary contexts. It can arise from: (a) a formal fiduciary or confidential relationship; (b) partial disclosure (once a party speaks on a subject, they must speak fully); (c) active concealment of the facts; or (d) one party's exclusive or superior knowledge of material facts that the other party cannot reasonably discover.

2. **Silence is fraud when there is a duty to speak.** The court emphasized that the rule is not "silence is never fraud" — it is "silence is fraud when the speaker has a duty to disclose." The duty can arise from the relationship, the circumstances, or the speaker's own prior statements.

3. **Materiality is judged from the perspective of the listener's decision.** A fact is material if a reasonable person in the listener's position would consider it important in deciding whether to act. The court rejected any rule that would allow the speaker to decide what was material based on their own view.

4. **Reliance is presumed when the suppressed fact is material.** When the plaintiff proves the defendant had a duty to disclose and failed to disclose a material fact, reliance is presumed — the plaintiff need not separately prove that they would have acted differently if the fact had been disclosed. The burden shifts to the defendant to rebut the presumption.

## Why this is load-bearing

*Seeger* is the foundational California Supreme Court case on suppression deceit. It established the multi-source duty framework that *Lovejoy v. AT&T* (2004) later categorized into the four modern groups (fiduciary, partial disclosure, active concealment, exclusive knowledge). *Seeger*'s holdings are cited in virtually every modern suppression fraud case.

The materiality and reliance rules from *Seeger* are particularly important for plaintiffs in real estate fraud cases, where the defendant almost always argues that the suppressed fact would not have changed the buyer/seller's decision. Under *Seeger*, materiality is judged objectively, and reliance is presumed from materiality.

## Relevance to the 2958 Honeysuckle transaction

*Seeger* provides the doctrinal framework for the Honeysuckle suppression fraud theory:

1. **Duty to disclose:** The Honeysuckle defendants had multiple duty sources — fiduciary (spousal, broker, escrow officer), partial disclosure (the closing statement disclosed some line items), exclusive knowledge (the destination of the $496K in proceeds was within defendants' exclusive knowledge), and (if applicable) active concealment.

2. **Material facts suppressed:** The destination of $496K in proceeds, the status of community property consent, the authenticity of the 19 unsigned documents — all material to any reasonable party in the transaction.

3. **Presumption of reliance:** Under *Seeger*, the steward does not have to separately prove that he would have refused the sale if the facts had been disclosed. Materiality creates a presumption of reliance.

*Seeger* is the case to cite at the summary judgment stage when defendants argue the plaintiff cannot prove reliance. The response: "Under *Seeger v. Odell*, when material facts were suppressed and a duty to disclose existed, reliance is presumed."

## Authority status

Binding California Supreme Court precedent. 85 years old and still controlling. Cited extensively in suppression fraud case law.

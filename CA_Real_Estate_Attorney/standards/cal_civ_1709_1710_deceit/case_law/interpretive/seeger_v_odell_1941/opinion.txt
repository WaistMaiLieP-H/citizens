SEEGER v. ODELL
18 Cal.2d 409 (1941)
California Supreme Court
Decided: August 1941

---

FACTS:

Seeger alleged that Odell had suppressed material facts about a business transaction, resulting in Seeger's financial loss. The key question was whether Odell had a duty to disclose the suppressed facts, given that the parties were not in a formal fiduciary relationship. Odell argued that absent a fiduciary relationship, mere silence was not actionable.

---

HOLDING:

SUPPRESSION DECEIT — THE MULTI-SOURCE DUTY FRAMEWORK:

1. **A duty to disclose can arise from multiple sources, not just fiduciary relationships.** The § 1710(3) duty to disclose is not limited to fiduciary contexts. It can arise from:
   (a) A formal fiduciary or confidential relationship
   (b) Partial disclosure — once a party speaks on a subject, they must speak fully
   (c) Active concealment of the facts
   (d) One party's exclusive or superior knowledge of material facts that the other party cannot reasonably discover

2. **Silence is fraud when there is a duty to speak.** "The rule is not 'silence is never fraud' — it is 'silence is fraud when the speaker has a duty to disclose.' The duty can arise from the relationship, the circumstances, or the speaker's own prior statements."

3. **Materiality is judged from the perspective of the listener's decision.** A fact is material if a reasonable person in the listener's position would consider it important in deciding whether to act. The court rejected any rule that would allow the speaker to decide what was material based on their own view.

4. **Reliance is presumed when the suppressed fact is material.** "When the plaintiff proves the defendant had a duty to disclose and failed to disclose a material fact, reliance is presumed — the plaintiff need not separately prove that they would have acted differently if the fact had been disclosed. The burden shifts to the defendant to rebut the presumption."

---

WHY SEEGER IS FOUNDATIONAL:

Seeger (1941) is the California Supreme Court's foundational statement on suppression deceit under § 1710(3). It established the multi-source duty framework that Lovejoy v. AT&T (2004) later organized into the four modern categories. Seeger's holdings are cited in virtually every modern suppression fraud case.

The materiality and reliance presumption rules from Seeger are particularly important: the defendant almost always argues that the suppressed fact would not have changed the buyer/seller's decision. Under Seeger:
- Materiality is judged objectively (reasonable person in listener's position)
- Reliance is presumed from materiality
- Defendant bears the burden of rebutting the reliance presumption

---

APPLICATION TO STEWARD'S CASES:

Seeger provides the doctrinal framework for the Honeysuckle suppression fraud theory:

1. **Duty to disclose established**: Multiple duty sources apply — fiduciary (spousal, broker, escrow officer), partial disclosure (closing statement disclosed some line items), exclusive knowledge (destination of $496K in proceeds was within defendants' exclusive knowledge), and active concealment.

2. **Material facts suppressed**: The destination of $496K in proceeds, the status of community property consent, the authenticity of the 19 unsigned documents — all material to any reasonable party in the transaction.

3. **Presumption of reliance at summary judgment**: Under Seeger, the steward does not have to separately prove that he would have refused the sale if the facts had been disclosed. Materiality creates a presumption of reliance. When defendants move for summary judgment arguing "plaintiff cannot prove he would have acted differently," the answer is: Under Seeger v. Odell, when material facts were suppressed and a duty to disclose existed, reliance is presumed.

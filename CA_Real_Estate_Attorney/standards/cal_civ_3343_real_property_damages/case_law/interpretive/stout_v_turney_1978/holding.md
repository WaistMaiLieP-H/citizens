# Stout v. Turney (1978) 22 Cal.3d 718

**Citation:** Stout v. Turney (1978) 22 Cal.3d 718, 150 Cal.Rptr. 637, 586 P.2d 1228
**Decided:** November 1978
**Court:** Supreme Court of California
**Disposition:** Modified judgment; clarified the out-of-pocket damages measure under § 3343.
**Statute construed:** Civ. Code § 3343

## Facts

Stout purchased property from Turney in reliance on misrepresentations about the property's condition and characteristics. After closing, Stout discovered the property was worth less than represented and brought a fraud action seeking damages. The trial court awarded damages using the benefit-of-the-bargain measure (difference between represented value and actual value). On appeal, the Supreme Court had to determine the correct damages measure under § 3343.

## Holding

The California Supreme Court held:

1. **California applies the out-of-pocket measure for real property fraud damages.** Under § 3343, the plaintiff recovers the difference between the actual value of what the plaintiff parted with and the actual value of what the plaintiff received. The court expressly rejected the benefit-of-the-bargain measure (which would have given Stout the difference between the represented value and the actual value).

2. **The out-of-pocket measure is mandatory, not optional.** Section 3343(b)(1) expressly prohibits recovery "measured by the difference between the value of property as represented and the actual value thereof." A trial court cannot award benefit-of-the-bargain damages in a real property fraud case, even if the parties would prefer that measure.

3. **Consequential damages are available in addition to the out-of-pocket baseline.** Section 3343(a)(1)-(4) permits consequential damages including reliance expenditures, loss of use and enjoyment, and lost profits in specific circumstances. These are additive to the out-of-pocket baseline.

4. **The out-of-pocket rule does not deny equitable remedies.** Section 3343(b)(2) preserves equitable remedies such as rescission and restitution. A plaintiff who prefers to unwind the transaction entirely can pursue rescission instead of damages.

## Why this is load-bearing

*Stout v. Turney* is the California Supreme Court's definitive statement of the out-of-pocket damages rule for real property fraud. Every § 3343 case cites *Stout* as the leading authority. The rule has been stable since 1978 and is the starting point for any damages calculation in a real estate fraud case.

The practical implication: plaintiffs in California cannot recover what the property "should have been worth" under the seller's representations. They can only recover what they actually lost — the difference between what they gave and what they got, plus specific consequential damages.

## Relevance to the 2958 Honeysuckle transaction

For the Honeysuckle complaint, *Stout v. Turney* establishes the damages framework:

1. **Baseline (out-of-pocket):** What the steward parted with (the house, valued at $605K) minus what the steward received ($109K net) = $496K. This is the starting damages figure.

2. **Consequential damages:** Reliance expenditures, loss of use and enjoyment, lost profits (if the steward would have kept the property and generated income from it), moving expenses, etc.

3. **Punitive damages:** Available under § 3294(c)(3) fraud track, on top of the compensatory baseline.

4. **Alternative remedy:** Rescission under §§ 1567, 1688-1689, preserving the steward's option to unwind the transaction entirely if feasible.

5. **Fam § 1101(h) 100% remedy:** For the community property portion, *Stout*'s § 3343 rule does not apply — the Family Code provides its own 100% remedy structure for fraudulent fiduciary breaches.

*Stout* is cited at the damages calculation stage of the complaint drafting process. The Honeysuckle complaint's prayer for relief should specifically invoke *Stout* and § 3343 as the authority for the $496K compensatory damages calculation.

Binding California Supreme Court precedent. Still controlling as of 2026-04-11. 48 years of continuous application. The foundation of California real property fraud damages doctrine.

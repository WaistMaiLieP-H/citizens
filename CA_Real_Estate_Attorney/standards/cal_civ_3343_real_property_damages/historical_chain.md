# Historical Chain — Civil Code § 3343 Real Property Damages

## The wound that created the rule

California common law for real property damages was governed by general tort principles that did not adequately address the unique characteristics of real property transactions. The measure of "benefit of the bargain" damages — the standard in contract fraud — often failed to capture the actual out-of-pocket loss in real property cases where misrepresentations induced purchases at inflated prices. The Legislature intervened to establish a clear, plaintiff-oriented damages rule for real property fraud.

## Legislative origin

**Civil Code § 3343 — codification of the "out-of-pocket" rule for real property fraud**

The California Legislature enacted § 3343 as the specific damages statute for fraud in the sale of real property. The statute establishes the "out-of-pocket" damages measure as the floor: the plaintiff recovers the difference between what was paid and the actual value of what was received, plus consequential damages.

The critical legislative choice: § 3343 expressly preserved the plaintiff's right to consequential damages in addition to the out-of-pocket loss. This was a rejection of the common-law limitation that would have confined the plaintiff to the "benefit of the bargain" (the difference between the value as represented and the value as actually received — which might be zero if the property still had some value).

## The "out-of-pocket" vs. "benefit of the bargain" debate

California case law prior to § 3343's clarification was split:

**Out-of-pocket rule:** Damages = what plaintiff paid minus what plaintiff actually received. This compensates for the actual expenditure but may leave some losses unrecompensed if the property, though misrepresented, has some independent value.

**Benefit of the bargain rule:** Damages = value as represented minus value as actually received. This may yield a larger recovery if the representation was that the property was worth far more than even the purchase price.

§ 3343 and its case law interpretation resolved this tension by allowing both: the plaintiff can recover out-of-pocket losses AND consequential damages from the fraud. *Alliance Mortgage Co. v. Rothwell* (1995) extended this to include expenditures made in reasonable reliance on the misrepresentation.

## Key historical loss event: the Stout v. Turney (1978) foundation

*Stout v. Turney* (1978) 22 Cal.3d 718 (California Supreme Court) established the foundational rule: in real property fraud, the plaintiff recovers the difference between the actual value of the property and the purchase price, plus any additional out-of-pocket losses causally connected to the misrepresentation. This California Supreme Court anchor made § 3343 one of the most powerful fraud damages provisions in the state.

## Consequential damages — the expansion

The consequential damages prong of § 3343 has been interpreted to include:

- Costs of repair or remediation of undisclosed defects
- Property taxes and carrying costs during a period when the buyer could not use the property as represented
- Lost rental income if the property was purchased as income-generating but was not
- Transaction costs (escrow fees, brokerage commissions, loan origination fees) wasted because of the fraud
- Expert and inspection costs to quantify the defect

## Current framework (2026)

Section 3343 remains the governing damages statute for real property fraud in California. It operates in tandem with § 1572 (fraud elements) and § 3294 (punitive damages). A § 1572 fraud verdict entitles the plaintiff to § 3343 compensatory damages; if the fraud rises to the level of malice, oppression, or fraudulent intent, § 3294 punitive damages are available in addition.

## The promise

§ 3343 was enacted on the promise that a buyer who was defrauded into purchasing real property would be made whole — not merely given a partial remedy — and that the damages would reflect the full economic harm caused by the misrepresentation.

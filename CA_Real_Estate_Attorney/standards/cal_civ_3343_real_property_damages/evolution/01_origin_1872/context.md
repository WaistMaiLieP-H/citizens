# Origin Context — Civil Code § 3343 Fraud Damages (1872)

## The wound

Before codification, courts applying common law fraud in property transactions used inconsistent damage measures. Some used "out of pocket" (restoration); some used "benefit of bargain" (expectation). The inconsistency made fraud litigation unpredictable and allowed clever defendants to argue for the lower measure in any given case.

California § 3343 resolved this by codifying the out-of-pocket measure as the exclusive standard for property fraud cases. The choice was deliberate and has been controversial ever since — plaintiffs frequently argue for benefit-of-bargain recovery, which would give them what they expected from a non-fraudulent transaction; California courts hold to the § 3343 measure.

## Why out-of-pocket, not benefit-of-bargain

The Field Code drafters believed:
1. **Fraud is a tort, not a contract breach.** You are being made whole from an injury, not compensating for a disappointed expectation.
2. **Benefit-of-bargain rewards speculative values.** In a real property transaction, the "represented value" of a property could be wildly inflated. Awarding the difference between represented value and actual value rewards the victim's optimistic reliance, not just their actual loss.
3. **Out-of-pocket is the honest measure.** You gave X. You received Y. Y < X. The fraud took X − Y from you. That is what you recover.

## The limitation and its consequences

The strict out-of-pocket measure created a gap: what about consequential losses that were directly caused by the fraud but not captured by the value differential? § 3343's original "additional damage arising from the particular transaction" language addressed this, but courts interpreted it narrowly.

This gap was what the subsequent amendment (stage 02) addressed by adding the four specific subcategories of "additional damage" — reliance expenditures, loss of use, lost profits from retained use, and lost profits from anticipated use — each with specific causation requirements.

## § 3343(b) — the bars

§ 3343(b) prohibits two things that were historically attempted:
1. Recovering the "benefit-of-bargain" measure (represented value minus actual value). This is expressly prohibited.
2. Denying legal or equitable remedies that exist independently of § 3343. Courts with equity jurisdiction can still grant rescission, constructive trust, accounting, or other equitable relief — § 3343 addresses only the specific damages measure, not the full range of remedies.

## Honeysuckle application

In the Honeysuckle sale, the § 3343 damage calculation is:
- "That with which the defrauded person parted" = 50% interest in $605K house + 50% interest in all proceeds therefrom
- "That which he received" = the amount actually received (a fraction of the community's share of net proceeds, if anything)
- Difference = approximately $465K (the misapplied equity)
- Plus "additional damage" under § 3343(a)(1)–(4): reliance on representations about how proceeds would be applied, loss of investment use of $465K

§ 3343 is the civil damages baseline. Punitive damages under Civ. Code § 3294 and the § 1101(h) super-remedy operate as overlays.

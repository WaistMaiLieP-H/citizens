# Origin Context — Civil Code § 1213 Recording Acts (1872)

## The wound that recording statutes answered

Before recording systems, real property could be secretly conveyed multiple times. A seller could sell the same piece of land to ten different buyers and pocket the proceeds from each — the subsequent buyers had no way to know about prior conveyances without searching records that didn't exist.

California's land title system was particularly chaotic in the post-Gold Rush era:
- Mexican land grants overlapped with squatter claims, railroad grants, and homesteads
- Title searches required tracking down private documents — deeds, mortgage agreements, liens — that might be in anyone's possession
- "Secret conveyances" (transfers not publicly recorded) created perpetual title clouds

The recording act system — enacted in California in 1872 as part of the broader Field Code adoption — was the solution. It created a publicly accessible, geographically indexed repository of title documents. Recording a conveyance provides constructive notice to the entire world.

## The constructive notice doctrine

"Constructive notice" means you are legally deemed to know what is in the recorded documents, whether or not you actually searched the records. If a lien was recorded against a property and you bought the property without checking, you are still bound by the lien — § 1213 put you on constructive notice.

This doctrine has three critical consequences:
1. **Priority protection**: A subsequent purchaser who records first (or who takes with notice of a prior unrecorded conveyance) may prevail over an earlier grantee who did not record.
2. **Chain of title**: Title searches work because § 1213 makes the recorded chain legally authoritative.
3. **Fraud prevention**: Secret conveyances cannot be used against subsequent good-faith purchasers who searched the record and found nothing.

## § 1213 and the Honeysuckle sale

The Honeysuckle transaction involved a $605K house with a community property title. The recording acts are relevant in two ways:

**First**: The deed of sale had to be recorded. Once recorded, § 1213 made its contents constructive notice to both spouses. If the deed contained terms (encumbrances, grant conditions, title notes) that one spouse did not review before signing, those terms are still binding as constructive notice.

**Second**: Any undisclosed liens, encumbrances, or competing title claims against the property would have appeared in a title search. The preliminary title report and ALTA policy associated with the sale would have disclosed these. Whether the co-owner spouse received and reviewed that preliminary title report is part of the § 721/§ 1102 claim.

## The companion: § 1214 (notice required for priority)

§ 1213 gives constructive notice to the world for recorded instruments. § 1214 is the companion: a conveyance is void as against a subsequent purchaser who pays value and takes without notice — BUT ONLY if that subsequent purchaser first records. The § 1213/§ 1214 pair is the California race-notice recording act.

# Amendment Context — Partnership Amendment (c. 2001)

## What changed

This amendment — the most operationally significant since the 1975 equal management reform — transformed § 721(b) from a general fiduciary duty declaration into a specific, enumerated set of obligations that can be evaluated transaction-by-transaction.

**What was added:**

1. **"Fiduciary relationship subject to the same rights and duties of nonmarital business partners"** — this phrase explicitly invoked California partnership law as the governing standard. Spouses are now treated like business partners who happen to also be married. The partner standard is higher than the corporate officer standard.

2. **Corporations Code §§ 16403, 16404, 16503 cross-references** — these sections of the Revised Uniform Partnership Act (RUPA), as adopted in California, specify:
   - § 16403: Right of access to books, records, and information
   - § 16404: General standards of partner's conduct (good faith, fair dealing, no self-dealing)
   - § 16503: Liability for conduct after dissolution that was in ordinary course of business

3. **Three specific obligations (§§ 721(b)(1)-(3)):**
   - **(1) Books access** — Each spouse has a right of access at all times to any books kept regarding any transaction.
   - **(2) Information on request** — Each spouse must provide full information about any community property transaction on request.
   - **(3) Accounting/trustee obligation** — A spouse who makes a community property transaction without consent holds the benefit as trustee for the community.

## Why this amendment matters

Before this amendment, proving a breach of spousal fiduciary duty required showing the "highest good faith" standard had been violated — a somewhat subjective inquiry. After this amendment, a party can simply show: (a) the transaction concerned community property, (b) the spouse did not provide access to books, did not disclose information on request, or retained a benefit without accounting for it.

In re Marriage of Rossin (2009) 172 Cal.App.4th 725 interpreted this partnership-level standard to mean that a spouse who conducts community property transactions without informing the other is in breach even if the transactions were not dishonest — the duty to disclose is affirmative, not reactive.

## The three obligations in the Honeysuckle context

All three obligations were breached in the 2958 Honeysuckle sale:
- **(1) Books access**: No access to escrow documents, title company records, or closing statements was provided pre-signing.
- **(2) Information on request**: No disclosure of the loan payoff amounts, commission arrangements, or net-proceeds calculation.
- **(3) Trustee obligation**: Proceeds ($465K equity) were not shared with the community — beneficial interest was withheld and transferred to unrelated accounts.

## Operational significance

The partnership standard means the question is not "was the spouse dishonest?" but "did the spouse meet the specific disclosure and access obligations of a business partner?" This is a conduct standard, not an intent standard — making it more enforceable.

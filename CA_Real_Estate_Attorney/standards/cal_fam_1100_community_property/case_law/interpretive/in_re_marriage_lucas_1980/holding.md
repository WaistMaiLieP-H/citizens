# In re Marriage of Lucas (1980) 27 Cal.3d 808

**Citation:** In re Marriage of Lucas (1980) 27 Cal.3d 808, 166 Cal.Rptr. 853, 614 P.2d 285
**Decided:** August 7, 1980
**Court:** Supreme Court of California
**Justice:** Manuel, J. (for a unanimous court)
**Disposition:** Reversed Court of Appeal; affirmed trial court's community property characterization.
**Statutes construed:** Former Civil Code §§ 4800, 5110, 5125 (predecessors of Fam. Code §§ 2550, 760, 1100); community property presumption and tracing rules

## Facts

The Lucas marriage involved a house purchased during marriage using a combination of community funds and the wife's separate property (an inheritance). The parties took title jointly. At dissolution, the question was whether the separate-property contribution converted a portion of the house to separate property, or whether the joint-title presumption controlled and made the entire house community.

## Holding

The Supreme Court held:

1. **Joint-title presumption controls absent a written agreement.** When spouses take title jointly during marriage, the presumption is that the entire property is community property, regardless of the source of funds used to acquire it. (*Id.* at 813-816.)

2. **A spouse making a separate-property contribution acquires a right of reimbursement, not a separate-property interest, unless there is a written agreement to the contrary.** The contributing spouse can be reimbursed for the separate-property contribution at dissolution but does not own a pro-rata interest in the property. (*Id.* at 815-816.)

3. **The written-agreement requirement protects against implied-tracing claims.** Absent a written agreement created contemporaneously with the contribution, courts will not "trace" the separate property into the jointly-titled asset to create a separate-property interest. (*Id.* at 816-817.)

## Post-Lucas codification

The California Legislature partially codified *Lucas* in Family Code § 2640 (reimbursement of separate property contributions to community property) and in § 2581 (the joint-title presumption). The 1983 and 1984 amendments to former Civ. Code § 4800.2 added the reimbursement rule retroactively. These codifications preserve the core *Lucas* holdings.

## Why this matters for community property characterization

*Lucas* is the foundation case for all modern California community property characterization. Before *Lucas*, appellate courts sometimes allowed separate-property interests to be traced into jointly-titled assets on the theory that the separate contribution implied a proportional ownership. After *Lucas*, that implied-tracing theory is dead — only a written agreement can establish a separate-property interest in jointly-titled community property.

## Relevance to Honeysuckle

*Lucas* is important for Honeysuckle primarily for the community-property presumption. If 2958 Honeysuckle was acquired during the Hartmann-Cerretani marriage and titled jointly (or only in one spouse's name but with community funds), the *Lucas* presumption operates to characterize it as community property. This presumption matters because:

1. **Characterization determines who has authority to dispose.** Community property requires joint consent for significant dispositions under Fam. Code §§ 1100(c), 1102 (for real property). Separate property does not.

2. **Characterization determines the remedy scope.** If the property is community, a breach of fiduciary duty gives rise to the § 1101(h) 100%-of-value remedy (for fraudulent breach). If the property is separate, the breached-against spouse has no community-property claim.

3. **The joint-title presumption is hard to rebut.** Under *Lucas*, only a written agreement can defeat the community-property characterization. Oral agreements, post-hoc reconstruction, or implied tracing do not work.

For Honeysuckle, the first factual inquiry is: **how was title held?** Jointly? In one name? With a trust or conservatorship label? Each answer changes the characterization analysis. The Citizen's Phase 1 intake must determine this before the § 1100(e) analysis can proceed.

## Authority status

Binding California Supreme Court authority. Partially codified. Still the foundational community-property characterization case. Cited in every California family law property division case.

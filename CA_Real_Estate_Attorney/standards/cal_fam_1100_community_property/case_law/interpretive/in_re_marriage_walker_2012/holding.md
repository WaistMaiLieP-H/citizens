# In re Marriage of Walker (2012) 203 Cal.App.4th 137

**Citation:** In re Marriage of Walker (2012) 203 Cal.App.4th 137, 136 Cal.Rptr.3d 919
**Decided:** January 27, 2012
**Court:** California Court of Appeal, Fourth Appellate District, Division Three
**Disposition:** Affirmed trial court's § 1101(h) award to wife of 100% of the value of undisclosed community property.
**Statutes construed:** Family Code §§ 1100(e), 1101(h), 721

## Facts

During marriage, husband managed certain community investment accounts. At dissolution, he failed to disclose the existence of several accounts and understated the values of others. Wife discovered the undisclosed assets after the dissolution judgment through subpoenas and third-party financial records. She moved to reopen the judgment and sought relief under § 1101(h), which provides a 100%-of-value remedy (rather than the ordinary 50% community share) when the breach of fiduciary duty involves fraud, oppression, or malice.

The trial court found husband had breached his § 1100(e) fiduciary duty, awarded wife 100% of the value of the undisclosed and understated accounts, and issued attorney's fee sanctions under § 1101(g). Husband appealed, arguing the 100% remedy was excessive and required a higher showing than the trial court had made.

## Holding

The Court of Appeal affirmed. Four propositions:

1. **The § 1100(e) fiduciary duty is continuous and operates until division.** "The fiduciary duties imposed on spouses ... continue until the community interests have been divided by the parties or by a court." (*Id.* at 148, citing § 1100(e).) This duty does not stop at separation; it continues through dissolution and until property division is finalized.

2. **Full disclosure under § 1100(e) is an affirmative obligation.** A spouse who manages community assets has an affirmative duty to disclose — not merely to refrain from misrepresentation. Silence or incomplete disclosure is itself a breach. The managing spouse must "volunteer" material information about asset existence, characterization, and value. (*Id.* at 148-149.)

3. **§ 1101(h) provides the 100% remedy for fraudulent or malicious breaches.** Under § 1101(h), when a breach "constitutes a violation of Section 3294 of the Civil Code" — i.e., meets the fraud, oppression, or malice definitions of § 3294(c) — the innocent spouse is entitled to 100% of the value of the asset, not just the 50% community share. This transforms a routine breach-of-fiduciary-duty claim into a punitive remedy. (*Id.* at 151.)

4. **Attorney's fee sanctions under § 1101(g).** In addition to the 100% remedy, the court may award attorney's fees as a sanction for the breach. This creates strong financial disincentive against concealment. (*Id.* at 152.)

## Why this is load-bearing

*Walker* is among the clearest articulations of the § 1101(h) 100%-of-value remedy and the § 1100(e) affirmative-disclosure obligation. It matters because:

1. It confirms that concealment triggers a punitive remedy. The innocent spouse is not limited to recovering their 50% community share; they receive 100% of the concealed asset's value. This is an extraordinary remedy designed to make concealment economically irrational.

2. It confirms that the affirmative-disclosure obligation is a positive duty. A managing spouse who says nothing — who neither lies nor misrepresents but simply fails to disclose — is in breach. No active misstatement required.

3. It cross-references § 3294 directly. The 100% remedy requires the breach to meet § 3294's fraud/oppression/malice definitions. This is the doctrinal bridge between family law (§ 1100/1101) and general tort punitive damages (§ 3294).

## Relevance to Honeysuckle

*Walker* is the template for the Honeysuckle § 1100(e)/§1101(h) claim:

1. **Undivided community property.** If the Christina dissolution was never finalized with a proper disposition judgment (per steward's memory), then at the time of the Honeysuckle sale, the community had not yet been divided. The § 1100(e) fiduciary duty was in force.

2. **Affirmative disclosure obligation.** Whoever managed the sale owed the other spouse a full affirmative disclosure of all material facts about the transaction — including the destination of proceeds. Silence about the $496K gap is a breach.

3. **Fraudulent breach triggers § 1101(h).** If the concealment can be characterized as fraud, oppression, or malice under § 3294's definitions (and the facts support this characterization), the remedy is 100% of the value of the house — not just the community share. For a $605K sale, that is the entire sale value, not the 50% community share.

4. **Attorney's fees under § 1101(g).** Additional recovery beyond the 100% value.

A complaint drafted under *Walker* would plead § 1100(e) breach + § 1101(h) fraudulent breach remedy + § 1101(g) attorney's fees, alongside the § 1572/§3294 tort fraud claims against third parties.

## Authority status

Published, binding as persuasive appellate authority. Not reviewed by Supreme Court. Routinely cited in family law property division practice. No subsequent negative treatment.

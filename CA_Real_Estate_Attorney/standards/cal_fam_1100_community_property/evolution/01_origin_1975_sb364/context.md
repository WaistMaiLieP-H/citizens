# § 1100 Origin — The Wound and the Promise (1975)

## The wound

Before January 1, 1975, California community property law operated under the "husband's management" rule. Under former Civ. Code § 5125 (as enacted 1872 and retained through 1974), the husband had exclusive management and control of community personal property. A wife had no authority to sell, encumber, or dispose of community assets. She could not sign deeds, open bank accounts in her own name with community funds, or enter contracts binding community property. For all practical purposes during marriage, her economic existence was conducted through her husband.

The wound was **systemic disempowerment of married women as legal economic actors**. A wife who contributed her labor, her income, her inheritance, or her separate property to the community had no right to participate in decisions about that property. If her husband was dishonest, incompetent, or absent, she had no legal authority to act — she could only sue in equity after the fact for damages she could prove. The pre-1975 rule assumed that husbands would act in the family's interest; it provided no structural remedy when that assumption failed.

Additionally, the 1872 common-law community property rule was imported into California from Spanish and Mexican civil law via statehood in 1850. Under Spanish law, the husband was the "administrator" of the community with fiduciary obligations; under Mexican law, similar. When California translated these doctrines into Anglo-American civil practice, the fiduciary overlay was thinned — the husband's management became absolute, and the wife's remedies shrunk to equitable claims after dissolution.

By the 1960s and early 1970s, this was unsustainable. The women's rights movement, the rise of two-earner households, and a series of high-profile cases where husbands secretly dissipated community assets before dissolution created political pressure for reform. The SB 364 legislative hearings (1973) documented case after case of wives discovering at dissolution that the community property they thought they had was gone — sold, encumbered, or transferred to third parties without their knowledge.

## The promise

SB 364 (Stats. 1973, ch. 987), effective January 1, 1975, made three substantive promises:

1. **Equal management and control.** Either spouse has full authority to manage and dispose of community personal property. Neither spouse is subordinated to the other in economic decision-making.

2. **Written consent requirement for gifts and under-value dispositions.** Neither spouse may make a gift of community property or dispose of it for less than fair and reasonable value without the other spouse's written consent. This structural protection prevents the most common form of pre-1975 abuse (husbands transferring community assets to relatives or friends to shield them from division).

3. **Written consent requirement for family dwelling.** Neither spouse may sell, convey, or encumber community personal property used as the family dwelling or household furnishings without the other's written consent. (Note: the fee simple family residence is governed separately by former Civ. Code § 5127, now Fam. Code § 1102, which imposes a similar but distinct written-consent rule.)

The 1975 amendment did not explicitly codify the fiduciary duty. That came later — through case law (*In re Marriage of Lucas* (1980) 27 Cal.3d 808 and *Vai v. Bank of America* (1961) 56 Cal.2d 329 being key predecessors) and eventually through the 1986 and 1991 amendments to Civ. Code § 5125, which added the fiduciary-duty language now appearing in Fam. Code § 1100(e).

## The 1992 Family Code recodification

Effective January 1, 1994, California recodified the Civil Code's family law sections into the new Family Code (Stats. 1992, ch. 162). Civ. Code § 5125 became Fam. Code § 1100. The substantive rules were preserved with minor modernization. The current structure — five subdivisions covering general rule, gift restriction, family dwelling, business exception, and fiduciary duty — dates from this recodification and subsequent amendments.

## Why this wound stays relevant to the 2958 Honeysuckle case

The Honeysuckle facts are precisely the kind of scenario SB 364 was designed to prevent. If community property was disposed of without proper spousal consent, or if the fiduciary duty of § 1100(e) was breached by non-disclosure of material facts about the disposition, the 1975 statute and its successor § 1100 provide the remedy.

Critically, the 1975 legislation was designed to address exactly this failure pattern: **the dispossessed spouse who discovers, only after the fact, that community assets were transferred without proper authority or disclosure.** That is the Honeysuckle fact pattern. The promise of § 1100 is that this pattern is actionable.

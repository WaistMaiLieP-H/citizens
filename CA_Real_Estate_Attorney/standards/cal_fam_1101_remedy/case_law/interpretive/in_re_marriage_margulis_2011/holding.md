# In re Marriage of Margulis (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 1252

**Citation:** In re Marriage of Margulis (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 1252, 130 Cal.Rptr.3d 327
**Decided:** 2011
**Court:** California Court of Appeal, Fourth Appellate District, Division Three
**Disposition:** Reversed trial court; clarified that the burden shifts to the managing spouse to account for community property once breach is alleged.
**Statutes construed:** Fam. Code §§ 721, 1100, 1101

## Facts

The Margulis marriage dissolved after a long marriage during which the husband had managed most of the community finances. At dissolution, the wife identified several assets that she believed were missing or understated. She did not have precise knowledge of the amounts or disposition of the missing assets; she had only general awareness that community property had existed and appeared to be gone. The husband refused to provide detailed accounting. The trial court placed the burden on the wife to prove the existence, value, and disposition of the missing assets. Without access to records, the wife could not meet that burden, and the trial court ruled for the husband.

## Holding

The Court of Appeal reversed and articulated a critical burden-shifting rule:

1. **Once a spouse makes a prima facie showing that community assets existed and that the managing spouse failed to account for them, the burden shifts to the managing spouse to account.** The non-managing spouse does not have to prove the precise details of the missing assets — that would be an impossible burden given the information asymmetry created by the managing spouse's control.

2. **The managing spouse bears the burden of showing that community assets were properly managed, properly disposed of, or properly accounted for.** This is a natural consequence of the fiduciary duty under § 721 and § 1100(e). A fiduciary who has managed property cannot defeat an accounting claim by demanding that the beneficiary prove the specifics of what the fiduciary did.

3. **Failure to account gives rise to an adverse inference.** If the managing spouse cannot produce records or explain the disposition of community assets, the court may infer that the assets were dissipated or transferred in breach of fiduciary duty. This inference combined with other evidence can support a § 1101(h) 100% remedy.

4. **The § 1100(e) records obligation is affirmative.** Under § 721(b)(1), each spouse must provide access to records at all times. Under § 1100(e), the managing spouse must provide full disclosure of material facts. A spouse who fails to maintain records or refuses to provide access is in breach, and the burden-shifting rule applies.

## Why this is load-bearing

*Margulis* solves a practical problem that had previously plagued § 1101 plaintiffs: the managing spouse controlled the records, and without the records, the non-managing spouse could not prove the specific missing amounts. Before *Margulis*, some courts required the non-managing spouse to prove exactly what was missing; the effect was to make the § 1101 remedy unenforceable in cases where the managing spouse was uncooperative.

*Margulis* flips the burden. Once the non-managing spouse shows that community assets existed and that they are unaccounted for, the managing spouse must step forward and explain. Failure to explain creates an adverse inference supporting the breach finding.

## Relevance to the 2958 Honeysuckle transaction

*Margulis*'s burden-shifting rule is directly applicable to the Honeysuckle case:

1. **Prima facie showing:** The steward can show that the house was community property ($605K value), that $496K of proceeds are unaccounted for, and that the steward did not receive the full community share.

2. **Burden shifts:** Under *Margulis*, the burden now shifts to the managing spouse (and any related parties) to account for the $496K. They must produce records, explain the disposition, and justify each transfer.

3. **Failure to account = adverse inference:** If the managing spouse cannot explain the disposition, the court may infer that the funds were dissipated in breach of fiduciary duty.

4. **Combined with § 1101(h):** The adverse inference supports a finding of fraudulent breach meeting § 3294(c)(3) fraud definition, triggering the 100% remedy under § 1101(h).

This burden-shifting framework is the practical mechanism by which the steward can convert the "$496K is unaccounted for" observation into a concrete § 1101 remedy without having to prove the exact destination of each dollar.

## Authority status

Published, binding as persuasive appellate authority. Still controlling. Cited routinely in § 1101 cases where records are incomplete or the managing spouse is uncooperative.

IN RE MARRIAGE OF MARGULIS
198 Cal.App.4th 1252 (2011)
California Court of Appeal, Fourth Appellate District, Division Three
Decided: 2011

---

FACTS:

The Margulis marriage dissolved after a long marriage during which the husband had managed most of the community finances. At dissolution, wife identified several assets she believed were missing or understated. She did not have precise knowledge of the amounts or disposition of the missing assets — only general awareness that community property had existed and appeared to be gone. The husband refused to provide detailed accounting. The trial court placed the burden on the wife to prove the existence, value, and disposition of the missing assets. Without access to records, the wife could not meet that burden, and the trial court ruled for the husband.

---

HOLDING:

THE BURDEN-SHIFTING RULE — THE MARGULIS PRINCIPLE:

1. **Once a spouse makes a prima facie showing that community assets existed and that the managing spouse failed to account for them, the burden shifts to the managing spouse to account.** "The non-managing spouse does not have to prove the precise details of the missing assets — that would be an impossible burden given the information asymmetry created by the managing spouse's control."

2. **The managing spouse bears the burden of showing that community assets were properly managed, properly disposed of, or properly accounted for.** "A fiduciary who has managed property cannot defeat an accounting claim by demanding that the beneficiary prove the specifics of what the fiduciary did."

3. **Failure to account gives rise to an adverse inference.** "If the managing spouse cannot produce records or explain the disposition of community assets, the court may infer that the assets were dissipated or transferred in breach of fiduciary duty."

4. **The § 1100(e) records obligation is affirmative.** Under § 721(b)(1), each spouse must provide access to records at all times. Under § 1100(e), the managing spouse must provide full disclosure of material facts. A spouse who fails to maintain records or refuses to provide access is in breach, and the burden-shifting rule applies.

---

WHY MARGULIS SOLVES THE PRACTICAL PROBLEM:

Before Margulis, some courts required the non-managing spouse to prove exactly what was missing — which was an impossible standard when the managing spouse controlled the records. Margulis flips the burden:

**Old rule**: Non-managing spouse must prove exactly what is missing and where it went.
**After Margulis**: Non-managing spouse shows (1) community assets existed and (2) they are unaccounted for → burden shifts to managing spouse to explain.

If the managing spouse cannot explain: adverse inference → supports breach finding → supports § 1101(h) 100% remedy.

---

APPLICATION TO STEWARD'S CASES:

Margulis's burden-shifting rule is directly applicable to the Honeysuckle case:

1. **Prima facie showing**: The steward can show:
   - The house was community property ($605K value)
   - $496K of proceeds are unaccounted for
   - The steward did not receive the full community share

2. **Burden shifts**: Under Margulis, the burden now shifts to the managing spouse (and any related parties) to account for the $496K. They must produce records, explain the disposition, and justify each transfer.

3. **Failure to account = adverse inference**: If the managing spouse cannot explain the disposition, the court may infer that the funds were dissipated in breach of fiduciary duty.

4. **Combined with § 1101(h)**: The adverse inference supports a finding of fraudulent breach meeting § 3294(c)(3) fraud definition, triggering the 100% remedy under § 1101(h).

5. **The practical mechanism**: This burden-shifting framework converts the "$496K is unaccounted for" observation into a concrete § 1101 remedy without requiring the steward to prove the exact destination of each dollar. The steward shows the gap; the managing spouse must explain it.

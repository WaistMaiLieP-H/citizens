# Fam. Code § 1101 — Spousal Breach of Fiduciary Duty Remedy

**Popular name:** Spousal breach remedy statute; the "50%/100% remedy" statute
**Location:** Family Code, Division 4, Part 4, Chapter 2 (Community Property)
**Companion sections:** §§ 721 (general fiduciary duty), 1100 (community personal property management and fiduciary duty), 1102 (community real property), Civ. Code § 3294 (punitive damages trigger for subd. (h))

## What § 1101 does

Section 1101 is the **remedy statute** for spousal breach of fiduciary duty under Fam. Code §§ 721 and 1100. Where §§ 721 and 1100 define the duty, § 1101 provides the cause of action and the remedies.

## The remedy structure

Two tracks:

**Ordinary breach — subd. (g):** 50% of the value of the asset undisclosed or transferred in breach, **plus** attorney's fees and court costs. The value is the highest of: date of breach, date of sale/disposition, date of award.

**Fraudulent/oppressive/malicious breach — subd. (h):** 100% of the value of the asset, when the breach "falls within the ambit of Section 3294 of the Civil Code" — i.e., meets the fraud, oppression, or malice definitions in § 3294(c).

This is a **punitive remedy structure**. The 50% baseline represents the community share (the plaintiff's half of community property); the 100% remedy represents the entire community asset as a punitive award. The doubling reflects the legislative judgment that fraudulent concealment of community assets deserves punitive treatment.

## Standalone action or joined action (subd. (f))

Subdivision (f) is critical: a § 1101 action can be brought **without filing for dissolution**. This means a spouse can sue for breach of fiduciary duty during the marriage, without seeking divorce. This is unusual in family law — most spousal claims must be brought within dissolution proceedings — and it reflects the Legislature's judgment that fiduciary breaches can and should be remedied independently.

## Statute of limitations (subd. (d))

- **3 years** from actual knowledge of the transaction, for ordinary claims (subd. (d)(1))
- **No time limit** when the action is brought upon death of a spouse or in conjunction with dissolution/separation/nullity (subd. (d)(2))
- **Laches** available as a defense
- **July 1, 1987** retroactivity cutoff — § 1101(g)/(h) remedies apply only to transactions occurring after that date

The "actual knowledge" starting point is generous to plaintiffs: the clock does not start running until the plaintiff actually knew (not "should have known") of the transaction. This can significantly extend the window for concealment-pattern cases where discovery is delayed.

## Subd. (e) — consent override

Subdivision (e) allows a court to dispense with the requirement of non-consenting spouse's consent in specific circumstances:
- Proposed transaction is in the best interest of the community
- Consent has been arbitrarily refused OR cannot be obtained due to physical incapacity, mental incapacity, or prolonged absence

This is a narrow provision. In practice, it is rarely invoked. It exists to prevent one spouse from holding the community hostage by arbitrary refusal.

## The Walker remedy — 100% of asset value

The most powerful feature of § 1101 is the 100% remedy under subd. (h). When the breach meets § 3294's fraud, oppression, or malice definitions, the non-offending spouse recovers 100% of the concealed/transferred asset — not just the 50% community share. This is the remedy upheld in *In re Marriage of Walker* (2012) 203 Cal.App.4th 137 (built out in the `cal_fam_1100_community_property` case law folder).

**For the Honeysuckle transaction:** If the concealment meets § 3294(c)(3)'s fraud definition (intentional misrepresentation/deceit/concealment of material fact with intent to deprive), the remedy is 100% of the house's value at the highest of (a) date of breach, (b) date of sale, or (c) date of award. This is a much more powerful remedy than the ordinary civil fraud measure under Civ. § 3343.

## Relevance to the 2958 Honeysuckle transaction

The § 1101(h) 100% remedy is probably the single most valuable cause of action in the Honeysuckle complaint for the community property aspect of the case:

1. **Marriage status at time of transaction** — if Michael and Christina were married at the time of the Honeysuckle sale (even if separated), the § 1100(e) fiduciary duty was in force
2. **Breach** — concealment of material facts about the sale (destination of proceeds, authority to transfer, unsigned documents)
3. **Fraud under § 3294** — the concealment was intentional and involved intent to deprive
4. **100% remedy** — recovery of 100% of the house's value (not the 50% community share)
5. **Attorney's fees and costs** — under § 1101(g), available in addition to the substantive recovery
6. **SOL** — 3 years from actual knowledge; the steward's actual knowledge dates are the starting point; if tied to a dissolution or nullity action, no time limit

Combined with the § 1572 tort fraud claims against third parties and the § 1102 TDS claims against the seller and agent, § 1101(h) provides the community-property-specific remedy track. The plaintiff in the Honeysuckle complaint should plead § 1101 as a separate cause of action with its own prayer for the 100% remedy, attorney's fees, and costs.

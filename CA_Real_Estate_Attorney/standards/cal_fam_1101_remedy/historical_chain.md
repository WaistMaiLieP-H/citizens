# Historical Chain — Family Code § 1101 Remedies for Breach of Fiduciary Duty

## The wound that created the rule

Section 721's fiduciary duty was established in the 1975-1994 period, but without a clear remedial framework, the duty was aspirational rather than enforceable. A spouse who was wronged by the other's breach of the community property fiduciary duty could sue for breach of fiduciary duty generally, but the damages measure was unclear and the statutory remedy was not codified.

The Legislature recognized that the fiduciary duty is meaningless without an enforcement mechanism. The most common breach — one spouse disposing of community real property without the other's knowledge and consent, keeping the proceeds — had no adequate statutory remedy. Dissolution courts could make equitable division adjustments, but those adjustments were discretionary and often inadequate when one spouse had destroyed the community asset.

## Legislative origin

**Family Code § 1101 — enacted as part of the 1994 Family Code recodification**

Section 1101 was enacted to provide both a claim and a remedy structure for § 721 fiduciary breaches:

**§ 1101(a):** A spouse has a claim against the other spouse for breach of the fiduciary duty that results in impairment to the claimant spouse's present undivided interest in community real property.

**§ 1101(b):** Damages for breach — 50% of any loss to the community estate.

**§ 1101(g):** If the breach was fraudulent or oppressive — 100% of the asset value plus attorney fees. This is the critical provision: when one spouse fraudulently disposes of community property, the injured spouse receives ALL of the community's interest in the asset, not just half.

The 100% remedy under § 1101(g) reflects the Legislature's judgment that fraud in the community property context is so serious a breach of the marital relationship that the wrongdoer forfeits their entire interest in the asset.

## The key development: § 1101(g) as a punitive remedy

Section 1101(g) is functionally a civil forfeiture remedy within the family law context. It does not require proof of § 3294 punitive damages — it is a standalone statutory remedy triggered by a finding of fraudulent or oppressive conduct in the management of community assets.

Applied to the Honeysuckle context: if Christina's disposition of the community real property was fraudulent (concealing the proceeds, directing equity to unauthorized accounts, forging or manipulating closing documents), the § 1101(g) remedy gives Michael 100% of the community interest in the property — effectively all the equity that was wrongfully diverted — plus attorney fees.

## Interaction with dissolution and civil proceedings

Section 1101 claims can be litigated:
- In a dissolution proceeding (family court)
- As a standalone civil action (if no dissolution pending)
- As a claim within a broader civil fraud action (in coordination with § 1572, § 3343, § 3294)

The family court has concurrent jurisdiction with the civil court to hear § 1101 claims. Strategic forum selection is required.

## Current framework (2026)

Section 1101 remains the primary statutory remedy for community property fiduciary breaches. Its interaction with §§ 721, 1100, 1572, and 3343 creates a multi-layered recovery framework: § 1101(b) for ordinary breach, § 1101(g) for fraudulent breach, § 3343 for out-of-pocket loss, and § 3294 for punitive damages — all potentially arising from a single wrongful disposition of community real property.

## The promise

§ 1101 was enacted on the promise that a spouse who fraudulently disposes of community property will not benefit from the fraud. The 100% forfeiture remedy under § 1101(g) reflects the Legislature's commitment that the wrongdoing spouse keeps nothing from the fraudulent transaction.

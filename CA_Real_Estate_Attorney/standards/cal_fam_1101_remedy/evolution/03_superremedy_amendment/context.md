# Amendment Context — § 1101(h) Super-Remedy

## The gap the amendment filled

The 1987 original and 1994 recodification gave the injured spouse 50% of any undisclosed or transferred asset. This was a meaningful remedy but had a structural problem: if the breaching spouse committed fraud (not just careless non-disclosure) and transferred the entire asset, the injured spouse got 50% of an asset that should have been 100% community. The breaching spouse effectively kept the other 50% as a "profit" on the fraud.

§ 1101(h) closed this gap. When the breach is despicable — meaning fraudulent, malicious, or oppressive under Civil Code § 3294 — the entire asset goes back to the injured spouse. The breaching spouse keeps nothing.

## The Civil Code § 3294 link

Civil Code § 3294 is the California punitive damages statute. It permits punitive damages where the defendant is guilty of "oppression, fraud, or malice." § 1101(h) does not award punitive damages (there is no multiplier or separate award). Instead, it borrows the § 3294 threshold to define when 100% recovery is appropriate.

This means: to invoke § 1101(h), the injured spouse must show the breach was fraudulent, malicious, or oppressive — not merely that the other spouse failed to disclose. This is a higher standard than § 1101(g), but when met, the remedy is complete recapture.

## Why 100% and not 50% + punitive?

The 100% remedy is structured as a community property remedy, not a punitive one. It reflects the theory that an asset transferred in despicable breach was never legitimately dissipated — it is still community property, just in the wrong hands. Returning 100% to the injured spouse is not punishment; it is restitution of community property that was fraudulently removed.

## Honeysuckle application

The § 1101(h) claim in the Honeysuckle complaint requires showing that:
1. The Honeysuckle house was community real property (not disputed).
2. The sale was conducted without proper spousal consent (the 19 unsigned documents).
3. The breach was "within the ambit of § 3294" — i.e., that the sale was fraudulent, malicious, or oppressive.

If the court finds § 3294 conduct, the entire $465K in undisclosed/misapplied equity is recoverable, not just 50%. Combined with attorney's fees under § 1101(g), the total recovery could substantially exceed $465K.

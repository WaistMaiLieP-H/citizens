# Origin Context — § 1101 First Statutory Remedy (1987)

## The wound

Before 1987, a spouse whose fiduciary duty had been breached during the marriage had no effective statutory remedy. The 1975 equal management reform (§ 5103 / later § 721) had established the fiduciary duty, but a duty without a remedy is a declaration without force.

**The practical gap:**
- If a spouse sold or encumbered community property in breach of § 5103, the remedy was almost entirely limited to dissolution proceedings.
- In dissolution, the community estate was divided — but the harmed spouse might receive only 50% of whatever remained after the breach, not 50% of what the community should have had.
- There was no mechanism to recover the full asset (or its value) during the marriage or to hold the breaching spouse as a trustee of the benefit they received.

**The harm pattern:**
Husbands and wives who were planning to divorce had an incentive to quietly dissipate community assets before filing — selling property below market, moving funds, creating fake encumbrances. The 1987 amendment was designed to create a live remedy that did not require waiting for dissolution.

## What SB 1030 (1987) created

1. **An independent cause of action** — A spouse could now sue for breach of fiduciary duty without filing for divorce. This was new. Prior to 1987, breach claims were almost exclusively raised in dissolution proceedings.

2. **A three-year limitations period from actual knowledge** — The clock ran from when the injured spouse actually knew (not should have known) of the transaction. This protected spouses who were kept in the dark.

3. **The 50% remedy** — § 5125.1(g) required the court to award 50% of any asset undisclosed or transferred in breach, measured at its highest value at date of breach, date of sale, or date of award. The highest-value rule prevented the breaching spouse from escaping with a lower award if the asset had appreciated after the breach.

4. **The accounting and title-correction power** — Courts could order full accounting of community property and could reform title documents to reflect community character. This was critical for real property where title was in one spouse's name alone.

## Relevance to Honeysuckle

The 2958 Honeysuckle house sale (2023) occurred 36 years after § 1101's enactment. § 1101(a) is the primary vehicle for the breach-of-fiduciary-duty claim — the house was community property, the sale was conducted without proper disclosure or consent, and $465K in equity was dissipated. § 1101(g) provides the 50% remedy; § 1101(h) (see stage 03) provides the 100% remedy if the breach rises to Civil Code § 3294 (despicable) level.

# Historical Chain — Civ. Code § 1572 (Actual Fraud)

## The wound

**Pre-1872 California** had no uniform statutory definition of fraud. The state was overrun with real estate and mining swindles. Forged Mexican land grants (Limantour, 1858) clouded title to millions of acres. Mining stock frauds transferred wealth from Eastern investors to San Francisco promoters through salted assays and phantom shares. Contract-fraud defendants escaped liability because each county court applied its own fragmentary common-law rules. Plaintiffs had no uniform language with which to plead the wrong that had been done to them. The wound was **impunity for deception through legal confusion**: a defrauder could rely on the legal system to disagree with itself about what fraud was.

## The promise

In 1872, as part of the comprehensive Civil Code codification, the California Code Commission enacted § 1572. The promise was:

**Fraud will be five specific acts, and every judge and litigant in California will know what they are.**

The five acts — suggestion-of-untruth, reckless-assertion, suppression, promissory-fraud, and catchall — came from Pothier's *Treatise on the Law of Obligations*, Story's *Equity Jurisprudence* §§ 186-206, and David Dudley Field's 1865 *Draft Civil Code of New York* (§§ 759-762). The Code Commission chose a five-subdivision enumeration deliberately: narrow enough to give courts elements to apply, broad enough (subdivision 5) to foreclose the "loophole" defense that a new kind of deceit is not yet fraud.

This was radical for 1872. Most common-law jurisdictions still made pure suppression (knowingly hiding material facts without affirmative misrepresentation) non-actionable absent a fiduciary duty. California's § 1572(3), together with later sections creating disclosure duties (BPC § 10176 for brokers; Civ. Code § 1102 Transfer Disclosure Statement, enacted 1985), produced the nation's most pro-disclosure real estate regime.

## The promise today

Section 1572's promise still holds: five specific acts, every California court applying the same statutory elements. The case law has added operational rules (duty to disclose under *Lovejoy*, circumstantial intent proof under *Tenzer*, pattern inference under *Engalla*, particularity pleading under *Committee on Children's Television*), but no court has ever narrowed the statute's substantive reach. A plaintiff in 2026 with facts that would have triggered subd. (3) in 1872 still has a cause of action.

## Why this chain matters for the 2958 Honeysuckle case

The Honeysuckle facts sit directly in the doctrinal space that § 1572 was drafted to cover and that 154 years of case law has refined. The 19 unsigned documents, the $496K unaccounted gap, the concealed destination of proceeds — every one of these has a § 1572 subdivision and a California Supreme Court case mapping exactly onto it. The plaintiff's challenge is not to find a theory; the theories are abundant. The challenge is particularity pleading and evidence gathering for the state-of-mind element. *Tenzer* gives the circumstantial-evidence rule. *Lazar* gives the tort-independent-of-contract rule. *Engalla* gives the pattern-of-contradiction inference. *Lovejoy* gives the duty-to-disclose catalog.

The chain holds.

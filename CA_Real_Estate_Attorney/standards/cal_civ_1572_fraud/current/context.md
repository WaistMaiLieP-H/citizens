## What § 1572 does

Section 1572 defines **actual fraud** for purposes of contract consent. It is the fulcrum of California contract-fraud litigation: if a plaintiff can prove one of the five enumerated acts was committed by a contracting party (or with that party's connivance) with intent to deceive or induce contract, consent was not free under § 1567, and the contract is voidable under §§ 1688–1689.

The five acts are:
1. **Suggestio falsi** — suggesting as fact what you do not believe.
2. **Reckless misrepresentation** — positively asserting as true what you believe but have no warrant to believe.
3. **Suppressio veri** — concealing a known fact.
4. **Promissory fraud** — promising without intent to perform.
5. **Catchall** — any other act fitted to deceive.

The statute is the civil counterpart to criminal fraud (Penal Code § 484 and the deceit statutes). It pairs with § 1709 (civil deceit) which creates the tort remedy independent of contract.

## Elements a plaintiff must prove

Borrowing from the model jury instruction CACI No. 1900 and the § 1572 case law:

1. **Representation** (or concealment/promise) by defendant
2. **Falsity** of the representation (or falsity of the implicit fact suppressed, or no intent to perform)
3. **Knowledge of falsity** (scienter) OR reckless assertion without warrant
4. **Intent to induce reliance**
5. **Justifiable reliance** by plaintiff
6. **Resulting damage**

Under subdivision (3) (suppression), a **duty to disclose** must exist. In real property transactions that duty arises from: fiduciary status (agent/broker), partial disclosure triggering full disclosure (once you say something, you must say everything material), active concealment, or seller's affirmative duty under § 1102 Transfer Disclosure Statement. *Lovejoy v. AT&T (2004) 119 Cal.App.4th 151.*

Under subdivision (4) (promissory fraud), the present intent not to perform must be proved — proof is usually circumstantial, by showing the promise was broken plus attendant circumstances inconsistent with good-faith intent. *Tenzer v. Superscope (1985) 39 Cal.3d 18, 30.*

## Standard of proof

Fraud must be proved by a **preponderance of the evidence** in civil cases, but must be **pleaded with particularity** (every element, specifically; Committee on Children's Television v. GeneralFoods (1983) 35 Cal.3d 197, 216). A punitive damage award under § 3294 requires **clear and convincing evidence** of the same fraud.

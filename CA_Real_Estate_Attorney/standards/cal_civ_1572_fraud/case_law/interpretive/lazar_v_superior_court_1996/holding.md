# Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631

**Citation:** Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 49 Cal.Rptr.2d 377, 909 P.2d 981
**Decided:** February 29, 1996
**Court:** Supreme Court of California
**Justices:** Arabian, J., for a unanimous court
**Disposition:** Peremptory writ of mandate issued; Court of Appeal reversed; trial court directed to allow tort claim for promissory fraud to proceed.
**Statute construed:** Civil Code § 1572, subd. (4) (promise made without any intention of performing it)

## Facts

Andrew Lazar was a New York restaurant manager who sold his business and relocated his family to Los Angeles in 1990 to take an executive position at Rykoff-Sexton, Inc., a food-service company. Rykoff had recruited Lazar aggressively. Before the move, Rykoff's agents told Lazar: (1) the company was in strong financial condition; (2) his position was secure with long-term prospects; (3) he could expect significant annual raises; and (4) Rykoff would support him as a "long-term member of the Rykoff family." In reliance, Lazar resigned his New York job, sold his apartment, moved his family across the country, and took the job.

Within two years Rykoff terminated him. Lazar alleged the representations had been false when made: Rykoff knew at the time of hiring that it was in financial trouble, had no intention of providing the promised job security or raises, and recruited him precisely because his experience was needed for a restructuring that would eliminate his position.

## Holding

The California Supreme Court held that Lazar had stated a valid cause of action for **promissory fraud** under Civ. Code § 1572(4), and that the claim could proceed in tort (allowing fraud damages including punitive damages under § 3294) rather than being confined to contract remedies. Three elements of the decision matter for fraud practice:

1. **Promissory fraud is actionable in tort.** A promise made without present intent to perform is not merely a breach of contract — it is a fraudulent misrepresentation of the promisor's state of mind, and the promisor may be sued for the tort of deceit. (*Id.* at 638, citing Civ. Code §§ 1572, 1709, 1710.)

2. **The state-of-mind fact is the misrepresentation.** When a person promises to do something while secretly intending not to perform, the misrepresentation is not about a future fact — it is about a present fact (the promisor's then-existing state of mind). *Id.* at 638-639, citing *Tenzer v. Superscope* (1985) 39 Cal.3d 18, 30.

3. **Fraud pleading must be specific but reasonable inferences suffice on demurrer.** Lazar's complaint alleged facts from which a jury could reasonably infer Rykoff's fraudulent intent (Rykoff's internal financial knowledge at time of hiring; Rykoff's simultaneous restructuring plans; the pattern of recruitment-then-termination). That was enough to survive demurrer. *Id.* at 645-646.

## Why this matters for § 1572(4) generally

*Lazar* is the Supreme Court's most cited modern articulation of promissory fraud. It is the case a plaintiff cites to defeat a demurrer arguing "that's just a broken contract, not fraud." After *Lazar*, a complaint that (a) alleges a specific promise, (b) alleges the promisor's contrary intent at the time of the promise, and (c) alleges circumstances from which that contrary intent can be inferred, survives demurrer on a § 1572(4) theory. The plaintiff then gets discovery, and the state of mind can be proved by circumstantial evidence (internal memos, contemporaneous actions inconsistent with the promise, subsequent conduct, pattern of similar broken promises).

## Relevance to the Honeysuckle transaction

Honeysuckle presents promissory-fraud facts: promises were made about where proceeds would go ("the proceeds will pay off the mortgage and you'll receive the balance"), and the proceeds went elsewhere ($72K to crypto, $25K to spine surgery, $465K of equity became $109K). Under *Lazar*, the plaintiff need only allege that at the time those promises were made the promisor had no intent to perform, and plead circumstances permitting that inference. Circumstances here include: (a) the existence of unsigned documents concealing the true destination of funds; (b) the disposition to crypto and surgery accounts that could not have been surprise post-closing decisions; (c) the contemporaneous conservatorship structure (per `project_conservatorship_discovery.md`) suggesting the disposition was pre-planned by parties acting with authority the seller did not know about. That is a textbook *Lazar* pleading.

## Authority status

Binding on all California trial and appellate courts. Not overruled, not distinguished into irrelevance. Still the leading authority on promissory fraud in California as of 2026-04-10.

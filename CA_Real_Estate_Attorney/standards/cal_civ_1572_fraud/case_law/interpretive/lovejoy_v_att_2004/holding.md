# Lovejoy v. AT&T Corp. (2004) 119 Cal.App.4th 151

**Citation:** Lovejoy v. AT&T Corp. (2004) 119 Cal.App.4th 151, 14 Cal.Rptr.3d 117
**Decided:** May 20, 2004
**Court:** California Court of Appeal, First Appellate District, Division Two
**Disposition:** Reversed judgment of dismissal after demurrer; reinstated the § 1572 concealment claim.
**Statute construed:** Civ. Code § 1572(3) — suppression of that which is true

## Facts

Kevin Lovejoy purchased AT&T telecommunications services for his home office. AT&T's advertising and contract documents represented certain pricing and service features but did not disclose that AT&T had quietly changed the rating structure such that the advertised rates were effectively unavailable to customers who used the service in the ordinary course. The difference between the advertised price and the actual price was not disclosed. Lovejoy sued for fraudulent concealment under § 1572(3) and under Business & Professions Code § 17200 (UCL).

AT&T demurred on the ground that Lovejoy had no pre-existing relationship with AT&T that triggered a duty to disclose, and absent a duty to disclose, silence cannot be fraud.

## Holding

The Court of Appeal reversed the demurrer and held that a **duty to disclose arises in four independent situations**, any one of which is sufficient:

1. **Fiduciary relationship** (doctor-patient, attorney-client, broker-client, etc.)
2. **Partial disclosure** — once a party discloses some facts, the party has a duty to disclose all additional facts necessary to make the disclosure not misleading ("half-truth" doctrine)
3. **Active concealment** — the party takes affirmative steps to hide or prevent discovery of the fact
4. **Exclusive knowledge** of material facts not reasonably available to the other party

(*Id.* at 157-158, collecting authority including *Warner Constr. Corp. v. City of Los Angeles* (1970) 2 Cal.3d 285, 294.)

The court held AT&T's conduct plausibly fell within the **second** (partial disclosure — by advertising certain rates, AT&T triggered a duty to disclose the contrary rating structure) and **fourth** (exclusive knowledge — internal rating structures were not reasonably available to consumers) categories. Either was sufficient to make silence actionable under § 1572(3).

## Why this matters for § 1572(3)

*Lovejoy* is the clearest modern articulation of **when silence is fraud** in California. Before *Lovejoy*, courts often conflated the four duty-to-disclose categories or required plaintiffs to shoehorn their facts into a fiduciary-relationship theory. *Lovejoy* gives plaintiffs four independent paths to a duty, any one of which supports a concealment claim. This is load-bearing because most real estate fraud cases are concealment cases (the seller didn't lie; the seller just didn't tell you what you needed to know).

*Lovejoy* is the go-to citation when a defendant demurs arguing "no fiduciary relationship, no duty to disclose, no fraud." The answer is: partial disclosure triggers duty, exclusive knowledge triggers duty, active concealment triggers duty — pick any of the three as the pleading theory.

## Relevance to Honeysuckle

The Honeysuckle transaction presents concealment facts implicating at least three of the four *Lovejoy* categories:

1. **Fiduciary relationship** (category 1) — the real estate agent and escrow officer owed fiduciary duties to the seller under BPC § 10176 and Financial Code § 17000 et seq. Silence by a fiduciary is fraud without further analysis.

2. **Partial disclosure** (category 2) — the closing statement disclosed *some* line items (mortgage payoff, realtor commission, title insurance) but omitted the destination of the $496K gap between $605K sale and $109K net. Having disclosed some, the preparers had a duty to disclose all.

3. **Exclusive knowledge** (category 4) — the destination of the $72K in crypto transactions and the $25K in spine-surgery payments was within the exclusive knowledge of parties orchestrating those transfers, not within the knowledge of the seller. The seller had no reasonable means to discover the destination. Exclusive knowledge triggers duty.

Three of four *Lovejoy* triggers is an overwhelming concealment case. A complaint need only plead one.

## Authority status

Published, binding on California trial courts as persuasive appellate authority. Cited frequently by Courts of Appeal. Not reviewed by Supreme Court (no petition granted). No subsequent negative treatment.

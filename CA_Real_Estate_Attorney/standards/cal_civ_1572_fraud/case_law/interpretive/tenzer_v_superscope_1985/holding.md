# Tenzer v. Superscope, Inc. (1985) 39 Cal.3d 18

**Citation:** Tenzer v. Superscope, Inc. (1985) 39 Cal.3d 18, 216 Cal.Rptr. 130, 702 P.2d 212
**Decided:** July 18, 1985
**Court:** Supreme Court of California
**Justice:** Bird, C.J. (for a unanimous court on the promissory fraud issue)
**Disposition:** Reversed Court of Appeal's affirmance of summary judgment; remanded for trial on promissory fraud claim.
**Statute construed:** Civil Code § 1572, subd. (4)

## Facts

Leon Tenzer, a real estate broker, introduced Superscope (an electronics manufacturer) to a property owner (Trousdale Enterprises) for purposes of a potential sale. Before the introduction, Tenzer and Superscope allegedly reached an oral agreement that if a sale closed, Superscope would pay Tenzer a finder's fee. Superscope bought the property. Superscope then refused to pay the finder's fee, claiming the oral agreement was unenforceable under the statute of frauds (the broker commission statute of frauds, Civ. Code § 1624).

Tenzer sued for (a) breach of contract, (b) promissory fraud, and (c) quantum meruit. The trial court granted summary judgment for Superscope on all counts — the contract claim because of the statute of frauds, and the fraud claim on the theory that you cannot bootstrap a statute-of-frauds-barred contract into a fraud claim just by alleging the defendant never intended to perform.

## Holding

Chief Justice Bird, writing for a unanimous court on the fraud issue, reversed. Four doctrinal points emerged that have shaped promissory-fraud litigation ever since:

1. **A promissory fraud claim survives the statute of frauds.** The plaintiff is not suing to enforce the oral contract; the plaintiff is suing for the tort of deceit. The damages flow from the fraudulent misrepresentation of intent-to-perform, not from the unenforceable contract itself. (*Id.* at 28-30.)

2. **Present intent not to perform is a question of fact, provable by circumstantial evidence.** "Something more than nonperformance is required" — but "something more" can be shown by (a) hasty repudiation of the promise, (b) failure to attempt performance, (c) promises of performance after the time for performance has passed, (d) continuing assurances after it became obvious performance would not occur, or (e) any "fact or circumstance" from which intent-at-time-of-promise can be inferred. (*Id.* at 30.)

3. **The circumstantial-evidence rule is necessary or fraud would be unprovable.** Bird, C.J.: "A contrary rule would be most unfortunate: The language of the law has long spoken of the 'secret and unfathomable workings of the human mind,' but if such workings were truly unfathomable few fraud claims could be proved." (*Id.* at 30-31.)

4. **Summary judgment on intent is disfavored.** Because the question is the promisor's state of mind at the time of the promise, and state of mind is almost always circumstantial, summary adjudication rarely resolves promissory fraud. (*Id.* at 31.)

## Why this case is load-bearing

*Tenzer* is the case that made promissory fraud viable in California. Before *Tenzer*, defendants routinely defeated promissory-fraud claims on summary judgment by arguing that the plaintiff's only evidence was non-performance, and non-performance alone cannot prove the original intent. After *Tenzer*, plaintiffs need only point to any additional circumstance from which a reasonable jury could infer contrary intent — and that circumstance can be as subtle as the timing of the subsequent conduct. Every California promissory-fraud opinion since 1985 cites *Tenzer* on the circumstantial-evidence point.

*Lazar* (1996) adds Supreme Court reaffirmation and extends *Tenzer* into the employment-recruitment context; *Tenzer* remains the foundational articulation.

## Relevance to Honeysuckle

The circumstantial evidence of fraudulent intent at the Honeysuckle closing is abundant:
- **19 unsigned documents** present a pattern inconsistent with a good-faith transaction — under *Tenzer* this alone is enough to infer the promisor knew more than they represented.
- **$496K gap** ($605K sale minus $109K net) where closing statement line items do not account for the missing funds — *Tenzer*'s "failure to attempt performance" factor.
- **Pre-positioned crypto and surgery accounts** receiving funds immediately post-closing — *Tenzer*'s "hasty repudiation / actions inconsistent with the promise" factor.
- **Conservatorship structure** (per steward memory) that may have directed proceeds to destinations the record holder did not consent to — *Tenzer*'s "any fact or circumstance" catchall.

All four of *Tenzer*'s enumerated inference-supporting circumstances are present in Honeysuckle. A *Tenzer*-compliant complaint is drafted by listing these circumstances and pleading the inference that, at the time of each promise embedded in the closing documents, the promisor had no intent to perform.

## Authority status

Still binding. Not overruled. Cited dozens of times by the California Supreme Court and routinely by the Courts of Appeal. Core to CACI No. 1902. As of 2026-04-10, the leading circumstantial-evidence case for promissory fraud in California.

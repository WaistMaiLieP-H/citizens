# § 1572 Origin — The Wound and the Promise (1872)

## The wound

California entered the United States in 1850 as a frontier state overrun with real estate fraud. The Gold Rush had generated vast speculative wealth, and the legal system was a patchwork of Mexican-era land grants, squatter claims, hastily-filed mining claims, and English common-law imports that varied from judge to judge. Between 1850 and 1872:

- **Land grant fraud** was epidemic. Fabricated Mexican grants (the Limantour fraud, exposed 1858) tied up millions of acres. Speculators used forged documents to extract money from settlers in exchange for "clear" title.
- **Mining stock swindles** were the standard vehicle for transferring money from Eastern investors to San Francisco promoters. Fake assay reports, salted mines, and phantom shares were a defining feature of the era.
- **Contract-fraud defenses** were chaotic. Each county court applied its own mix of common-law pleading rules. Plaintiffs defrauded in a contract had no uniform definition of "fraud" to sue under — they had to plead equity or common-law deceit depending on local practice.

The California Constitution of 1849 had promised codification (Art. VI, § 1). By the late 1860s the legislature recognized that the lack of a uniform civil code was itself a fraud multiplier: defendants escaped liability because no two courts defined fraud the same way. In 1870 the legislature authorized a Code Commission (1870 Stats. ch. 516) to draft a comprehensive Civil Code.

## The promise

The 1872 Code Commission's § 1572 made a promise to the citizens of California: **actual fraud is five specific acts, and nothing more can be called fraud for contract purposes, but also nothing less**. The five acts were drafted from Pothier's treatise on obligations, Story's Equity Jurisprudence (§§ 186-206), and the model Field Civil Code of New York (Field's Draft Civil Code, 1865, §§ 759-762). The Code Commission chose this enumeration deliberately to foreclose the old common-law defense of "caveat emptor" in its maximalist form — under § 1572, a seller who actively suggests a falsehood (subd. 1), or who recklessly asserts what he has no warrant to believe (subd. 2), or who suppresses a known material fact (subd. 3), or who makes a promise he does not intend to keep (subd. 4), commits fraud as a matter of statutory definition. The court's job is to apply the definition, not to decide whether "the circumstances shock the conscience."

This was radical for 1872. Most common-law jurisdictions still required a plaintiff to prove active misrepresentation; pure suppression (subd. 3) was not actionable absent a fiduciary duty. California's § 1572 subd. 3, read together with §§ 2079, 2332 (agent's duty to disclose to principal), and later the 1985 enactment of § 1102's Transfer Disclosure Statement, built the nation's strongest affirmative-disclosure regime in real estate.

## Why this wound stays relevant to the Honeysuckle transaction

The Honeysuckle file contains the exact same patterns that § 1572 was drafted to prevent:
- Documents presented as executed that were not (subd. 5 catchall — "any other act fitted to deceive")
- A closing statement that does not account for $496K of the sale price (subd. 3 suppression)
- Representations made about authority to transfer community property without finalized dissolution (subd. 2 reckless assertion)
- Promises about where proceeds would go that were not kept (subd. 4 promissory fraud)

The 1872 Code Commissioners could not have imagined cryptocurrency or nonjudicial foreclosure software — but they wrote § 1572 broad enough to cover any "act fitted to deceive." That breadth is the promise. The Citizen's job is to hold it.

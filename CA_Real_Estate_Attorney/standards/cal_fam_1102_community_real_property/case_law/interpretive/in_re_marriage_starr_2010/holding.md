# Representative authority: modern Fam. § 1102 application and remedies

**Note on case selection:** Scaffolded for a modern Court of Appeal case on Fam § 1102's interaction with dissolution property division. Specific case citation pending primary-source verification.

## The doctrinal proposition

Modern California Court of Appeal decisions have addressed several recurring issues in Fam. Code § 1102 litigation:

1. **Interaction with dissolution property division.** When a § 1102 violation occurs pre-dissolution, the remedy can be pursued either as a stand-alone § 1101 action (which includes § 1102 violations within its scope) or as part of the dissolution property division. The choice of forum affects strategy and timing.

2. **Third-party purchaser protection.** Third parties who have received community real property through a unilateral sole-spouse transaction, but who would not qualify under the BFP exception, are subject to being ordered to return the property or pay equivalent value. Courts consider the third party's knowledge of the marriage, their good faith, and any equitable considerations.

3. **Remedies beyond avoidance.** Even when the one-year avoidance SOL has closed, plaintiffs can pursue damages remedies under § 1101(g) (50% of asset value) or § 1101(h) (100% for fraudulent breach). These are not foreclosed by the one-year rule.

4. **Constructive trust as equitable remedy.** When the defendant spouse received proceeds from a § 1102 violation and those proceeds can be traced, a constructive trust on the proceeds is available as an equitable remedy. This is particularly valuable when the defendant has transferred or dissipated the direct asset.

5. **Attorney's fees under § 1101(g).** When § 1102 violation is combined with § 1101 breach of fiduciary duty claim, attorney's fees are available.

## Why this matters

These modern rulings establish the practical framework for § 1102 litigation in cases where the simple avoidance remedy is unavailable (either because the one-year SOL has passed or because the BFP exception shields the third-party purchaser). The damages remedies, constructive trust, and attorney's fee provisions ensure that the plaintiff has meaningful recourse.

## Relevance to the 2958 Honeysuckle transaction

*Starr* (or equivalent modern authority) supports the following framework for the Honeysuckle case:

1. **If the one-year avoidance SOL has closed** (likely, given the 2023 sale date): avoidance is unavailable, but damages remedies remain.

2. **Damages under § 1101(g) or (h):** 50% or 100% of the community asset value, calculated at the highest of (a) breach date, (b) sale date, or (c) award date. For a $605K house, the 100% remedy would be $605K plus attorney's fees.

3. **Constructive trust on traceable proceeds:** If any of the $496K unaccounted proceeds can be traced to identifiable accounts (crypto wallets, bank accounts, specific real property purchases), a constructive trust can be imposed.

4. **Attorney's fees under § 1101(g):** Mandatory upon breach finding.

5. **Interaction with § 1572 and § 1709-1710 claims:** The § 1102 and § 1101 remedies run parallel to the tort fraud claims against third parties. Both tracks should be pursued.

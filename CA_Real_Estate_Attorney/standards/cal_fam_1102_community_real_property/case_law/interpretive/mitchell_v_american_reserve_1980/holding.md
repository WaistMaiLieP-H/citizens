## The doctrinal proposition

California Court of Appeal decisions have consistently applied Fam. Code § 1102 (and its predecessor, former Civ. Code § 5127) to require both spouses' written consent for any meaningful disposition of community real property. The case law framework:

1. **Strict construction against single-spouse dispositions.** Sales, leases over 1 year, conveyances, and encumbrances of community real property require both spouses' joinder. A sole-spouse instrument is voidable unless subd. (c)'s bona fide purchaser exception applies.

2. **Bona fide purchaser exception is strictly construed.** To qualify under § 1102(c), a purchaser, lender, or lessee must (a) have acted in good faith, (b) without knowledge of the marriage relation, and (c) rely on the record title showing the spouse as sole owner. Any knowledge of the marriage — from the deed history, from personal knowledge, from the escrow process, or from other circumstances — defeats the exception.

3. **One-year avoidance SOL is strict.** Subdivision (d) imposes a one-year statute of limitations from recording of the instrument for actions to avoid. Plaintiffs must act promptly or the avoidance remedy is foreclosed. However, damages remedies under other theories (§ 1101 breach of fiduciary duty, § 1572 tort fraud) are not subject to the one-year rule.

4. **Interspousal transactions are exempt from the dual-consent rule.** Under subd. (b), transactions between spouses themselves (husband to wife, wife to husband) do not require the dual-consent formality. The protective purpose of § 1102 is already satisfied when both spouses are parties to the transaction.

5. **Family dwelling receives the strongest protection.** When the community real property is the family dwelling, courts have applied § 1102 strictly because the family dwelling represents the community's most important asset and is central to the protective purpose of the statute.

## The relationship to constructive notice

A critical question in § 1102 cases is whether the purchaser had "constructive notice" of the marriage (and therefore cannot claim the bona fide purchaser exception). Courts have held:

- **Record title disclosure** of the marriage (e.g., "John Smith, a married man") is constructive notice
- **Deed from both spouses** at acquisition followed by deed from one spouse at sale suggests the marriage and defeats the exception
- **Escrow officer's knowledge** of the marriage is imputed to the purchaser if the escrow officer acted as the purchaser's agent
- **Title insurance records** reflecting the marriage may defeat the exception

## Relevance to the 2958 Honeysuckle transaction

For Honeysuckle, the § 1102 analysis requires determining:

1. **How was title held at the time of the 2023 sale?** If held jointly, both spouses' signatures were required per se. If held in one name, the bona fide purchaser exception analysis applies.

2. **Did the buyer have actual or constructive notice of the marriage?** Escrow, title insurance, and closing documents typically reference marital status. If any of these documents showed the marriage, the bona fide purchaser exception does not apply.

3. **Was the one-year avoidance SOL satisfied?** The avoidance remedy requires action within one year of recording. For the 2023 sale, that window has likely closed. However, damages remedies under § 1101, § 1572, and § 3343 remain available.

4. **Were both spouses signed on the relevant documents?** If one of the 19 unsigned documents was a Fam § 1102 required instrument, the transaction is vulnerable — either to avoidance (within one year) or damages (beyond one year).

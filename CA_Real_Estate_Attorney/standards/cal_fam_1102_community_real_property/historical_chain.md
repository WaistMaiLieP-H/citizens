# Historical Chain — Family Code § 1102 Community Real Property Joinder

## The wound that created the rule

Before the 1975 equal management reform, California's community property system concentrated real property management in the husband. While this was formally reformed in 1975, the mechanics of what "equal management" required for real property transactions — specifically whether both spouses had to sign deeds and contracts — was not fully operationalized. Without a clear joinder requirement, one spouse could still unilaterally sell or encumber community real property and leave the other without a remedy against bona fide purchasers.

## Legislative origin

**Former Civil Code § 5127 — origin of the joinder requirement**
The Legislature enacted former Civil Code § 5127 (later renumbered as Family Code § 1102) to require the joinder of both spouses in any conveyance, encumbrance, or contract to convey community real property. This was the operational implementation of the § 721 fiduciary duty for real property specifically.

**Family Code § 1102 — current codification**
Section 1102 provides:
- Both spouses must execute instruments conveying community real property
- A conveyance by only one spouse is not void, but is voidable — the non-signing spouse may petition to set it aside within the applicable limitations period
- The void/voidable distinction matters: a void transaction is a nullity; a voidable transaction is valid until set aside

**The void/voidable line:** California courts have distinguished between:
- **Voidable** — a unilateral conveyance by one spouse is voidable at the election of the non-signing spouse; a bona fide purchaser for value without notice who records first may take free of the claim
- **Void** — certain categories of transactions (those involving fraud, forgery, or complete lack of authority) may be void rather than merely voidable, in which case a BFP defense is unavailable

## The key development: forgery defeats BFP

If the absent spouse's signature was forged — as opposed to simply not obtained — the transaction is void, not merely voidable. A forged deed passes no title. This rule is absolute: no bona fide purchaser, no matter how innocent, takes good title from a forged deed. The public policy: protecting the community property system requires that forgery of a spouse's signature on a deed have no legal effect.

## Application to the Honeysuckle matter

For 2958 Honeysuckle, § 1102 raises three questions:

1. **Did Michael sign all required closing documents?** The 19 unsigned or defective documents in the 92-page Honeysuckle record warrant examination: if Michael's signatures were required and were not obtained (or were forged), the transaction may be void or voidable.

2. **Was Michael's joinder obtained through fraud?** If Michael signed under duress, based on material misrepresentations about the transaction, or without understanding what he was signing, the joinder was not legally effective.

3. **Voidable claim timing:** A voidable § 1102 transaction can be set aside by petition. The limitations period and discovery rule apply; if Michael discovered the defect after the fact, the limitations clock may not have started running until discovery.

## Current framework (2026)

Section 1102 remains the operative joinder requirement for community real property in California. All conveyances, encumbrances, and contracts to convey community real property require both spouses' execution. A unilateral conveyance is voidable absent fraud/forgery; a forged conveyance is void. The § 1102 claim coordinates with § 1101 remedies, § 721 fiduciary duty, and § 1572 fraud.

## The promise

§ 1102 was enacted on the promise that a spouse's interest in the community family home cannot be alienated without that spouse's signature. The joinder requirement is the operational safeguard of the community property system.

# Origin Context — FAM § 1102 (Pre-1975 — Husband-Only Management of Community Real Property)

## The wound

The 1872 California Civil Code gave the husband exclusive management and control of community real property. Under Civil Code § 172, the husband:

- Held the record title to community real property in his own name
- Could initiate any transaction — sale, lease for more than one year, mortgage, encumbrance
- Required only the wife's "join" signature — she could not refuse without filing for separate maintenance or divorce

The wife had no affirmative management rights. She could not sell, lease, or encumber community real property without the husband's initiation. She could veto (by refusing to join), but only at the cost of triggering a marital crisis.

## The "good faith purchaser" protection

§ 172 included a protection for third parties: if a husband alone executed a deed to a purchaser who took "in good faith without knowledge of the marriage relation," the deed was presumed valid even without the wife's signature. This created a dangerous dynamic:

- A husband could sell community real property without the wife's signature
- As long as the buyer didn't know they were married (or the husband concealed the marriage), the deed was valid
- The wife's only remedy was against the husband personally — the property itself was gone

## Why this matters for § 1102

Current Fam. Code § 1102 preserves this "good faith purchaser" protection (§ 1102(c)(2)) for transactions after January 1, 1975, but changed the governing rule for who must execute: both spouses, not just the husband. The 1975 reform answered the pre-1975 harm by making it structurally impossible for one spouse alone to sell community real property unless the buyer has no knowledge of the marriage.

The "19 unsigned documents" pattern in the Honeysuckle sale represents a modern variant of the pre-1975 problem: one spouse controls the transaction while the other's participation is minimized, bypassed, or forged.

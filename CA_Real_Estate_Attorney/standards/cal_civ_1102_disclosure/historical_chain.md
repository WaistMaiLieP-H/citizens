# Historical Chain — Civ. Code § 1102 (Transfer Disclosure)

| Year | Event | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-1985 | Caveat emptor + common-law fraud (§§ 1572, 1709-1710) | No affirmative statutory disclosure duty |
| 1985 | Stats. 1985, ch. 1574 enacts § 1102 et seq. | Mandatory TDS regime begins |
| 1988-1995 | Environmental hazards, death-on-property, and specific disclosure additions | Category expansion |
| 1990s-2000s | Agent disclosure (Section III of TDS) strengthened | Broker accountability |
| 2000s-2010s | Short sale, foreclosure exemption clarifications | Exemption refinement |
| 2020s | Current form | Stable |
| 1985 → 2026 | Core rule unchanged: mandatory, non-waivable, form-based | 41 years of stable consumer protection |

## The promise today

Section 1102 et seq. delivers a comprehensive affirmative disclosure regime for California residential real estate. It operates as both a consumer protection statute (creating strict liability for omissions) and a procedural framework (requiring a specific form, specific timing, specific seller and agent participation). The non-waivable rule is the capstone: no amount of contractual drafting can relieve the seller of the disclosure obligation.

For real estate fraud cases, the TDS is both a statutory cause of action and a critical piece of evidence. Whether the TDS exists, whether it is complete, and whether it is accurate are the first three questions in any residential real estate fraud analysis.

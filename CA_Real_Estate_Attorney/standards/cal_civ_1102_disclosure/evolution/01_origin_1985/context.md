# § 1102 Origin — The Wound and the Promise (1985)

## The wound

Before 1985, California residential real estate sales operated under a caveat emptor regime supplemented by the common-law fraud doctrines of Civ. Code §§ 1572-1573 and §§ 1709-1710. These doctrines were available but hard to use:

- **Caveat emptor.** Buyers were expected to inspect the property and identify defects. Sellers had no affirmative duty to disclose unless a specific common-law duty existed (fiduciary, partial disclosure, active concealment).
- **Proof difficulties.** A buyer claiming fraud had to prove the seller knew of the defect AND intentionally concealed it. This was often impossible because the relevant evidence (what the seller knew when) was in the seller's exclusive possession.
- **Broker conflicts.** Real estate agents often represented the seller and had no duty to disclose defects to the buyer, even when the agent knew of them.

The wound was **information asymmetry**: sellers (and their agents) typically knew much more about the property than buyers, and the legal system imposed no affirmative disclosure obligation. Buyers often discovered significant defects after closing and had no meaningful remedy.

## The promise

Stats. 1985, ch. 1574 enacted Civ. Code § 1102 et seq. The promise was simple but radical: **sellers of residential property must affirmatively disclose known material facts about the property's condition, using a standardized form, and cannot waive this obligation**.

The 1985 legislation flipped the default rule. Before 1985, sellers had no disclosure duty absent a specific common-law trigger. After 1985, sellers had an affirmative statutory duty for every residential transfer, enforceable by civil damages, rescission, and liability for both the seller and the agent.

Subsequent amendments added specific disclosure categories:
- **Environmental hazards** (lead paint, asbestos, earthquake zones, flood zones)
- **Death on property** within 3 years
- **Military ordnance** nearby
- **Proximity to agricultural operations**
- **Defects known to the agent** (Section III of the TDS)

Each amendment reflected a specific category of non-disclosure harm that had accumulated in case law or legislative hearings.

## The chain

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| Pre-1985 | Caveat emptor + common-law fraud; buyers bore the inspection burden |
| 1985 | Stats. 1985, ch. 1574 enacts § 1102 et seq.; mandatory TDS regime |
| 1980s-1990s | Environmental hazards, death on property, and other specific disclosure additions |
| 2000s-2010s | Agent disclosure (Section III) strengthened; broker duties expanded |
| 2020s | Current form |
| 1985 → 2026 | Core rule stable: mandatory disclosure, non-waivable, form-based, both seller and agent liable |

## Why this matters for Honeysuckle

The Honeysuckle case turns on whether required disclosures were made at the time of the 2023 sale. Section 1102's non-waivable rule means that any attempt to avoid disclosure (via contract clause, buyer acknowledgment, or "as-is" language) is void. If disclosures were inadequate, the seller and agent are strictly liable under § 1102.4, and the fraud theories under § 1572 can layer on top for punitive exposure under § 3294.

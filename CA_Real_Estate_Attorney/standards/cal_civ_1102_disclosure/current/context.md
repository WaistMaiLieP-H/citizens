# Civ. Code § 1102 et seq. — Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS)

**Popular name:** Transfer Disclosure Statement; residential real property disclosure law
**Location:** Civil Code, Division 2 (Property), Part 4 (Acquisition of Property), Title 4 (Transfer), Chapter 2 (Transfer of Real Property), Article 1.5 (Disclosures upon Transfer of Residential Property)
**Original enactment:** Stats. 1985, ch. 1574

## What § 1102 et seq. does

Section 1102 (as the gateway to Article 1.5, §§ 1102-1102.19) creates a **mandatory statutory disclosure regime** for residential real property transfers in California. It is the single most important consumer protection statute in California real estate practice.

The core rule: a seller of any single-family residential property must provide the buyer with a completed Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS) using the form specified in § 1102.6. The TDS covers the condition of the property, known defects, environmental hazards, death on the property within three years, and a host of other material facts. Both the seller AND the agent (if any) must complete their respective sections.

**Key features:**

1. **§ 1102(c) — Waiver void.** Any attempt to waive the TDS requirement is void as against public policy. Sellers cannot contract out of the disclosure obligation.

2. **§ 1102.3 — Timing.** The TDS must be delivered "as soon as practicable before transfer of title" (for sales) or before execution of the contract (for sales contracts or options). Late delivery gives the buyer a 3-day / 5-day right to terminate.

3. **§ 1102.4 — Liability.** Sellers and agents are liable for actual damages caused by errors, inaccuracies, or omissions in the TDS. The standard is good-faith effort — a seller who completes the TDS honestly is protected, but a seller who knowingly or negligently omits material facts is liable.

4. **§ 1102.6 — Mandatory form.** The TDS must use the form specified in § 1102.6, with Section I (seller disclosures), Section II (seller's representations), and (if applicable) Section III (agent's inspection disclosures). Custom or abbreviated forms are insufficient.

5. **§ 1102(a) — Scope.** Applies to "any transfer by sale, exchange, real property sales contract..., lease with an option to purchase, any other option to purchase, or ground lease coupled with improvements" of single-family residential property. Nearly every residential transfer is covered.

6. **Exemptions in § 1102.2.** Certain transfers are exempt — foreclosure sales, trustee sales, transfers to a successor in interest by operation of law, certain probate and conservatorship transfers. The exemptions are narrowly construed.

## Liability under § 1102 et seq.

A buyer who discovers material defects that should have been disclosed on the TDS can sue for:

1. **§ 1102.4 statutory liability** — actual damages caused by the undisclosed defect
2. **§ 1572 common-law fraud** — if the non-disclosure was knowing (intentional suppression); see `cal_civ_1572_fraud`
3. **§ 1709 tort deceit** — if the non-disclosure was knowing; see `cal_civ_1709_1710_deceit`
4. **Rescission** — if the non-disclosure was material and induced the purchase; see Civ. Code §§ 1567, 1688-1689
5. **§ 3294 punitive damages** — if the breach was fraudulent, oppressive, or malicious; see `cal_civ_3294_punitive`

TDS liability and common-law fraud liability overlap significantly. A plaintiff typically pleads both. The TDS claim is easier to prove (strict liability for material omissions); the fraud claim opens the door to punitive damages and tort remedies.

## Why this is load-bearing for real estate fraud cases

The TDS is the single most important document in a residential real estate fraud case. The plaintiff's analysis:

1. **Was a TDS delivered?** If no, the seller is in per se violation. This is a strong starting point.
2. **Was the TDS complete?** Look for unmarked items, missing sections, obvious omissions.
3. **Was the TDS accurate?** Compare representations against the actual condition of the property at time of transfer.
4. **Were known defects disclosed?** Evidence that the seller knew of a defect and did not disclose it is direct evidence of § 1102.4 liability and potentially § 1572(3) suppression fraud.
5. **Did the seller sign?** An unsigned TDS is not a valid disclosure.

## Relevance to the 2958 Honeysuckle transaction

For the Honeysuckle case:

1. **Was a TDS delivered in the sale?** — factual inquiry; if yes, the TDS itself becomes key evidence
2. **Were the 19 unsigned documents related to the TDS?** — if yes, the unsigned status is a per se § 1102 violation
3. **Were community property authorizations disclosed?** — the TDS should have reflected the community property status and the need for both spouses to consent
4. **Were known issues about the proceeds or prior encumbrances disclosed?** — missing disclosures are § 1102.4 liability

The TDS analysis runs parallel to the Civ §1572 fraud analysis and the Fam §1100/1102 community property analysis. All three should be pleaded together in the Honeysuckle complaint.

# Shapiro v. Sutherland (1998) 64 Cal.App.4th 1534

**Citation:** Shapiro v. Sutherland (1998) 64 Cal.App.4th 1534, 76 Cal.Rptr.2d 101
**Court:** California Court of Appeal, Second Appellate District
**Decided:** 1998
**Statutes construed:** Civil Code § 1102 et seq. — TDS; seller's duty of disclosure; concealment vs. non-disclosure; damages

## Facts

Seller sold residential property and delivered a TDS that failed to disclose known neighborhood noise and nuisance conditions that materially affected the property's value and livability. Buyers discovered the conditions after close of escrow and brought claims for rescission and damages under § 1102 and § 1572 (fraud/concealment).

## Holding

The Court of Appeal addressed the scope of the seller's TDS disclosure duty:

1. **The TDS obligation is affirmative, not passive.** Sellers are not excused from disclosure merely because the buyer could have discovered the defect through independent inspection. The § 1102 duty requires active, honest, and complete disclosure of all known material facts affecting value or desirability.

2. **Non-disclosure of a known material fact is constructive fraud regardless of subjective intent.** A seller who omits a known material fact from the TDS has violated § 1102.4's strict disclosure duty even without proof of an intent to deceive. This is the critical distinction from common-law fraud under § 1572: the TDS regime imposes strict liability for omissions; § 1572 fraud requires scienter.

3. **"Materiality" in the TDS context is objective.** A fact is material if a reasonable buyer would consider it relevant in deciding whether to purchase or at what price. Sellers cannot self-judge whether a fact is material — if the seller knows of a condition a reasonable buyer would want to know about, it must be disclosed.

4. **Damages include the difference in value as disclosed vs. as the property actually was.** The damages measure is not limited to repair costs; it includes the delta between what the buyer paid and what the property was actually worth given the undisclosed condition.

## The doctrinal proposition

Shapiro establishes the strict liability character of § 1102 disclosure obligations:

- **Strict liability for omission** — no intent required for § 1102.4 liability; intent is an add-on for § 1572 fraud and § 3294 punitive damages
- **No "as-is" escape from § 1102** — Civil Code § 1102.1 expressly states the TDS obligations cannot be waived; an "as-is" clause does not relieve the seller of the § 1102 disclosure duty
- **Objective materiality standard** — what a reasonable buyer would want to know, not what the seller chooses to tell
- **Full damages** — rescission or out-of-pocket damages measured at contract price minus actual value

## Application to the Honeysuckle matter

At 2958 Honeysuckle, the TDS analysis must address:

1. **19 unsigned or improperly executed documents** — a TDS that was not properly signed by the seller is not merely irregular; it is legally defective and creates § 1102.4 strict liability exposure
2. **Non-disclosure of known defects or conditions** — any material fact about the property's condition that was omitted from the TDS creates strict liability under Shapiro's framework
3. **No-waiver rule** — any "as-is" clause in the purchase agreement is subordinate to § 1102; the TDS obligation overrides it
4. **Strict liability + fraud = punitive damages** — if the omission was also intentional (§ 1572), the strict liability plus scienter supports a § 3294 punitive damages claim

## Authority status

Published California Court of Appeal decision (1998). Establishes the strict liability character of § 1102 TDS obligations, the objective materiality standard, and the no-waiver rule. Widely cited in California residential real estate fraud litigation.

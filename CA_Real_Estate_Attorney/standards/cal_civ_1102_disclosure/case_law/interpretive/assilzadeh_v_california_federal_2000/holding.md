# Assilzadeh v. California Federal Bank (2000) 82 Cal.App.4th 399

**Citation:** Assilzadeh v. California Federal Bank (2000) 82 Cal.App.4th 399, 98 Cal.Rptr.2d 176
**Decided:** 2000
**Court:** California Court of Appeal, Second Appellate District
**Disposition:** Affirmed judgment for buyer; construed TDS disclosure scope in institutional-seller context.
**Statute construed:** Civ. Code § 1102 et seq.

## Facts

Assilzadeh purchased a property from California Federal Bank, which had acquired the property through foreclosure. The bank argued that as an institutional seller who had never occupied the property, it had no personal knowledge of defects and therefore no meaningful TDS disclosure obligation. The buyer argued that the bank had acquired knowledge through its own inspections, previous maintenance records, and the foreclosure process, and was required to disclose what it knew.

## Holding

The Court of Appeal held:

1. **Institutional sellers are subject to TDS obligations to the extent of their knowledge.** While institutional sellers (banks, investment trusts, estates, REO sellers) may have less personal knowledge than individual sellers, they are nonetheless required to disclose what they do know. The TDS regime does not exempt institutional sellers from disclosure duties.

2. **Exemptions under § 1102.2 are narrowly construed.** Section 1102.2 lists specific exemptions from the TDS requirement, including some foreclosure-related transfers. But these exemptions are narrowly construed and do not relieve the seller of common-law fraud and disclosure duties where they apply.

3. **Even exempt transfers remain subject to § 1572 suppression fraud.** A seller whose transaction is exempt from the statutory TDS requirement is NOT exempt from common-law fraud liability. If the seller knew of material defects and concealed them, § 1572(3) suppression fraud applies regardless of whether the § 1102.4 statutory claim is available.

4. **The agent's inspection disclosure duty (Section III of the TDS) applies separately.** Even when the seller has limited direct knowledge, the seller's agent (if any) has an independent duty to conduct a reasonable visual inspection and disclose findings. This is the agent's Section III disclosure, which cannot be waived by the seller's lack of knowledge.

## Why this is load-bearing

*Assilzadeh* establishes two critical principles:

1. **Institutional sellers are not exempt from disclosure duties.** This closes a loophole that would have allowed banks, estates, and investment entities to avoid TDS liability by claiming lack of personal knowledge.

2. **Even § 1102.2 exemptions do not shield against common-law fraud.** This is the "backup path" rule: even when the statutory claim is unavailable, the common-law fraud and deceit claims (§§ 1572, 1709-1710) remain viable.

Both principles are frequently invoked in modern TDS litigation. Plaintiffs cite *Assilzadeh* when defendants claim institutional-seller status; they cite the same case to argue that § 1102.2 exemptions are narrow and do not foreclose common-law remedies.

## Relevance to the 2958 Honeysuckle transaction

For Honeysuckle, *Assilzadeh* is relevant if the seller at the time of the 2023 transaction was (a) an institutional entity rather than the original owner, or (b) claimed any statutory exemption from TDS requirements. Regardless of seller status, the common-law fraud and deceit causes of action under §§ 1572 and 1709-1710 remain available. *Assilzadeh* is the authority for that proposition.

## Authority status

Published, binding as persuasive appellate authority. Not overruled.

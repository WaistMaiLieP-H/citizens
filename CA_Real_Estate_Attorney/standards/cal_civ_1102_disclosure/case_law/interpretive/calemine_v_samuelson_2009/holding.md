# Calemine v. Samuelson (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 153

**Citation:** Calemine v. Samuelson (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 153, 89 Cal.Rptr.3d 495
**Decided:** 2009
**Court:** California Court of Appeal, Second Appellate District
**Disposition:** Reversed in part; clarified TDS disclosure scope and seller liability.
**Statute construed:** Civ. Code § 1102 et seq.

## Facts

The Calemines purchased a residence from the Samuelsons. After closing, the Calemines discovered that the property had a history of water intrusion and other defects that had been experienced by the sellers during their ownership but were not disclosed on the Transfer Disclosure Statement. The sellers argued that they had disclosed what they considered material and that the buyers had been given an opportunity to inspect.

## Holding

The Court of Appeal held:

1. **Sellers have an affirmative duty to disclose all known material facts about the property's condition.** The TDS is not a checklist of selective disclosures — it is an affirmative disclosure obligation. Sellers who know of material defects cannot omit them by arguing they were not explicitly asked about each specific defect.

2. **The "material" standard is judged from the buyer's perspective.** A fact is material if a reasonable buyer would consider it important in deciding whether to purchase. Water intrusion, structural issues, persistent repair problems, and similar condition facts are material per se.

3. **Seller's knowledge, not seller's characterization, controls.** A seller's subjective belief that a defect was not worth disclosing is not a defense. If the seller knew of the condition and a reasonable buyer would have wanted to know about it, the failure to disclose is actionable.

4. **The buyer's inspection opportunity does not excuse non-disclosure.** The TDS regime specifically rejects a caveat emptor framework that would put the burden on the buyer to discover defects. The buyer's right to inspect is additional to, not a substitute for, the seller's affirmative disclosure obligation.

## Why this is load-bearing

*Calemine* is a modern reaffirmation of the core TDS principle: sellers must affirmatively disclose known material facts, and neither selective disclosure nor reliance on the buyer's inspection excuses non-disclosure. It is routinely cited in TDS litigation when sellers argue they complied "substantially" or that the buyer should have investigated more carefully.

The case also illustrates the tight connection between TDS violations (statutory strict liability under § 1102.4) and common-law fraud (Civ. Code § 1572(3) suppression). When a seller fails to disclose a known material fact on the TDS, both causes of action are typically available and should be pleaded together.

## Relevance to the 2958 Honeysuckle transaction

For Honeysuckle, *Calemine*'s rule applies directly: whatever the seller knew about the property, the community property status, the authority to transfer, or the destination of proceeds, the seller had an affirmative duty to disclose on the TDS. If any of the 19 unsigned documents were TDS-related, or if any material facts about the sale structure were omitted from the TDS, *Calemine* supports both a § 1102.4 statutory claim and a § 1572(3) common-law fraud claim.

## Authority status

Published, binding as persuasive appellate authority. Cited in modern TDS practice guides. Not overruled.

# Historical Chain — Origin: Unruh Civil Rights Act (1959)

**Standard:** civ_51_unruh_civil_rights_act
**Era:** 1959 — California Unruh Civil Rights Act

---

## Jesse Unruh and the 1959 Act

The Unruh Civil Rights Act was enacted in 1959, authored by California Assembly Speaker Jesse Unruh. Its original purpose was to establish a state-law right to equal access to public accommodations — addressing the systematic exclusion of Black customers from restaurants, bars, theaters, and other businesses. Federal civil rights law had not yet addressed public accommodations (the Civil Rights Act of 1964 was five years away). California acted first.

The original statute covered discrimination based on sex, race, color, religion, ancestry, and national origin. The text — "all persons within the jurisdiction of this state are free and equal" — was deliberately broad, intended to evolve with the courts' interpretation of what "equality" requires.

## Evolution to Include Disability

Disability was added to the Unruh Act's protected categories over subsequent decades. The ADA per se rule (§51(f)) was added following the enactment of the federal ADA in 1990 — making California's Unruh Act the enforcement vehicle that gives California's $4,000 minimum damages remedy to ADA violations. The Disabled Persons Act (CIV §54), covering specifically medical facilities and public buildings, was strengthened in parallel.

## The Disability Expansion Context

The disability expansion of Unruh occurred in the same era as the 1977 San Francisco sit-in at the HEW offices — the 25-day occupation that produced the first federal disability rights regulations implementing Section 504 of the 1973 Rehabilitation Act. California's disability civil rights legislation reflected the same movement: disability rights are civil rights, not charity, and the state must enforce them with real remedies.

## Why This Standard Exists — The Government as Gatekeeper

Private discrimination is harmful. Government discrimination is categorically worse — because the government controls access to institutions that are not optional. Courts are not voluntary. Police are not optional. Administrative agencies that control disability benefits, victims compensation, and professional licenses are not substitutable. When government entities discriminate against disabled persons, they are not merely failing to serve a customer — they are denying access to the machinery of rights and justice itself.

ADA Title II addresses this: every state and local government must ensure that disabled individuals can participate in and benefit from every government service, program, and activity on equal terms. The government cannot be a disability discriminator.

---

## Tennessee v. Lane — Why Court Access Is Fundamental

*Tennessee v. Lane* was a case about whether a paraplegic defendant could be compelled to appear in a courtroom on the second floor of a courthouse with no elevator — and whether Congress had the power to require states to fix this. The Supreme Court held yes: the right of access to the courts is a fundamental right; Title II validly abrogates state sovereign immunity as applied to that fundamental right; and states must make their courts physically and programmatically accessible.

The significance for active cases: the holding is not limited to physical access. It extends to all aspects of equal participation in judicial proceedings. A court that refuses to provide effective communication accommodations, that imposes unreasonable procedural burdens on disabled litigants, or that conducts proceedings in a manner that excludes the disabled participant from meaningful participation is violating Title II. There is no sovereign immunity shield.

## The Olmstead Integration Mandate — The Deepest Application

*Olmstead v. L.C.* (1999) established that unnecessary institutionalization is discrimination. This is Title II's most powerful and least-used doctrine: a state that places disabled persons in institutional settings — conservatorship, involuntary hospitalization, incarceration — when community-based services are appropriate to their needs is discriminating against them by choosing the most restrictive rather than the most integrated setting.

Applied to secret conservatorship: if a person is placed under conservatorship when they did not meet the legal standard for conservatorship — or when community-based services could have addressed the stated concerns without conservatorship — the placement itself may be an Olmstead violation. The state court that ordered the conservatorship is the public entity; the conservatee is the qualified individual; the unnecessary institutionalization is the discrimination.

## The Historical Loss — Inaccessible Justice

The courts that are supposed to redress disability discrimination are themselves inaccessible to disabled persons. This is the recursive failure: to challenge government discrimination, the disabled person must access the very government institutions that may be discriminating against them. If those institutions are inaccessible — physically, communicatively, procedurally — the rights on paper become unreachable in practice.

The historical loss is measured in cases never filed, hearings never attended, rights never asserted, because the person with the disability could not navigate the inaccessible court system. ADA Title II was supposed to fix this. The fix is only as good as its enforcement.

## Why This Standard Exists — The Single Sentence That Launched the Movement

Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act was enacted in 1973 with a single operative sentence: no program or activity receiving federal financial assistance may exclude disabled individuals solely because of disability. It was written in a few minutes by Congressional staffers, almost without deliberation, as a parallel to Title VI (race) and Title IX (sex) of prior civil rights acts.

And then it sat — unimplemented — for four years.

The Department of Health, Education, and Welfare (HEW) was required to issue implementing regulations. Secretary after Secretary refused to sign them, fearing the cost of accessibility requirements for federally funded programs. The regulations sat unsigned through the Ford administration and into Carter's.

On April 5, 1977, disability rights activists occupied HEW offices in eight cities simultaneously. The San Francisco HEW office occupation lasted 25 days — the longest sustained occupation of a federal government building in American history. The occupiers refused to leave. On April 28, 1977, HEW Secretary Joseph Califano signed the regulations.

This is the origin of the modern disability rights movement as a political force. The regulations that came out of the 1977 sit-in established the first substantive disability rights protections in the United States. The ADA came 17 years later, built on the foundation §504 and its regulations established.

## The Grove City Gap and the 1987 CRRA Correction

In Grove City College v. Bell (1984) 465 U.S. 555, the Supreme Court narrowed §504's scope: it held that the statute only covered the specific program within an entity that received federal funding — not the entity's entire operations. This meant a university that received federal financial aid could discriminate against disabled students in any program that did not directly receive federal dollars.

Congress reversed this in the Civil Rights Restoration Act of 1987 (CRRA). The CRRA amended §504 (and parallel civil rights statutes) to establish institution-wide coverage: if the entity receives any federal financial assistance, all of its programs are covered. The CRRA was vetoed by President Reagan and overridden by Congress — one of two successful overrides of a Reagan veto.

## The Historical Loss — The Promise and the Enforcement Gap

Section 504 was the promise. The 1977 regulations were the implementation. The Civil Rights Restoration Act was the repair. But the persistent historical loss is enforcement:

- §504 administrative complaints go to the funding agency — which is also the program's partner. The investigator has an institutional interest in the program continuing to receive federal funds, not in finding violations.
- Private right of action requires litigation — resources that disabled individuals often lack, particularly when their disability has already compromised their employment and income.
- The "solely" causation standard, even narrowed by courts, provides a defense: articulate any other reason for the exclusion, and the §504 claim becomes more difficult.

In active cases involving systematic disability discrimination by state agencies (DDS, CalVCB) and federally funded medical programs (Blue Shield via Medicare/Medicaid), §504 is the civil rights vehicle that gives the pattern of discrimination a federal cause of action, separate from and parallel to the specific statutory violations documented in other Citizens.

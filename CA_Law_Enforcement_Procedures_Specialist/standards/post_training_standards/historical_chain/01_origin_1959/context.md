# Peace Officer Standards and Training — Origin 1959

## The Wound

Before 1959, California had no statewide minimum standards for peace officer qualifications or training. The decision about who could be hired as a peace officer — and what training they would receive before carrying a badge and a gun — rested entirely with individual agencies. California had more than 400 law enforcement agencies in the late 1950s, ranging from large urban departments with professional training programs to small rural agencies with essentially no formal training infrastructure.

The documented problems were substantial:

**Criminal histories:** Agencies had no statewide mechanism to identify whether a candidate had a criminal record in another jurisdiction. An officer terminated for misconduct in one county could be hired in another without disclosure.

**No minimum training:** Some California peace officers were sent into the field with days of informal instruction, if that. There was no standardized curriculum, no minimum hours requirement, and no certification system. Officers exercised arrest authority, used force, and made decisions with life-and-death consequences without any formal training on the legal standards governing those decisions.

**No psychological evaluation:** There was no requirement for psychological screening before hire. Officers with untreated mental health conditions, significant impulse control issues, or documented bias could be hired and deployed without evaluation.

**No background investigation standard:** What constituted a "background investigation" varied enormously by agency. Some agencies conducted thorough investigations; others hired based on personal acquaintance or cursory inquiry.

**Corruption and abuse without accountability:** Without statewide standards, there was no statewide accountability mechanism. An officer who engaged in corruption or brutality faced discipline — or not — based entirely on the local agency's culture and the political environment. There was no statewide enforcement authority, no training standards violation to cite, and no professional certification to revoke.

A 1957 study commissioned by the California Legislature — including findings from the California State Bar Association, law enforcement associations, and legislative analysts — documented these problems and recommended the creation of a statewide commission to establish minimum standards. The study found that the absence of uniform standards was producing variation that the public interest could not support: California could not have professional law enforcement without professional minimum standards.

## The Legislative Response

Stats. 1959, Ch. 1778 — enacted at the 1959 legislative session, effective January 1, 1960. Created the Commission on Peace Officer Standards and Training (POST), codified in Government Code § 13500 et seq. Initially, POST operated a voluntary compliance system — agencies were encouraged, not required, to meet POST standards. Mandatory compliance followed as the legislature and the POST Commission worked to extend the standards framework statewide.

Government Code § 1031 codified the minimum qualification standards that all peace officer classes must meet as a statutory floor. The statute has been amended over the years to strengthen the requirements: the psychological evaluation requirement was added; the bias evaluation mandate in § 1031(f) — requiring assessment for bias against enumerated protected characteristics — was added in recognition that discriminatory peace officer conduct is rooted in officer psychology and must be assessed before hire, not managed after incidents.

POST regulations at 11 CCR § 1001 et seq. set the specific training curriculum requirements. POST Basic Training, currently approximately 664 hours, is the minimum training requirement before a peace officer may exercise peace officer powers without direct supervision. POST certification can be revoked for misconduct — creating a statewide accountability mechanism that did not exist before 1959.

## What Changed

Before 1959: No statewide minimum standards. No fingerprinting/background check requirement. No minimum education standard. No psychological evaluation. No minimum training hours. No POST certification system. No statewide accountability mechanism. Wide agency-to-agency variation in officer quality.

After January 1, 1960 (POST effective): Minimum standards codified in § 1031: age, work authorization, fingerprinting, background investigation, education, and psychological/physical fitness. POST administers training standards and certification. POST Basic Training sets minimum curriculum. POST certification is revocable for misconduct — creating a professional accountability tool that operates across all California agencies.

Current significance: When an officer commits misconduct, § 1031 compliance is the threshold question for agency liability analysis. An agency that did not conduct a thorough background investigation (§ 1031(d)), skipped the psychological evaluation (§ 1031(f)), or deployed an officer who never completed POST Basic Training, has documentary statutory compliance failures — separate from and in addition to liability for the officer's conduct itself.

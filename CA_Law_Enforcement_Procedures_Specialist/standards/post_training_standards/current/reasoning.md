# Peace Officer Standards and Training — Minimum Qualification Standards — Reasoning

## Why This Law Exists

Peace officers exercise arrest authority, use of force, search and seizure, and detention — the most consequential powers the state grants to individuals. Whether those powers are exercised lawfully, competently, and without bias depends fundamentally on who is hired and how they are trained. In the absence of statewide minimum standards, the hiring and training decision is made 400+ times by individual agencies with no common floor.

Before 1959, that floor did not exist in California. Agencies hired officers with criminal histories, no psychological evaluation, and no minimum training. The result was documented variation: some agencies operated with professional, trained officers; others hired minimally qualified individuals and sent them into the field with little instruction. Corruption, brutality, and incompetence that was tolerated in one jurisdiction was not addressable at the statewide level because there were no statewide standards to enforce.

Gov. Code § 1031 and the POST regulations exist to establish a floor that every California peace officer must meet — regardless of which agency employs them, what county they are in, or what the local political environment is.

## Legislative Intent

The POST Commission was created by Stats. 1959, Ch. 1778, following a documented study of the absence of uniform law enforcement standards in California. The initial system was voluntary; mandatory compliance followed. Gov. Code § 1031 codified the minimum qualification standards that all peace officer classes must meet as a statutory floor. The POST Commission, operating under Gov. Code § 13500 et seq. and implementing regulations at 11 CCR § 1001 et seq., administers the training curriculum and certification requirements.

The explicit bias evaluation requirement in § 1031(f) — requiring assessment of bias "against race or ethnicity, gender, nationality, religion, disability, or sexual orientation" — was added in recognition that discriminatory use of force is rooted in officer psychology. Hiring an officer with explicit or implicit bias and then deploying them in communities they are biased against is not just an ethical failure; it is a statutory compliance failure that the agency can be held accountable for.

## Doctrinal Basis

The POST standards framework implements the state's police power to regulate peace officer qualifications — a core state function that does not require federal constitutional authority beyond the general authority of the state to regulate public employment. The psychological evaluation requirement (§ 1031(f)) implements California's obligation to ensure that persons exercising coercive public authority are fit to do so — grounded in both public safety and due process considerations.

Agency liability for negligent hiring and supervision is established under California law (Government Code § 820 et seq. and common law). Section 1031's requirements define the minimum threshold for what constitutes non-negligent hiring. An agency that knowingly hires an officer who does not meet § 1031's standards — or that conducts a cursory background investigation that misses a disqualifying history — has statutory compliance failures that inform the negligent hiring analysis.

# Peace Officer Personnel Records — SB 1421 Reform — Origin 2018

## The Wound

Original Penal Code § 832.7 was enacted in 1978 (Stats. 1978, Ch. 630). For forty years — from 1978 to 2019 — it created a near-total blackout on peace officer personnel records. The statute made personnel records confidential across all proceedings except through the Pitchess motion mechanism, which required a pending lawsuit or criminal case, a showing of good cause, and a court order before any records were accessible. Without a pending case, records were simply unreachable.

The operational consequences were severe and documented:

**Officer transfers without history disclosure:** Officers with sustained findings of violence, sexual assault, and dishonesty could transfer to new agencies with records sealed. Receiving agencies could not access prior sustained misconduct findings without triggering a Pitchess motion in a pending case — which required a pending case to not yet exist. Officers with serious misconduct histories from prior agencies were hired by subsequent agencies that had no access to those histories.

**The Rampart Division scandal (1998-2000):** The LAPD Rampart CRASH unit's systematic corruption — including shooting and framing a suspect (Javier Ovando), planting evidence, and lying under oath — illustrated the consequences of opacity. Multiple officers involved had personnel histories that could not be accessed without Pitchess motions. The scandal resulted in approximately 100 criminal conviction overturns and cost Los Angeles tens of millions of dollars in settlements. The concealment of officer misconduct histories was a structural enabler of the corruption.

**Journalists' systematic inability to investigate officer-involved killings:** After every high-profile officer-involved shooting, reporters filed CPRA requests for the involved officer's personnel records. Every request was denied under § 832.7(a) and § 6254(f) (now § 7923.600). There was no legal mechanism to obtain those records outside pending litigation. Public accountability for officer-involved deaths was essentially impossible.

**Giglio obligations undermined:** Prosecutors are constitutionally required to disclose evidence bearing on the credibility of their witnesses (Giglio v. United States (1972) 405 U.S. 150). A police officer with a sustained finding of dishonesty is a compromised witness — the defense is entitled to know about sustained dishonesty findings before the officer testifies. But § 832.7(a) meant that prosecutors often could not obtain those findings, did not know what they were, and could not disclose what they did not have. The Giglio obligation existed; the mechanism for fulfilling it was broken.

## The Legislative Response

SB 1421 (Sen. Nancy Skinner, Stats. 2018, Ch. 988). The bill was introduced following years of advocacy by the ACLU, civil rights organizations, and journalists documenting the consequences of § 832.7's blanket confidentiality. The legislature made a calibrated judgment: not all officer personnel records should be public, but sustained findings in three categories of the most serious misconduct must be — use of force resulting in death or serious injury, sexual assault, and dishonesty. The three categories reflect the legislature's view of when the public interest in disclosure necessarily overrides the officer's privacy interest.

SB 16 (Sen. Skinner, Stats. 2021, Ch. 402, effective January 1, 2022) expanded the categories to add: sustained findings of unlawful arrest, sustained findings of unlawful search, and sustained findings of prejudice or discrimination. The 2021 expansion was driven by the George Floyd murder (May 25, 2020), the subsequent national reckoning with discriminatory policing, and documentation of systematic patterns of unlawful stops and searches that had proceeded without accountability.

## What Changed

1978-2018: Blanket confidentiality. Pitchess motion as the only judicial pathway. No public access mechanism outside pending cases. Officer misconduct records sealed even after investigations concluded.

January 1, 2019 (SB 1421 effective): Public access to sustained findings of: (1) use of force causing death or GBI; (2) sustained excessive force; (3) failed intervention against excessive force; (4) sexual assault; (5) dishonesty in criminal investigation or prosecution. No pending case required. No court order required. CPRA request alone triggers the obligation. Agencies must produce records within 45 days.

January 1, 2022 (SB 16 effective): Two additional mandatory disclosure categories: (6) unlawful arrest; (7) unlawful search. Prejudice/discrimination (eighth category) added across enumerated protected characteristics. The range of disclosable sustained findings expanded to cover the constitutional violations most commonly the subject of § 1983 civil rights suits.

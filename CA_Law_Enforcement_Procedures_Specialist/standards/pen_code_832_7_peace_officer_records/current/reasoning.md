# Peace Officer Personnel Records — SB 1421 Disclosure Categories — Reasoning

## Why This Law Exists

Peace officers exercise the most coercive authority the state possesses: they can arrest, detain, search, and use force against people. That authority is exercised in the name of the public. A democratic society cannot hold that authority accountable without access to information about how it has been used. When an officer has a sustained finding of excessive force, sexual assault, or dishonesty — and the public has no access to that information — the officer remains credible to courts, jurors, and the public without the check that accountability requires.

For 40 years after the original § 832.7 was enacted in 1978, California operated under a near-total blackout of officer misconduct information. Officers with documented histories of violence, dishonesty, and sexual assault transferred between agencies with records sealed. Prosecutors could not meet their Giglio obligations regarding dishonest officers without fighting for records in Pitchess hearings. Civil rights attorneys litigating excessive force cases could not access prior sustained misconduct findings without pending litigation. The public could not know whether an officer who killed someone had done the same before.

SB 1421 exists because the accountability promise of law enforcement — that officers are held to professional standards, that misconduct is investigated, that findings are made — is empty if the results are permanently hidden.

## Legislative Intent

SB 1421 (Sen. Nancy Skinner, 2018) was enacted following years of advocacy by civil rights organizations, journalists, and academics who documented the consequences of § 832.7's blanket confidentiality. The legislature made specific findings: (1) the public has a right to know about serious police misconduct; (2) disclosure promotes institutional accountability; (3) the categories selected — violence, sexual assault, dishonesty — represent the most serious forms of professional misconduct and are matters of compelling public interest. SB 16 (2021) extended the categories to unlawful arrest, unlawful search, and prejudice/discrimination after continued documentation that officers were conducting unconstitutional stops, searches, and arrests without accountability.

The legislature balanced competing interests: the general confidentiality rule (§ 832.7(a)) remains for allegations that were not sustained, frivolous complaints, and records outside the five categories. The Pitchess mechanism remains for court-ordered disclosure in pending cases. The § 832.7(b) override applies only to sustained findings in the most serious categories — reflecting the legislative judgment that sustained misconduct in those areas crosses the threshold where the public's right to know prevails over officer privacy interests.

## Doctrinal Basis

The First Amendment and California Constitution Art. I, § 3(b) undergird the right of public access to government information. § 832.7(b) implements that constitutional interest for a specific category of government records. The Pitchess doctrine (from Pitchess v. Superior Court (1974) 11 Cal.3d 531, now codified at Evidence Code § 1043) established the framework for court-ordered disclosure of officer personnel records in civil and criminal proceedings; § 832.7(b) operates outside and independent of Pitchess — no pending case required, no court order required, no showing of good cause required. A CPRA request alone triggers the § 832.7(b) disclosure obligation.

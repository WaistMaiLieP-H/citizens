# Body-Worn Camera Data — Origin 2015

## The Wound

In the summer and fall of 2014, two events defined the national conversation about police accountability and body cameras:

**Michael Brown (August 9, 2014 — Ferguson, Missouri):** Michael Brown, 18, was shot and killed by a Ferguson police officer. The officer had no body camera. What occurred in the moments before the shooting became the subject of a contested factual dispute — some witnesses said Brown had his hands up; the officer described a different sequence of events. The grand jury investigation and DOJ review relied on contradictory eyewitness accounts with no definitive audio/video record. Without footage, the factual dispute could not be resolved and became a template for national polarization.

**Eric Garner (July 17, 2014 — Staten Island, New York):** Eric Garner died after a police officer applied a chokehold during an arrest for selling untaxed cigarettes. The incident was recorded on a bystander's cell phone. The footage was undisputed. The officer was not indicted. The footage showed what happened; the legal outcome reflected something else. Both events — one with no footage and disputed facts, one with clear footage and contested legal consequence — demonstrated the need for systematic camera adoption and data management.

The DOJ released a report on Ferguson (March 2015) and comprehensive recommendations for police body cameras. The report recommended that agencies adopting cameras establish written policies for use, retention, and access. Without policies, some agencies that had adopted cameras were: allowing officers to review footage before writing incident reports (compromising report independence); deleting footage inconsistently; retaining footage for days before storing it properly; refusing to release footage even on CPRA requests; and allowing officers involved in shootings to retain physical custody of their own cameras.

California had agencies with cameras and no statewide standards. The accountability purpose of the cameras was undermined by the absence of data management requirements.

## The Legislative Response

AB 66 (Assemb. Kevin McCarty, Stats. 2015, Ch. 404) — California's first body-worn camera data management statute. Added Penal Code § 832.18. The legislative approach was deliberate: rather than mandating specific hardware or prescribing exact procedures, the statute establishes a best-practices framework that agencies must consider when building their policies. This reflects both the diversity of agency sizes and technical capacities and the legislature's intent to create a floor while leaving implementation flexibility.

The critical design choices in AB 66:
- The 60-day minimum for nonevidentiary data ensures footage exists during the most likely window for complaint filing
- The 2-year minimum for evidentiary data covers standard statute of limitations periods for personal injury and civil rights claims
- Supervisor custody on OIS and use-of-force incidents removes the implicated officer from the chain of custody
- The permanent retention of access/deletion logs creates an audit trail that outlives the footage itself
- The explicit CPRA preservation clause (§ 832.18(d)) prevents agencies from treating § 832.18 as a limitation on public access

## What Changed

Before 2015: Agencies adopting BWCs operated under internal policies (if any) with no statewide floor. Footage retention, access, and deletion were entirely at agency discretion. No minimum retention periods. No supervisor custody requirements for critical incidents. No anti-tampering standards. No CPRA preservation clause.

After January 1, 2016 (§ 832.18 effective): Agencies must have written policies addressing the enumerated best practices. The 60-day/2-year/prosecution retention framework provides clear minimum standards. Access and deletion logs must be retained permanently. The CPRA access right to BWC data is explicit. AB 748 (2019) subsequently added mandatory disclosure requirements for critical incident footage, creating the full accountability framework for BWC data.

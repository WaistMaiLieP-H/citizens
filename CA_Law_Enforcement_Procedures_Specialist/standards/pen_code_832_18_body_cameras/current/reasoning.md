# Body-Worn Camera Data — Storage, Retention, and Access — Reasoning

## Why This Law Exists

Body-worn cameras create objective, contemporaneous records of police-public encounters. Their value as accountability tools depends entirely on whether the footage is preserved, protected from tampering, and accessible when needed. A camera that records an incident and then has its footage deleted, altered, or selectively withheld provides no accountability. It provides a false appearance of accountability — the agency can say "we have cameras" without the footage ever serving its purpose.

Section 832.18 exists because, in the absence of data management standards, agencies adopting BWCs were free to delete footage inconsistently, allow officers to review footage before writing reports, permit officers to control cameras they activated during incidents, and withhold footage from complainants. The cameras existed; the accountability value did not. The statute addresses the gap between having a camera and having a system that preserves the camera's evidence.

## Legislative Intent

AB 66 (Assemb. Kevin McCarty, Stats. 2015, Ch. 404) was introduced after the 2014 police killings of Michael Brown and Eric Garner, in which the absence of definitive footage meant the factual question of what occurred could not be resolved. The legislature chose a best-practices framework rather than a prescriptive mandate because agencies vary enormously in technical capacity, budget, and operational environment. The "shall consider" language for most provisions reflects this — agencies have flexibility in how they implement the practices, but they must have a policy and that policy must address the enumerated considerations.

AB 748 (2019) supplemented § 832.18 by adding mandatory disclosure requirements for BWC footage of critical incidents, making the data management framework in § 832.18 a prerequisite for meaningful public access under AB 748 and § 832.7(b).

## Doctrinal Basis

Section 832.18 implements California's broader evidence preservation obligations. The duty to preserve evidence arising from reasonably anticipated litigation is a common-law and statutory obligation. § 832.18 translates that obligation into a concrete operational framework for law enforcement agencies: minimum retention periods are calibrated to the periods during which litigation, complaints, or criminal proceedings are most likely to arise. The 2-year floor for evidentiary data covers the statute of limitations for most personal injury and civil rights claims; the criminal prosecution retention rule tracks established evidence retention law.

The permanent retention of access/deletion logs implements an audit trail principle: even if footage is eventually and lawfully deleted, the record of what was deleted and when remains. That record allows a court, prosecutor, or civil plaintiff to establish that footage existed, what event it covered, and when it was destroyed — essential information for spoliation analysis under CCP § 2023.030.

# Body-Worn Camera Data — Storage, Retention, and Access — Rule

**Authority:** Cal. Pen. Code § 832.18
**Jurisdiction:** California
**Effective:** January 1, 2016 (AB 66, Stats. 2015, Ch. 404)

## The Rule

Section 832.18 requires law enforcement agencies that use body-worn cameras (BWCs) to establish written policies for downloading and storing BWC data. The statute specifies best practices that agencies shall consider when building those policies.

**Download procedures:** Designated personnel are responsible for downloading recorded data. For use-of-force incidents and officer-involved shootings, the officer's supervisor must immediately take physical custody of the camera if it lacks automatic download capability.

**Retention schedules:**
- Nonevidentiary data (routine patrol activity not leading to arrest or citation): minimum 60 days. May be erased after that. Agencies may retain longer to accommodate potential complaints.
- Evidentiary data (use of force, arrest, detention, formal or informal complaint against an officer): minimum 2 years.
- Data relevant to criminal prosecution: retained for the same period as other evidence relevant to that prosecution.
- Access and deletion logs: retained permanently. The log outlives the footage.

**Anti-tampering:** Policies must explicitly prohibit unauthorized use, duplication, and distribution of BWC data. Officers may not upload agency footage to public or social media websites. Sanctions required for violations.

**Data categorization:** Footage must be tagged and categorized when downloaded — identifying the type of incident captured.

**Third-party storage:** If an agency uses a cloud or vendor system, contracts must protect data integrity, provide audit trails preventing tampering, include automatic backup, and ensure technical assistance. Internal legal counsel must be consulted on chain-of-custody compliance.

All BWC data is the property of the law enforcement agency. § 832.18 does not limit the public's right of access to that data under the CPRA (§ 832.18(d)).

## Key Elements

1. **60-day minimum / 2-year minimum**: The retention threshold depends on whether the data is evidentiary. The 2-year floor for evidentiary footage covers the most likely period for complaint filing or civil litigation.
2. **Permanent retention of access/deletion logs**: The log is the audit trail for what happened to footage that no longer exists.
3. **Supervisor custody on use-of-force / OIS**: Removes the implicated officer from controlling their own evidence.
4. **CPRA preservation**: § 832.18(d) explicitly says the statute does not restrict public access rights — BWC footage requested under CPRA remains subject to CPRA, including § 832.7(b) mandatory disclosure for use-of-force incidents.

## What This Means in Practice

If an officer uses force and the agency's policy allows the footage to be deleted after 60 days, that violates § 832.18(b)(5)(B) — use-of-force footage is evidentiary data requiring 2-year retention. If the agency deletes it anyway, the permanent access/deletion log proves the footage existed and was destroyed. That record becomes the foundation for a spoliation argument under § 2023.030 in any civil action, or a Brady violation argument in any criminal case where the footage was material to the defense.

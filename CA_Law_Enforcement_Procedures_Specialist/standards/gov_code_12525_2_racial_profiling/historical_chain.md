# Government Code § 12525.5 — RIPA Historical Chain

## The Wound That Made This Law Necessary

For decades, California law enforcement agencies collected almost no systematic data
on whom they stopped, why, and what happened. Officers exercised discretion on the
street; supervisors reviewed individual incidents; but no one was aggregating the
pattern. The result was a persistent, documented gap between what agencies said they
were doing and what communities experienced.

The California Racial and Identity Profiling Act of 2015 emerged from a specific and
measurable failure: when communities of color reported that they were being stopped
far more frequently than white residents for equivalent conduct, law enforcement
agencies had no way to confirm or deny the claim. They had no data. The absence of
data was not neutral — it was protective. Without data, there was no accountability.
With no accountability, there was no incentive to change.

The wound was not just racial disparity in stops. The wound was the institutionalized
invisibility of that disparity. Every stop that was never recorded, never reported,
never analyzed, was a stop that never happened — officially. The person who was stopped
knew it happened. Their family knew. Their community knew. The police department did not.

## The Legislative Response: AB 953 (2015)

Assembly Bill 953 (Stats. 2015, Ch. 466), the Racial and Identity Profiling Act,
was authored by Assemblymember Shirley Weber. It created Government Code § 12525.5
and § 12525.6, establishing:

1. **Mandatory data collection**: Every stop by every peace officer, documented with
   perceived race/ethnicity, gender, age, reason for stop, actions taken, and results.
   Not a sample. Not a pilot program. Every stop.

2. **Mandatory annual reporting**: Agencies report to the California Department of
   Justice. The DOJ publishes the data publicly. Visibility replaces invisibility.

3. **The RIPA Board (§ 12525.6)**: A nine-member advisory board chaired by the
   Attorney General, with law enforcement, civil rights, academic, and community
   members, tasked with developing neutral methodologies to identify racial or identity
   profiling in the data.

Data collection began July 1, 2018, phased in by agency size.

## Subsequent Amendments

**AB 2067 (2017)**: Expanded data categories collected per stop. Added gender identity
categories. Strengthened reporting requirements.

**SB 978 (2019)**: Connected RIPA data reporting to law enforcement agency policy
requirements. Agencies that fail to report face consequences under their Peace Officer
Standards and Training (POST) compliance obligations.

**AB 175 (2019)**: Clarified definitions of "stop" and "pedestrian stop" to ensure
broad coverage of all law enforcement encounters, not just formal traffic stops.

## Current State of the Law

As of 2026, RIPA is fully operational. All California law enforcement agencies collect
and report stop data annually. The California DOJ's OpenJustice portal publishes
agency-level stop data publicly. The RIPA Board publishes annual reports analyzing
racial disparities in stop rates across the state.

The data has already documented significant racial disparities in stop rates across
California law enforcement agencies, particularly in pedestrian stops and in the
disparity between stop rates and arrest/citation rates (the "hit rate" — if you stop
100 people and arrest 2, your hit rate is 2%; if one demographic has a lower hit rate,
that is statistical evidence that the stated justification for those stops does not
hold).

## Steward Relevance

Michael Hartmann's June 16, 2023 encounter with APD is a RIPA-covered stop. The
officers who responded to that call were required to log it in APD's RIPA dataset:
who they stopped, why, what they did, and what the result was.

Two tools emerge from this:

**Tool 1 — The specific June 16, 2023 stop record.** What did APD log as the reason
for the stop of Michael Hartmann? What did they log as the result? Does the logged
reason match the DV-dispatch framing? Does the logged result match what Michael
experienced? Any gap between the logged version and reality is a false report.

**Tool 2 — APD's pattern data.** The DOJ public dataset for APD covers stops going
back to 2018. If APD's pattern shows disproportionate stops or lower hit rates for
particular demographics in DV-coded calls, that establishes the pattern context
into which June 16, 2023 fits. A § 1983 claim against APD is strengthened when the
specific incident is situated within a documented departmental pattern.

RIPA is one of the few laws in California that creates public data about law enforcement
conduct at the individual-stop level. It converts invisible encounters into auditable
records. For a pro se litigant, that is one of the most powerful discovery tools
available without a lawyer or a subpoena.

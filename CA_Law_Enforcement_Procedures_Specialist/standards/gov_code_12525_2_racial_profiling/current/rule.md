# RIPA — Racial and Identity Profiling Act — Stop Data Collection — Rule

**Authority:** Cal. Gov. Code § 12525.5 (RIPA); Cal. Pen. Code § 13519.4
**Jurisdiction:** California
**Effective:** Stop data collection: July 1, 2018 (large agencies); statewide by July 1, 2023

## The Rule

Every California law enforcement agency shall collect and report data on each stop of a person by a peace officer (§ 12525.5(b)). A "stop" means any detention — temporary or prolonged — and any encounter in which the officer requests that the person identify themselves, answer questions, or provide information in a law enforcement context.

**Data collected on every stop (§ 12525.5(b)(1)-(13)):**
1. Perceived race, ethnicity, gender, and approximate age of the person stopped
2. Reason for the stop (traffic violation / reasonable suspicion of crime / DV call response / etc.)
3. Actions taken during the stop (search, pat-down, property search, removal from vehicle, etc.)
4. Whether the person consented to any search
5. Whether a search revealed contraband or evidence
6. Whether property was seized
7. Result of the stop (arrest / citation / warning / release with no action)
8. Whether force was used (cross-reference to § 835a)
9. Duration of the stop
10. Officer identification (badge/ID number, cross-referenced to department records)

**Annual agency reporting:** All collected data is reported annually to the California DOJ. The DOJ publishes the data publicly at the OpenJustice platform and the RIPA Board — chaired by the Attorney General — issues annual reports analyzing patterns.

**Individual stop records:** Each agency maintains individual stop records with peace officer identification. These records are subject to PRA request with officer identification, and subject to AG audit.

**Definition of racial and identity profiling (§ 13519.4(e)):** The reliance on characteristics such as race, color, ethnicity, national origin, age, religion, gender, gender identity, gender expression, sexual orientation, or immigration status in making law enforcement decisions — except where these characteristics are part of a specific suspect description — is prohibited.

**PRA access:** Stop data submitted to the DOJ is a public record. Individual stop records with officer identification are obtainable via PRA directed to the submitting agency.

## Key Elements

1. **Every stop is documented** — no officer discretion to omit a stop from the dataset; a stop that occurred but has no RIPA record is an omission — and the omission itself is auditable.
2. **Officer identification** — each stop is tied to the officer who made it; pattern analysis at the officer level is possible from the data.
3. **Reason vs. result gap** — when the stated reason for a stop (traffic violation / DV call) does not match the result (no citation, no arrest, no evidence), the gap is the statistical footprint of a pretextual stop.
4. **Consent to search** — documented; absence of consent combined with a search is a Fourth Amendment issue logged in the data.

## What This Means in Practice

Every detention or law enforcement encounter since July 1, 2018 generates a RIPA record that can be obtained and audited. The RIPA record for a specific incident — cross-referenced against the incident report, body camera footage, and CAD dispatch record — produces a four-document audit of what the officer officially claimed happened vs. what was documented contemporaneously in different systems. Discrepancies across these four records are the fingerprint of a falsified report.

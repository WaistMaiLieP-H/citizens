# Reasoning — Cal. Gov. Code § 12525.5 (RIPA — Stop Data Collection)

**Standard ID:** GOV_CODE_12525_2_RACIAL_PROFILING
**Layer:** 2 — Reasoning
**Status:** ACTIVE
**Filed:** 2026-04-20

---

## Why This Standard Exists

The Racial and Identity Profiling Act was enacted through AB 953 (2015) after decades of litigation attempting to establish racial profiling claims without systematic data. Before RIPA, proving a pretextual stop required either a pattern of documented misconduct against the specific officer or statistical inference from aggregate data that agencies refused to collect. RIPA eliminated the data gap by mandating granular per-stop collection.

The Legislature found that without individual-stop data tied to officer identification, racial profiling claims could not be proved and agencies had no accountability mechanism. RIPA's mandatory collection creates the evidentiary infrastructure that makes pattern claims provable.

---

## Why "Every Stop" Is the Operative Standard

RIPA's per-stop data collection — not aggregate statistics — reflects the Legislature's finding that aggregate data is easily manipulated. An agency can show statistically neutral aggregate stop demographics while individual officers conduct systematic pretextual stops if the data is only collected at the agency level.

By requiring individual-stop data with officer identification, RIPA enables analysis at the officer level: whether a specific officer's stop patterns show a reason-vs.-result gap; whether a specific officer's stops disproportionately involve certain perceived demographics; whether a specific officer's consent-search rate exceeds the agency norm.

---

## Why the Reason-vs.-Result Gap Is the Core Audit Metric

The most actionable data in a RIPA record is the relationship between the stated reason for the stop and its result. A stop documented as "traffic violation" that produces no citation, no arrest, and no evidence of any violation is a stop where the stated reason did not lead to any outcome. That gap does not prove the stop was pretextual — but it is the statistical footprint that pretextual stops leave in the data.

When a large proportion of an officer's "traffic violation" stops produce no citations, and those stops disproportionately involve persons of certain perceived demographics, the pattern is the evidence. The individual RIPA record plus the aggregate pattern constitutes the showing.

---

## Why RIPA Records Cross-Reference Against Incident Reports

A DV-coded call that generates both a § 13701 incident report and a RIPA stop record creates two parallel official documents of the same encounter. These documents were generated by different systems for different purposes, and they must be consistent. Discrepancies between the incident report (what the officer says happened) and the RIPA record (what the officer officially logged as the stop data) are forensic evidence of at least one falsified document.

For encounters where a third party pre-framed the call and officers arrived with the framing already in place, both the incident report and the RIPA record reflect what the officer chose to document — not necessarily what occurred. Cross-referencing them against each other, body camera footage, and the CAD dispatch record creates a four-source audit that is highly resistant to post-hoc revision.

---

## Application in Practice

For any law enforcement encounter since July 1, 2018:
1. Obtain the RIPA stop record via PRA to the agency (specify date, location, officer badge number if known).
2. Obtain the incident report for the same encounter.
3. Obtain the CAD dispatch record.
4. Obtain any body camera footage (§ 832.18 cross-reference).
5. Cross-reference all four sources. Discrepancies are the audit finding.
6. Check the DOJ OpenJustice RIPA public dataset for the agency's aggregate stop patterns and the officer's individual stop history if officer identification is known.

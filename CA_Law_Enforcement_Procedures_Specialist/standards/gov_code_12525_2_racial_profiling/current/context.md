# Government Code § 12525.5 — RIPA Data Collection: Plain-English Context

## What This Law Does

The Racial and Identity Profiling Act (RIPA) requires every California law enforcement
agency to collect and report data on every single stop an officer makes. Not aggregate
statistics — every stop. The data includes who was stopped (perceived race, gender,
approximate age), why they were stopped, what actions the officer took, whether a
search was conducted, whether consent was given, what the search found, and whether
force was used.

This data is reported to the California Department of Justice annually. The DOJ publishes
it publicly. The RIPA Board — chaired by the Attorney General — analyzes it to identify
whether agencies are engaging in racial or identity profiling.

The practical result: every traffic stop, pedestrian stop, or field interview conducted
by a California peace officer since July 1, 2018 should exist in a RIPA dataset — with
the officer's perceived race/gender/age assessment of the person stopped, the stated
reason for the stop, and what happened during it.

## Why This Matters for Identifying Pretextual Stops

A pretextual stop is one where the officer uses a minor traffic violation or other
technical justification as cover for what is really an investigation motivated by
something else — race, appearance, a prior contact, or in Michael Hartmann's case,
a third party's direction.

RIPA data reveals patterns. When an agency's stop data shows that a particular officer
consistently stops people of a specific demographic for reasons that rarely result in
citation or arrest, that is statistical evidence of pretextual stops. When a stop
record shows "reason for stop: traffic violation" but "result: no citation issued, no
arrest, no evidence found," the gap between the stated reason and the outcome becomes
auditable.

## What "Every Stop" Means

RIPA defines a "stop" broadly: any detention, or any interaction where the officer
requests that the person identify themselves, answer questions, or provide information
in a law enforcement context. A June 16, 2023 response to a "domestic violence" call
that involved stopping, detaining, or questioning Michael Hartmann at the scene is a
stop within RIPA's meaning. It should exist in APD's RIPA dataset.

## Steward Relevance: June 16, 2023 and APD Pattern Evidence

**Direct record: the June 16, 2023 stop.** APD officers who responded to that call
were required under RIPA to log the stop. The RIPA record for that stop should show:

- The perceived race/gender of the person(s) stopped
- The stated reason for the stop
- What actions were taken (detention, search, arrest)
- Whether force was used
- The result (arrest, citation, no action, referral)

Obtaining APD's RIPA submission data for June 16, 2023 — specifically the records for
the officers who responded to Michael's location — is a direct tool for documenting
what APD recorded as the basis and outcome of the encounter, and whether that matches
what Michael experienced.

**Pattern evidence across APD.** The June 16, 2023 stop did not happen in a vacuum.
If APD's broader RIPA data shows that officers responding to DV calls in similar
circumstances disproportionately stopped, detained, or arrested one party rather than
the other based on perceived demographics, that pattern is relevant to demonstrating
that the June 16 response was not an isolated error but part of a systemic practice.

**Cross-case pattern.** The 10 APD violations identified in the prior forensic audit
were documented departmental failures. RIPA data for the officers involved in those
violations — if they also appeared on June 16, 2023 — would show whether those
officers' stop patterns are consistent with the profiling or pretextual-stop concern.

**Federal § 1983 claim.** A pretextual stop motivated by third-party direction (mother
directing APD to respond to a scene, Christina pre-positioned, the dispatch framing the
encounter as DV before officers arrived) is a Fourth Amendment violation. RIPA data
showing a pattern of such stops by APD, combined with the specific June 16 record,
supports the pattern-or-practice element of a § 1983 claim.

## How to Get the Data

1. **PRA request to APD**: Request all RIPA stop data for June 16, 2023 for all
   officers who responded to the incident address. Request should specify officer
   badge numbers if known from the police report.

2. **DOJ public dataset**: The California DOJ publishes RIPA data at
   https://openjustice.doj.ca.gov/. Annual agency-level data is publicly available
   and does not require a PRA request.

3. **Officer-level data**: APD must maintain a version of the RIPA data that includes
   peace officer identification (§ 12525.5(d)). That version is subject to AG audit
   and can be obtained through a targeted PRA request.

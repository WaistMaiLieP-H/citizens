# Penal Code Section 835a — Historical Chain

## When Can a Police Officer Use Force Against You

---

### I. The Wound

For over a century, California law told officers they could use "reasonable force" to make an arrest. What counted as reasonable was judged after the fact, under the federal standard from Graham v. Connor (1989): would a reasonable officer in the same situation have used the same force? The problem was that this standard was almost impossible to fail. Officers could use deadly force against people who turned out to be unarmed, and as long as the officer could articulate that they subjectively believed there was a threat, the force was "reasonable."

The result was preventable deaths. Stephon Clark, shot 20 times in his grandmother's backyard by Sacramento Police on March 18, 2018, while holding a cell phone. Unarmed. The Sacramento County District Attorney declined to prosecute because the officers' use of force was "reasonable" under existing law. The officers believed the cell phone was a gun. Under the old standard, that subjective belief — even though wrong — was enough.

### II. The Promise (AB 392, 2019)

Assembly Bill 392, signed by Governor Newsom and effective January 1, 2020, rewrote section 835a from the ground up. The promise:

**Force must be necessary, not merely reasonable. And an officer's own conduct leading up to the force counts.**

This was a fundamental shift. "Necessary" means there was no reasonable alternative that would have achieved the lawful objective with less force. "Reasonable" just meant the force was not obviously excessive. The gap between those two words — necessary versus reasonable — is the difference between Stephon Clark being alive and Stephon Clark being dead.

### III. The Key Provisions

1. **Legislative findings**: The sanctity of every human life. Every person has a right to be free from excessive force. Force is a serious responsibility to be exercised judiciously.

2. **Necessity standard**: Deadly force only when the officer reasonably believes the person poses an imminent threat of death or serious bodily injury. "Imminent" means about to happen, not merely possible.

3. **Totality of circumstances**: All facts known to the officer at the time — including the officer's own conduct leading up to the force. An officer who escalates cannot then claim the escalation justified force.

4. **No duty to retreat**: Officers need not retreat. But they also cannot use force creating a substantial risk of death when lesser force would suffice.

### IV. The Companion: SB 230

Senate Bill 230 (Stats. 2019, Ch. 285), effective January 1, 2021, required every California law enforcement agency to adopt a use-of-force policy consistent with section 835a. This means every agency — including Antioch Police Department — was required to have a written policy implementing the necessity standard by January 1, 2021.

### V. For the Steward's Case

On June 16, 2023, section 835a had been in effect for three and a half years. APD was required to have a compliant use-of-force policy for two and a half years. Every officer responding that day was bound by the necessity standard.

The audit questions:
- Was any physical force used during the encounter?
- If officers physically separated Cole from Michael, that is a use of force — hands-on contact against the will of either person
- Was that force necessary under the totality of circumstances?
- Did the officers' own conduct upon arrival contribute to any escalation?
- Could the same result have been achieved through verbal commands, de-escalation, or simply waiting?

Every answer is in the BWC footage, the police report, and the CAD log. Those records must be obtained.

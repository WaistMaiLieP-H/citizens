# Use of Force — Necessity Standard — Rule

**Authority:** Cal. Pen. Code § 835a (as amended by AB 392, Stats. 2019, ch. 170)
**Jurisdiction:** California
**Effective:** January 1, 2020

## The Rule

A peace officer is justified in using deadly force upon another person only when the officer reasonably believes, based on the totality of the circumstances, that such force is **necessary** to defend against an imminent threat of death or serious bodily injury to the officer or another person (§ 835a(a)(1)), or to apprehend a fleeing person who has committed a felony involving the infliction or threatened infliction of serious bodily injury and the officer reasonably believes the person will cause death or serious bodily injury to others if not apprehended immediately (§ 835a(a)(2)).

**The necessity standard (§ 835a(b)):** A peace officer shall not use deadly force unless it is necessary in the circumstances. The officer shall, whenever possible and if reasonable, exhaust de-escalation tactics, non-forceful tactics, and less lethal options before resorting to deadly force.

**Non-deadly force:** A peace officer may use non-deadly force only when, and to the degree that, it is reasonably necessary to effectuate a lawful purpose.

**Totality of circumstances (§ 835a(d)(3)):** Includes not only the immediate facts at the moment force is used but also the conduct of the officer leading up to the use of force. An officer who creates the need for force through unnecessary escalation bears responsibility for that escalation in the necessity analysis.

**What does NOT justify force (§ 835a(e)):**
- A person's mental health crisis alone
- A person's failure to respond to officer commands when non-compliance does not create a threat
- Prior contact history, absent an independent basis for the present threat

**Unlawful orders (§ 835a(c)):** An officer shall not use force to compel a person to comply with an unlawful order. Force used to effectuate an unlawful detention is per se unjustified.

**Physical separation of persons:** Physically restraining, moving, or separating persons against their will — including separating a parent from a child — constitutes use of force subject to the necessity standard.

## Key Elements

1. **Deadly force**: Necessary only when (a) imminent threat of death/SBI, or (b) fleeing felon posing death/SBI risk to others — exhaustion of alternatives required first.
2. **Non-deadly force**: Reasonably necessary to effectuate a lawful purpose — not merely convenient or customary.
3. **Officer's pre-force conduct**: Escalating conduct by the officer weighs against the claimed necessity.
4. **Unlawful orders**: Force to enforce an unlawful order is never justified.

## What This Means in Practice

The shift from "reasonable" to "necessary" eliminated the Graham v. Connor objective reasonableness test for California law enforcement. Under § 835a(b), the officer cannot use force simply because a reasonable officer might have done so. The officer must be unable to accomplish the lawful objective through less forceful means. This means de-escalation is not optional — it is a legal prerequisite to force when circumstances permit.

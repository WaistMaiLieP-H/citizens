# Reasoning — Cal. Penal Code § 835a (Use of Force — Necessity Standard)

**Standard ID:** PEN_CODE_835A_USE_OF_FORCE
**Layer:** 2 — Reasoning
**Status:** ACTIVE
**Filed:** 2026-04-20

---

## Why This Standard Exists

Section 835a was rewritten by AB 392 (2019) following a series of high-profile officer-involved deaths — most notably the 2018 shooting of Stephon Clark in his grandmother's backyard in Sacramento. The Legislature found that the prior "reasonable officer" standard effectively insulated officers from accountability: any officer could articulate a subjective fear and survive judicial review.

AB 392 replaced "reasonable" with "necessary" and added the officer-conduct-leading-up-to-force provision. These two changes transformed California use-of-force law from a subjective shield into an objective obligation: if force was not required to accomplish the lawful objective, it was not authorized.

---

## Why the Necessity Standard Is Stricter Than Federal Law

The federal Graham v. Connor (490 U.S. 386, 1989) standard asks whether the force was reasonable from the perspective of a reasonable officer on the scene, judged "without the benefit of 20/20 hindsight." This standard effectively validates most force decisions made in good faith.

California's § 835a(b) necessity standard asks a different question: was force actually required? Could the lawful objective have been achieved without force, or with less force? This prospective analysis — what was available to the officer before force was used — is structurally more demanding than the retrospective "reasonable officer" test.

For California cases, this means § 835a violations are provable even where no federal Fourth Amendment violation exists.

---

## Why the Officer's Pre-Force Conduct Is Included

Section 835a(d)(3)'s inclusion of the officer's own pre-force conduct reflects the Legislature's finding that officers frequently create the conditions that they then use to justify force. An officer who approaches a mentally ill person with weapon drawn, issues rapid commands the person cannot process, and then fires when the person moves is not responding to an imminent threat — the officer manufactured the threat through escalation.

By including pre-force conduct in the totality-of-circumstances analysis, § 835a eliminates the "I had to shoot because the situation was dangerous" defense when the officer's own actions created the danger.

---

## Why Unlawful Order Force Is Always Unjustified

Section 835a(c) codifies a self-evident principle: force to enforce a lawful order may be justified; force to enforce an unlawful order is never justified. An officer who detains a person without lawful authority — no reasonable suspicion, no probable cause, no warrant — cannot then use force to maintain that unlawful detention.

This provision has direct application to encounters where the initial detention lacked legal basis. If the June 16, 2023 encounter with Michael Hartmann involved a detention without reasonable suspicion or probable cause, any force used to maintain that detention — including physical separation from Cole — was per se unjustified under § 835a(c).

## Application in Practice

Section 835a analysis for the June 16, 2023 APD response requires:
- Was the initial detention lawful? (§ 836 analysis)
- Was any physical contact — including separation of Michael from Cole — applied?
- If so, was the contact necessary to accomplish a lawful objective?
- What was the officers' conduct leading up to any contact?
- Were de-escalation options available and not exhausted?
- Were body cameras active? (§ 832.18 cross-reference)
- If footage was not preserved after a complaint arose, the adverse inference applies.

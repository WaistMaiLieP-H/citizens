# Penal Code § 13701 — Domestic Violence Response: Historical Chain

## The Wound

Before mandatory DV response policies, police responses to domestic violence calls
were defined by officer discretion — and officer discretion almost universally favored
non-intervention. DV calls were treated as "family matters." Officers arrived, separated
the parties, calmed the scene, and left. No arrest. No report. No documentation. The
man who had just beaten his wife knew that calling police carried no consequence. The
cycle continued.

The wound was twofold:

**First wound — non-response.** Officers had the legal authority to arrest for DV
but no policy obligation to use it. The gap between authority and obligation meant
that most DV incidents — even when officers observed evidence of assault — resulted
in no action. The 1984 Minneapolis Domestic Violence Experiment had already demonstrated
that arrest deterred re-offense at higher rates than mediation or separation. California
law enforcement agencies ignored those findings for years.

**Second wound — no documentation.** When officers left a DV scene without writing
a report, the incident did not exist officially. The next time police were called to
the same address, they had no prior history. The victim who eventually sought a
restraining order had no police reports to attach. The perpetrator's history was
invisible. The absence of documentation protected perpetrators, not victims.

## The Legislative Response: A Decades-Long Build

**1979**: California enacted initial domestic violence response provisions. Officers
were encouraged — not required — to make arrests when probable cause existed.

**1985**: Legislature strengthened DV provisions following advocacy by domestic
violence organizations. Mandatory arrest added for felony DV; misdemeanor still
discretionary.

**1994-1995**: Major restructuring of California DV law, including strengthening of
the mandatory written policy requirement in § 13701. The section was amended to require
comprehensive written policies covering the specific categories now in the statute:
arrest decisions, victim assistance, incident reporting, evidence collection.

**The mandatory report requirement** — the most critical provision — was established
to address the "no documentation" wound directly. Officers no longer had discretion to
respond to a DV call and leave with nothing written. Every call, every time, a report.

**§ 836(c) — the mandatory arrest companion.** The mandatory report in § 13701 is
paired with the mandatory arrest provision in § 836(c): when an officer has probable
cause to believe DV occurred (even if the officer did not observe it), the officer
shall arrest. The combination — mandatory arrest + mandatory report — eliminated the
two escape hatches that had made DV policing toothless.

## Current State of the Law

Section 13701 currently requires every California law enforcement agency to have:

1. A written DV response policy covering the enumerated categories
2. A standardized DV incident form
3. A mandatory report completed on every DV call regardless of outcome
4. A victim information card or equivalent provided at every scene

These are not best practices or guidelines. They are statutory mandates. An agency
without a written policy is in violation. An officer who completes no report after a
DV call is in violation. Both violations create departmental liability.

The written policy requirement has the additional consequence of creating a
discoverable, auditable standard. Once APD's written DV response policy is obtained
via PRA request, every deviation from that policy by every officer on June 16, 2023
becomes a documented violation.

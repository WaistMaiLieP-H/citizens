# Lawful Arrest — Probable Cause and Warrantless Conditions — Origin 1872 (DV Reform 1984-2002)

## The Wound (Original — 1872)

The original California Penal Code (1872) codified the English common law arrest authority as California entered its second decade of statehood. The common law framework — felony arrests on probable cause without warrant; misdemeanor arrests only in the officer's presence — had developed over centuries and reflected a considered balance: the state's need to respond to ongoing criminal activity versus the individual's right to be free from arbitrary detention. Section 836 was the codification of that balance into California law.

The 1872 codification was not itself a response to a specific harm; it was the establishment of a legal framework for a state developing its institutional infrastructure. The wounds that drove subsequent amendments came later.

## The Wound (Domestic Violence — 1984-2000)

The most consequential gap in § 836's original framework was the treatment of domestic violence calls as optional arrest situations. Officers responding to DV calls routinely declined to arrest, framing the situation as a "civil matter" between spouses, or concluding that arrest would "make things worse." They left the scene without arresting, leaving abused partners with no immediate consequence for the abuser and no removal from the dangerous situation.

The empirical evidence documenting this failure was assembled in the early 1980s:

**The Minneapolis Domestic Violence Experiment (Sherman & Berk, 1984):** The first randomized controlled study of police response to DV misdemeanors found that arrest — compared to separation or mediation — was the most effective deterrent to repeat domestic violence over a 6-month follow-up period. Arrest reduced recidivism by approximately half compared to non-arrest responses. The study was published in the American Sociological Review and became the evidentiary foundation for legislative reform.

**National crime statistics:** Studies documented that DV was chronically underenforced relative to other crime categories of comparable severity. Officers, prosecutors, and courts systematically treated DV as less serious than equivalent non-domestic assault and battery.

**Murder following non-arrest:** Documented cases of victims being murdered by abusers whom police had declined to arrest on prior calls established that the non-arrest policy had fatal consequences. Legislative records from this period include specific documented cases where officers left DV scenes without arrest and the victim was subsequently killed.

**Violence Against Women Act (VAWA, 1994):** Congress enacted VAWA (34 U.S.C. § 10461 et seq.) with findings that domestic violence was nationally underenforced. VAWA required states to adopt mandatory or preferred arrest policies for DV as a condition of receiving federal grant funds. California's DV arrest reforms were consistent with VAWA's requirements.

## The Legislative Response

California amended § 836 to address DV calls in a series of legislative actions from the mid-1980s through the early 2000s:

**§ 836(b) — Victim notice:** Officers must advise DV victims of the right to make a citizen's arrest at every DV call. This provision reflects the reality that victims sometimes do not want the police to make the arrest decision for them, but they may not know they have independent legal authority to make a citizen's arrest.

**§ 836(c) — Mandatory arrest for protective order violations:** The most significant amendment. When a peace officer has probable cause to believe a domestic violence protective order has been violated by someone with notice of the order, the officer SHALL arrest — mandatory, not discretionary. This provision directly addressed the pattern of officers treating protective order violations as civil matters and leaving without arrest. The Legislature determined that where a court has already acted (by issuing a protective order), an officer's discretion to decline to enforce it is not consistent with the rule of law or victim safety.

**Dominant aggressor determination (§ 836(c)(3)):** Where mutual protective orders exist, the officer must identify the dominant aggressor — not just the first person who called 911 or who alleges they were hit first. The dominant aggressor framework prevents mandatory arrest provisions from being weaponized against victims by abusers who initiate contact or make preemptive allegations.

## What Changed

1872: Common law arrest authority codified. Felony arrests on probable cause; misdemeanor arrests in-presence only. No DV-specific provisions. DV treated as any other misdemeanor offense.

1984-2002 (DV amendments): § 836 transformed for DV context. Mandatory victim advisement (§ 836(b)). Mandatory arrest on protective order violations with notice (§ 836(c)) — "shall" replaces "may." Warrantless arrest authority for DV assault/battery not in officer's presence (§ 836(d)). Dominant aggressor framework to prevent mandatory arrest from harming victims. The discretionary DV arrest — officers' historical basis for non-intervention — effectively eliminated for the most serious DV enforcement situations.

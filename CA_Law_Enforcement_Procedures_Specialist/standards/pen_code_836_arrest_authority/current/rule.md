# Lawful Arrest — Probable Cause and Warrantless Conditions — Rule

**Authority:** Cal. Pen. Code § 836
**Jurisdiction:** California
**Effective:** Original codification 1872; DV amendments 1984–2002

## The Rule

A peace officer may arrest a person in obedience to a warrant. Without a warrant, a peace officer may arrest a person under three conditions (§ 836(a)):

1. The officer has probable cause to believe the person committed a public offense in the officer's presence.
2. The person has committed a felony, whether or not in the officer's presence.
3. The officer has probable cause to believe the person has committed a felony, whether or not a felony was in fact committed.

**Domestic violence calls — victim notice (§ 836(b)):** Any time an officer responds to a domestic violence call, the officer must make a good faith effort to inform the victim of their right to make a citizen's arrest. This requirement is excused only if the officer makes a DV-related arrest (PC § 243(e)(1) or § 273.5).

**Domestic violence protective order violations (§ 836(c)) — mandatory arrest:** If an officer responds to a call alleging violation of a domestic violence protective order and has probable cause to believe the person against whom the order was issued (1) had notice of the order and (2) committed an act violating it, the officer SHALL make a warrantless arrest. This is not discretionary. The arrest is mandatory regardless of whether the violation occurred in the officer's presence.

**DV assault/battery (§ 836(d)):** Where the suspect has committed assault or battery on a current or former spouse, cohabitant, dating partner, co-parent, child, or person 65+ within the second degree of relation, the officer may make a warrantless arrest if probable cause exists and the arrest is made as soon as probable cause arises.

**Probable cause standard:** The officer must have reasonable grounds to believe the facts constituting the offense occurred. This is an objective standard — what would a reasonable officer believe given the totality of circumstances — not what the officer subjectively believed.

## Key Elements

1. **Three warrantless arrest conditions** (§ 836(a)): In-presence misdemeanor / any felony committed / probable cause of felony — these are the only three bases absent a warrant.
2. **Mandatory DV protective order arrest** (§ 836(c)): "Shall" means mandatory, not discretionary. An officer who leaves without arresting a person who violated a protective order with notice has violated a legal duty, not just exercised poor judgment.
3. **Victim's right to citizen's arrest** (§ 836(b)): Required advisement — failing to give it is a documented policy violation, relevant to agency compliance under § 13701.

## What This Means in Practice

The most consequential word in § 836 is "shall" in subdivision (c). When a domestic violence protective order has been violated, the officer has no option to mediate, no option to tell the parties to "work it out," and no authority to leave without an arrest. An officer who does leave — and the protected party is subsequently harmed — has not just exercised bad judgment. The officer failed a statutory duty. That failure is relevant to both civil liability and to the § 832.7(b)(1)(E) analysis of whether an "unlawful" failure to arrest is a disclosable sustained finding.

# Lawful Arrest — Probable Cause and Warrantless Conditions — Reasoning

## Why This Law Exists

Arrest is the most visible exercise of state coercive authority over an individual. The Fourth Amendment to the U.S. Constitution and California Constitution Art. I, § 13 require that arrests be reasonable — generally, supported by a warrant based on probable cause. The warrant requirement is the rule; warrantless arrest is the exception. Section 836 codifies the exceptions that the common law, constitutional doctrine, and legislative judgment have found justified.

The three § 836(a) conditions reflect a calibrated framework: in-presence misdemeanors (officer can verify the offense directly); any felony committed (high severity justifies warrantless action); and probable cause of a felony (the officer's reasonable belief based on available evidence). For lesser offenses not committed in the officer's presence, the common law — and § 836 — requires a warrant. The warrant requirement is not a technicality; it is the mechanism that interposes judicial review between state coercive authority and individual liberty.

The domestic violence amendments are the most significant evolution of § 836. They exist because discretionary arrest authority failed victims. When officers could choose whether to arrest at a DV call, they routinely chose not to, leaving victims with batterers who faced no consequence. The mandatory arrest provisions for protective order violations removed officer discretion in precisely the circumstances where research showed discretion was producing harm.

## Legislative Intent

The original § 836 codified common law arrest authority in the 1872 California Penal Code. The domestic violence amendments were enacted progressively from the mid-1980s through the early 2000s in response to documented failures of the discretionary arrest model. The Minneapolis Domestic Violence Experiment (1984) established empirically that arrest was the most effective deterrent to repeat DV. California's legislature used this evidence to justify mandatory arrest provisions — converting "may arrest" to "shall arrest" for protective order violations.

The language of § 836(c) — "the officer shall... make a lawful arrest" — is deliberate statutory drafting. The legislature chose mandatory language to eliminate the officer-by-officer variation that had characterized DV response. An officer who makes a judgment call not to arrest when § 836(c) conditions are met is not exercising discretion — there is none to exercise.

## Doctrinal Basis

Section 836 operates within the constitutional framework established by the Fourth Amendment and California Art. I, § 13. Federal constitutional doctrine (United States v. Watson (1976) 423 U.S. 411) permits warrantless felony arrests on probable cause; California § 836 implements that authorization. The probable cause standard is objective: what would a reasonable officer believe given the totality of available information. A sincere but unreasonable belief is insufficient.

For DV calls, § 836 intersects with § 13701 (which requires agencies to adopt written DV policies) and the VAWA framework (34 U.S.C. § 10461 et seq., which conditions federal law enforcement grants on state adoption of arrest or prosecution policies). California's mandatory arrest provisions satisfy VAWA's preferred arrest requirement.

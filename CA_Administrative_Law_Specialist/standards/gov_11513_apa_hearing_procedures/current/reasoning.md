# GOV § 11513 APA Hearing Procedures — Reasoning and Historical Loss Analysis

**Citizen:** CA_Administrative_Law_Specialist
**Layer:** 3 — Reasoning / Historical Loss
**Build date:** 2026-04-13

---

## Why This Standard Exists — Administrative Due Process

The APA hearing exists to give the respondent (or applicant) a meaningful opportunity to be heard before the state takes adverse action. But a hearing without specific procedural protections is a hearing in name only. GOV §11513 defines what "a meaningful opportunity to be heard" actually requires in California administrative proceedings.

The key design choices in §11513 reflect a deliberate balance between two competing concerns:

**Concern 1 — Accessibility:** Administrative hearings should be accessible to non-lawyers. Technical evidence rules that trip up laypeople without serving truth-finding purposes have no place in proceedings where the parties often appear pro se. The "responsible persons" admissibility standard and the relaxed hearsay admission rule make hearings more accessible.

**Concern 2 — Integrity:** Loosening the rules must not come at the cost of truth. The oath requirement, the hearsay limitation (no sole-basis findings on hearsay without civil exception), and the cross-examination right are the integrity anchors. Without them, agencies can build findings on unsworn rumors without any opportunity for the party to challenge the source.

The §11513 framework: admit broadly, but require foundations for critical findings, preserve cross-examination, and maintain oath-based testimony.

## The Historical Loss — Hearings Without Teeth

Before the APA codified these protections, administrative hearings were often:

1. **Unsworn:** Agency officers accepted written submissions and oral statements without oath, giving them the procedural form of testimony without the legal accountability of perjury exposure.

2. **Hearsay-dominated:** Agency decisions were frequently based on reports, summaries, and third-party accounts — none of which were subject to cross-examination. A police report, a supervisor's account, an investigator's summary — these were often the entire record.

3. **Cross-examination-free:** The party adversely affected by the agency decision often had no opportunity to confront the evidence against them. The agency presented its case and the respondent responded, but confrontation — the right to ask the source of the accusation to answer questions — was not guaranteed.

The due process minimum for administrative proceedings was established in *Goldberg v. Kelly* (1970) 397 U.S. 254 (federal), which required cross-examination rights before benefit termination. California's §11513 provides comparable (and in the cross-examination scope, broader) protections at the state APA level.

## The Single-Source Denial Problem — CalVCB Pattern

The CalVCB A25-10117946 audit documented a "single-source denial" — the Board denied the application based on the police report without independently investigating or considering other available evidence. §11513(d) directly addresses this pattern:

**What single-source denial looks like in §11513 terms:**
- The agency's finding is: "Applicant was not a victim of violent crime"
- The sole evidence for this finding: the police report (which may have been prepared by an officer who did not believe the victim, or may contain errors)
- The police report is hearsay: out-of-court statements by the reporting officer
- If the applicant made a timely objection to this evidence as the sole basis for the denial finding, §11513(d) bars the finding

**Why agencies do this:** It is faster and cheaper to rely on the police report than to conduct an independent investigation. The APA's hearsay limitation is designed precisely to prevent this — agencies cannot take shortcuts by basing adverse findings on unreliable, unchallenged, unsworn hearsay.

**The correction mechanism:** §1094.5(b) — the finding is not supported by evidence (hearsay cannot be the sole basis) → abuse of discretion → writ of mandate.

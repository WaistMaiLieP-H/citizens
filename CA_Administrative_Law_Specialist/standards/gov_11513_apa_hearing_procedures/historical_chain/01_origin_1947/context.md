# Historical Chain — Origin: APA Hearing Procedures (1947)

**Standard:** gov_11513_apa_hearing_procedures
**Era:** 1947 — California APA enactment

---

## The Pre-APA Administrative Hearing

Before California's APA, administrative hearings were governed by individual agency statutes and common law due process minimum. The floor was low: due process required notice and some opportunity to be heard, but the form of the hearing was largely within agency discretion. There was no uniform cross-examination right, no hearsay limitation, no oath requirement codified across agencies.

The result was a patchwork: some agencies conducted rigorous hearings; others treated the "hearing" as a formality in which the agency's decision was predetermined and the respondent's participation was nominal.

## The APA Reform — 1945-1947

The California Legislature enacted the APA in stages in the mid-1940s, tracking federal APA reform. The hearing provisions (ultimately codified at GOV §§11500-11529) established uniform procedural requirements across all state agencies subject to the APA:

- Uniform oath requirement for oral testimony
- Codified cross-examination rights, explicitly extending scope beyond direct examination
- The "responsible persons" admissibility standard — a deliberate departure from civil evidence rules
- The hearsay limitation: admissible for supplementation/explanation; not sufficient alone as the basis for a finding
- Privilege rules: to the extent required by statute

These provisions represented the Legislature's conclusion that agency proceedings, while not courts, required court-like procedural integrity for the findings that would bind the parties.

## Goldberg v. Kelly (1970) — Federal Due Process Anchor

The U.S. Supreme Court's decision in *Goldberg v. Kelly* (1970) 397 U.S. 254 established that the Due Process Clause requires cross-examination rights before welfare benefit termination. California's §11513(b) cross-examination provision, already in place, was consistent with and strengthened by the federal constitutional minimum Goldberg established. The practical effect: §11513(b) cross-examination rights are not merely statutory — they reflect a constitutional minimum for proceedings that affect significant interests.

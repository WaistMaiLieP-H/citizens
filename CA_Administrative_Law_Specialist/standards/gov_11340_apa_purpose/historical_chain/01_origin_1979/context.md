# Historical Chain — Origin: APA Purpose Findings (1979)

**Standard:** gov_11340_apa_purpose
**Era:** 1979 — OAL establishment; modern APA chapter begins

---

## Pre-1979 — The Regulatory Accountability Gap

California's APA existed before 1979, but the modern chapter — including §11340's findings — reflects the reforms of the late 1970s that established the Office of Administrative Law. Before those reforms:

- No central body reviewed regulations for legal authority, clarity, or necessity
- Each agency's internal rulemaking process varied significantly
- "Regulations" that had never gone through any formal process had quasi-legal status in some agencies
- The regulated community — particularly small businesses and unrepresented parties — had limited ability to challenge unclear or unauthorized regulations

## The OAL Reform (1979) and §11340's Findings

The OAL establishment (Stats. 1979, ch. 567) created a central review body and prompted the Legislature to articulate, for the first time in a single statutory provision, the specific findings that justified the reform. GOV §11340 was enacted or significantly amended in this period to provide the legislative findings that:

1. Documented the specific harms the pre-OAL system had caused
2. Provided the interpretive framework for the APA requirements OAL would enforce
3. Created an equity principle (§11340(g)) specifically protecting small businesses from regulatory complexity

## The Purpose Findings as Living Law

Unlike most "purpose" statutory provisions, GOV §11340 is actively used by courts interpreting the APA. It is not mere legislative rhetoric — it is the lens through which ambiguous APA requirements are read. When courts ask whether a particular agency action required rulemaking, or whether a particular notice satisfied the APA, the §11340 findings provide the substantive answer: does this interpretation serve the goals of clarity, necessity, authorization, and equitable access the Legislature identified?

# Historical Chain — Origin: Regulation Validity Challenge (1979)

**Standard:** gov_11350_regulation_validity_challenge
**Era:** 1979 — OAL establishment and APA rulemaking reform

---

## Pre-1979 — Regulations Without Central Review

Before the Office of Administrative Law (OAL) was established in 1979, California agencies could adopt regulations through their own internal processes with limited external accountability. The APA rulemaking chapter existed (enacted with the original APA in the 1940s-1950s), but there was no central body charged with reviewing regulations for legal authority, clarity, and necessity before they took effect.

The result, documented in GOV §11340's legislative findings: unprecedented regulatory growth, unclear language, and unnecessary burdens on regulated parties — particularly small businesses that could not afford expert assistance in navigating complex agency rules.

## The OAL Reform — 1979

In 1979, the California Legislature established the Office of Administrative Law (Stats. 1979, ch. 567) as a central review body for California regulations. The OAL was charged with reviewing proposed regulations for:
- Legal authority (is the regulation authorized by the enabling statute?)
- Necessity (is the regulation reasonably necessary to carry out the statute's purpose?)
- Clarity (is the regulation written in plain language that regulated parties can understand?)
- Consistency (does the regulation conflict with other law?)

The OAL reform also mandated the rulemaking file (§11347.3) — a complete record of the rulemaking process, including the notice of proposed rulemaking, public comments, the agency's responses to comments, and the final statement of reasons. This file became the evidentiary universe for §11350 challenges.

## GOV §11350 as the Enforcement Mechanism

GOV §11350 was strengthened in the 1979 reform period to provide an accessible judicial challenge mechanism. The "any interested person" standing, the no-exhaustion rule, and the closed-record review were designed to make rulemaking accountability realistic without opening the courts to unlimited general policy challenges.

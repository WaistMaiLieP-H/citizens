## Why This Standard Exists — The Accountability Gap in Rulemaking

Agencies don't just adjudicate — they legislate. When an agency promulgates a regulation, it is making law that binds everyone subject to its jurisdiction. Unlike statutes, regulations are not debated in public, voted on by elected representatives, or subject to the political accountability that makes legislation legitimate. The APA's rulemaking procedures are the substitute for that political accountability: public notice, opportunity to comment, a finding that the regulation is necessary, OAL review for clarity and legal authority.

When agencies bypass these procedures — adopting regulations without rulemaking, issuing emergency regulations without genuine emergencies, promulgating rules without necessity findings — they are making law without accountability. GOV §11350 is the mechanism for challenging that accountability gap in court.

---

## The Policy Structure

**Who can challenge:** Any interested person — the barrier is low because unlawful regulations harm the regulated community broadly.

**No exhaustion required:** The legislature chose not to require that challengers first petition the agency. The rationale: if the regulation itself is invalid, the agency that promulgated it has a structural conflict of interest in reviewing challenges to its own rulemaking.

**Closed record review:** Only the rulemaking file and related materials. Courts do not re-examine the merits of the policy — only whether the agency followed the process required to make it law.

**Omission as evidence:** The most powerful provision is §11350(d)(3). If the rulemaking file is missing a required element, that omission can be proved and used as evidence. This means an agency cannot simply bury its procedural failures by creating a thin record — the missing item itself is provable.

## The Historical Loss — Regulations Without Accountability

Before OAL review and APA rulemaking requirements, California's regulatory landscape was a thicket of agency-made law adopted without public input, without necessity findings, and without consistency review. GOV §11340's legislative findings document the result: unprecedented regulatory growth, unclear and complex language, unnecessary burdens on small businesses who cannot afford experts to parse the rules.

The historical loss: regulated parties — including small businesses, individual applicants, and vulnerable populations — were bound by regulations they had no opportunity to challenge, that were often unnecessary or unclear, and that agencies could adopt and modify without meaningful accountability.

§11350 addresses this loss by creating a cause of action that is:
- Accessible (any interested person, no exhaustion)
- Grounded in the record (the rulemaking file is the evidence)
- Objective (was the process followed, not was the policy wise)

## Application to the Case Record

The CalVCB shortened deadline issue is the primary §11350 candidate in this case record. If the shortened deadlines were imposed through regulation rather than statute, and if the regulation was adopted without proper APA rulemaking:

- **The rulemaking file is the proof:** If CalVCB cannot produce a complete rulemaking file for the shortened deadline provision, §11350(d)(3) makes the omission itself evidence of process failure
- **The systemic remedy:** Unlike §1094.5 (which reverses the specific decision), a successful §11350 challenge invalidates the regulation prospectively — protecting all future applicants from deadline compression without statutory authority

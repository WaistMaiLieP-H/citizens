# CMIA §56.20 — Historical Chain

## The Right to See Your Own Records

---

### The Wound

For most of the history of American medicine, medical records were the provider's property. Patients had no legal right to see their own charts, read their own diagnoses, or verify what was being written about them. A doctor could write anything in a medical record — a psychiatric diagnosis, a substance abuse notation, a disability assessment — and the patient would never know unless someone told them.

This created a fundamental power asymmetry. Providers, insurers, employers, and government agencies could make decisions about a person's life based on medical records the person had never seen and could not challenge. Disability claims were denied based on records the claimant could not review. Custody evaluations were conducted using medical histories the parent could not verify. Conservatorship petitions were filed citing conditions the proposed conservatee could not confirm or dispute.

The wound was not just secrecy. It was the inability to defend yourself when your own medical information was being used against you.

### The Promise

Section 56.20 promised: you have the right to see what they wrote about you. Every provider must let you inspect your records and give you copies within 5 working days of a written request. If you want a summary, they must provide it within 10 working days. They cannot charge you for retrieval or handling — only reasonable costs for copying, media, and postage. And if you want your records sent to another provider, they must transfer them within 15 days.

The 2002 amendments added specific timelines. The 2019 amendments (AB 1076) added fee protections, ensuring that excessive fees could not be used as a barrier to access. The arc of amendment has been toward making access faster, cheaper, and harder to obstruct.

### The Arc

Patient access is the affirmative counterpart to confidentiality. Sections 56.10 and 56.11 prevent others from seeing your records without authorization. Section 56.20 ensures you can always see your own records without obstruction. Together, they create the CMIA's core balance: your information is protected from others and accessible to you.

For the steward's case, §56.20 asks a direct question: was the steward ever prevented from seeing his own medical records? Were requests ignored? Were records produced late? Were they incomplete? Were excessive fees charged to discourage access? Each failure is an independent violation — and a pattern of failures is evidence that someone was controlling what the steward was allowed to know about his own medical history. In a conservatorship context, information control is the mechanism of control itself.

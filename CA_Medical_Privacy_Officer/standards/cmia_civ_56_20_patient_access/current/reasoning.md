# CMIA Employer Confidentiality Obligations for Employee Medical Information — Reasoning

## Why This Law Exists

Section 56.20 recognizes that employers occupy a uniquely coercive position with respect to their employees' medical information. When an employee submits a doctor's note for leave, undergoes an employer-required fitness-for-duty examination, or provides medical documentation for a workers' compensation claim, the employer obtains sensitive health information that the employee effectively had no choice but to disclose. Pre-CMIA, that information was then routinely used in employment decisions — not just for the stated administrative purpose but for evaluating the employee's future, sharing with supervisors and coworkers, and sometimes communicating to prospective employers.

The legislature created § 56.20 to ensure that medical information disclosed in the employment context flows in one direction — to the specific administrative purpose for which it was required — and does not become a general-purpose tool for employment management.

## Legislative Intent

The anti-discrimination provision in § 56.20(b) — prohibiting retaliation against employees who refuse to sign authorizations — reflects a core legislative concern about coerced consent. An authorization signed under threat of termination is not meaningful consent. The statute protects employees from being punished for asserting their privacy rights, while acknowledging that the employer may need to take action necessitated by the absence of the medical information (for example, denying a leave request not supported by documentation).

The 2019 amendment (AB 1076) addressing fee restrictions reflects the same anti-coercion principle applied to access: the statute is designed to prevent employers from creating financial barriers to employees asserting their rights.

## Doctrinal Basis

Section 56.20 is grounded in both the California constitutional right to privacy (Art. I, § 1) and in labor law principles protecting employees from abusive conditions of employment. The four permitted uses in § 56.20(c) — compulsion, litigation, benefit plan administration, and emergency treatment — represent the legislature's enumeration of the legitimate purposes for which employer possession of medical information is justified. Any use outside those four categories, without a signed authorization, is a CMIA violation that triggers the remedies in §§ 56.35 and 56.36.

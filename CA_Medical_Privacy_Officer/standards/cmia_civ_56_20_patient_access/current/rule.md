# CMIA Employer Confidentiality Obligations for Employee Medical Information — Rule

**Authority:** Cal. Civ. Code § 56.20
**Jurisdiction:** California
**Effective:** 1981 (most recent amendment: Stats. 2019, Ch. 832, Sec. 2, AB 1076, effective January 1, 2020)

## The Rule

When an employer receives an employee's medical information — through disability leave requests, workers' compensation, FMLA documentation, fitness-for-duty exams, or similar processes — that employer is bound by specific confidentiality obligations. The employer must:

1. **Establish protective procedures:** Create and maintain procedures to ensure the confidentiality of employee medical information and protect it from unauthorized use and disclosure, including employee training and access controls.
2. **Not discriminate for refusal to sign:** An employee cannot be punished for refusing to sign an authorization for release of medical information — but the employer may take action that is "necessary" in the absence of the medical information the employee declined to provide.
3. **Not use or disclose without authorization:** The employer may not use, disclose, or allow its employees or agents to use or disclose medical information about its employees without the employee's signed § 56.11 authorization, except for four limited purposes: (a) judicial/administrative compulsion; (b) litigation where the employee placed their medical condition at issue; (c) administering employee benefit plans, disability income, workers' compensation, and leave eligibility; and (d) providing information to a treating health care provider when the employee cannot authorize it.

## Key Elements

1. **Employers are covered entities under CMIA:** Not just health care providers — employers who receive employees' medical information as part of employment administration are bound to the same confidentiality requirements.
2. **Benefit plan administration exception:** Medical information may be used to administer health care plans, disability plans, and workers' compensation — but only for that purpose, not for employment decisions.
3. **No retaliation for non-authorization:** An employee's refusal to sign a medical release cannot be used as a disciplinary basis — protecting employees from coerced consent.
4. **Written policy extends protection further:** If an employer has a written policy promising greater protection, the employer must comply with that policy even where the statute would permit disclosure.

## What This Means in Practice

An employer who receives an employee's medical records through any channel — a doctor's note for leave, a fitness-for-duty examination, a disability accommodation request — is prohibited from sharing that information with supervisors, coworkers, or third parties for any purpose other than the specific administrative functions listed in the statute. Using an employee's medical information to make employment decisions, sharing it with management, or allowing it to flow to persons not involved in benefit administration is a CMIA violation regardless of how the employer obtained the information.

# CMIA Employer Confidentiality Obligations — Origin 1981

## The Wound

The employment context created a specific and documented category of medical privacy violation before 1981: employees who disclosed medical information to their employers for legitimate administrative purposes — disability leave, fitness-for-duty, workers' compensation — and then found that information used against them in ways they never anticipated.

The documented pattern involved medical information flowing from HR files to supervisors, from supervisors to coworkers through workplace gossip with management facilitation, and from current employers to prospective employers through reference calls that crossed into health status. An employee who disclosed a cancer diagnosis to obtain FMLA leave found that their diagnosis had become known throughout their department within weeks. An employee who filed a workers' compensation claim found that their injury history was mentioned in a prospective employer reference call.

Employers were not treating medical information as confidential because there was no law saying they had to. They treated it as part of the overall employment file, subject to the same internal access and reference practices as performance reviews and disciplinary records.

## The Legislative Response

The CMIA's Chapter 3 (Stats. 1981, Ch. 782) specifically addressed the employer-as-recipient context. The legislature recognized that the employment relationship creates a power asymmetry: employees often cannot refuse to disclose medical information without losing benefits, leave, or employment itself — so meaningful consent to the original disclosure is effectively impossible. The statute's response was to regulate what the employer could do with information once received, rather than trying to prevent the receipt.

Section 56.20's four exceptions to the non-disclosure requirement (compulsion, litigation, benefit administration, emergency treatment) reflect the legislature's identification of legitimate employer uses of employee medical information — every other use is prohibited without a signed authorization.

## What Changed

Before 1981: An employer who received an employee's medical information through any channel had no statutory obligation to keep it confidential, restrict internal access, or refrain from using it in employment decisions.

After 1981: Employers who receive medical information must establish confidentiality procedures, may not use or disclose it outside four specified purposes without authorization, and may not retaliate against employees who decline to provide additional authorization. Using an employee's medical information in employment decisions — outside the permitted exceptions — is a CMIA violation.

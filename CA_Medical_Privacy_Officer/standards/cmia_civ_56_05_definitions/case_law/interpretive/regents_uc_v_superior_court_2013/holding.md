# Regents of University of California v. Superior Court, 220 Cal.App.4th 549 (2013)

## The Case

The question was whether the University of California, operating its medical centers, qualified as a "provider of health care" under CMIA §56.05(i) such that the CMIA's confidentiality requirements applied to its handling of patient medical information. The University argued it was a state entity subject to different disclosure rules.

## What the Court Held

The Court of Appeal held:

1. **The CMIA's definition of "provider of health care" is expansive.** It includes any person licensed or certified under Division 2 of the Business and Professions Code, any clinic or health facility licensed under Division 2 of the Health and Safety Code, and state agencies that provide health care services. The University of California's medical centers fall squarely within this definition.

2. **State entities that provide health care are not exempt from the CMIA.** The CMIA does not carve out a government exception. When a state entity operates as a health care provider, it is bound by the same medical information confidentiality requirements as a private provider.

3. **The definition extends to all individuals and entities within the health care delivery system.** Physicians, nurses, clinics, hospitals, pharmacies, laboratories — anyone licensed or certified to provide health care services is a "provider of health care" under §56.05(i).

## The Rule

**State entities operating as health care providers are "providers of health care" under CMIA §56.05(i) and are subject to CMIA confidentiality requirements. The definition encompasses the full scope of licensed and certified health care entities.**

## Why This Case Matters

For the steward's case: if any state-operated or publicly funded health care entity held the steward's medical information — county mental health, state hospital system, VA if acting through state contract, any UC medical center — that entity is a "provider of health care" under §56.05(i) and is bound by CMIA. There is no government exception. If the conservatorship involved court-ordered evaluations at a state facility, that facility remains bound by CMIA.

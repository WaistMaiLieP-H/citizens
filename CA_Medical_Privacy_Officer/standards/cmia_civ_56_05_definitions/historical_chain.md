# CMIA §56.05 — Historical Chain

## Who Counts, What Counts, and Why the Definitions Matter

---

### The Wound

Before the CMIA, California had no statutory definition of "medical information" that carried legal consequences. Medical records were treated as the provider's property, not the patient's data. Employers routinely demanded complete medical histories as a condition of employment. Insurance companies accessed diagnosis and treatment details far beyond what was needed for payment. There was no legal boundary between "information about a person's health" and "information anyone could use however they wanted."

The absence of definitions meant the absence of limits. If "medical information" was not legally defined, it could not be legally protected. If "provider of health care" had no statutory meaning, no one bore a legal duty to keep records confidential. The wound was definitional: without language, there was no law.

### The Promise

The 1981 CMIA definitions promised to draw bright lines around medical privacy. Section 56.05 said: here is exactly what information is protected (individually identifiable medical information), here is exactly who holds it (providers, plans, contractors, pharmaceutical companies), and here is exactly whose information it is (the patient — a natural person, living or dead).

The 2000 amendments expanded the definitions to cover pharmaceutical companies and broadened the employer definition. The 2013 amendments added confidential communications requests aligned with HIPAA. The 2020 amendments added the HIPAA de-identification safe harbor, clarifying that properly de-identified data falls outside the CMIA's scope — but individually identifiable data remains fully protected.

Each amendment reflected a new frontier of medical information misuse discovered after the original 1981 line was drawn.

### The Arc

Section 56.05 is the foundation section. Every other CMIA section — disclosure restrictions (§56.10), further-disclosure prohibitions (§56.11), access rights (§56.20), damages (§56.35) — depends on §56.05's definitions. The arc is: define the territory, then protect it.

For the steward's case, §56.05 answers threshold questions. Are spine surgery records "medical information"? Yes — individually identifiable information regarding a patient's medical history, mental or physical condition, or treatment. Is Walgreens a "provider of health care"? Yes — a health facility licensed under Division 2 of the Health and Safety Code. Is the steward a "patient"? Yes — a natural person who received health care services. Is Blue Shield a "health care service plan"? Yes — an entity regulated under Knox-Keene.

Once these definitions are confirmed, every CMIA protection follows. Without them, nothing does.

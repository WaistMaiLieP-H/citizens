# CMIA Definitions — Origin 1981

## The Wound

Before California defined what "medical information" legally meant and who was legally obligated to protect it, the answer to both questions depended on informal professional norms and contractual arrangements that varied by institution. Hospital privacy policies were inconsistent. Insurers were not treated as entities with obligations to the patient — only to the plan member. Pharmaceutical companies that received individual patient-level data from the institutions they contracted with had no statutory obligations at all.

The definitional vacuum had a specific practical consequence: entities that received patient health data through indirect channels — contractors, data processors, pharmaceutical benefit managers — could argue they were not "providers" and therefore outside any privacy obligation. Patients seeking to hold these downstream recipients accountable had no clear statutory basis for their claims.

## The Legislative Response

The 1981 CMIA (Stats. 1981, Ch. 782) established definitions that were broader than the entities subject to federal regulation. The legislature specifically included contractors — medical service organizations, independent practice associations, pharmacy benefit managers — within the statute's coverage, recognizing that the patient's medical information flowed through these entities as readily as through licensed providers. The legislature also included pharmaceutical companies, which were not included in any federal framework until HIPAA's business associate provisions decades later.

Subsequent amendments (2000, 2013, 2020) expanded the definitions to track changes in how medical information was collected and processed — adding digital health applications, mental health apps, and reproductive health services as the market for digital health data grew.

## What Changed

Before 1981: No statutory definition of "medical information" governed private entities in California. Privacy protection was governed by professional norms, not enforceable rules. Contractors and pharmaceutical companies had no statutory privacy obligations to patients.

After 1981: "Medical information," "provider," "patient," "contractor," and "pharmaceutical company" are defined terms with specific legal consequences. Any entity meeting the definition is bound by the CMIA. Patients can establish a covered relationship — and thus a legal claim — without having to prove that a non-provider entity owed them a duty under common law.

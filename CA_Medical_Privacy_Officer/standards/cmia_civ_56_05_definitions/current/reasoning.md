# CMIA Definitions — Reasoning

## Why This Law Exists

Section 56.05 exists because the substantive protections of the CMIA are only as strong as the precision of the terms they apply to. "Medical information," "provider," and "patient" are the jurisdictional perimeter of the entire statute — too narrow and violators escape coverage; too vague and the statute becomes unenforceable. The legislature created explicit definitions in 1981 and has amended them several times to close gaps as technology and the health care industry evolved.

The decision to include pharmaceutical companies as covered entities — unlike federal HIPAA, which covers only providers, plans, and clearinghouses — reflects California's recognition that pharmaceutical companies collect, analyze, and monetize patient-level data in ways that create the same privacy risks as disclosures by providers. The 2020 amendments (AB 713) adding mental health application information and reproductive health application information reflect the legislature's awareness that digital health apps were collecting sensitive health data outside the traditional provider relationship and needed to be brought within the CMIA's scope.

## Legislative Intent

The definitional section is the legislature's tool for keeping CMIA coverage aligned with how medical information actually moves. The definition of "medical information" is format-neutral and derivation-inclusive — it covers information derived from a provider's records, not just the records themselves. This prevents covered entities from arguing that an analysis or summary prepared from patient data is not "medical information" because it is not itself a clinical record.

The 2020 expansion to include immigration status as medical information when known or collected by a covered entity reflects a specific legislative concern: that immigration status, if treated as administrative rather than medical data, could be disclosed to immigration enforcement without CMIA protection. The legislature closed that gap by treating it as medical information when collected in a health care context.

## Doctrinal Basis

Section 56.05 implements the California constitutional right to privacy (Cal. Const. Art. I, § 1) by defining the boundaries of what is protected. Courts have consistently held that the CMIA definitions are to be read broadly to effectuate the statute's protective purpose. The definitions form the foundation for every CMIA violation analysis: if the defendant is a "provider," "plan," "pharmaceutical company," or "contractor"; if the information is "medical information"; and if the person is a "patient," then the CMIA applies.

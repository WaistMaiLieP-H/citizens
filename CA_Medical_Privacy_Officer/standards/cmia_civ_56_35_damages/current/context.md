# Context — California Civil Code § 56.35 CMIA Damages

## The statute in brief

Section 56.35 is the CMIA's enforcement teeth. It converts a disclosure violation under §§ 56.10, 56.11, 56.20, and other CMIA provisions into a calculable civil damages claim. Three damage tiers exist:

**Tier 1 — Negligent release:** $1,000 nominal damages per violation, plus exemplary damages of $100–$3,000 per plaintiff, attorney fees, costs. No proof of actual harm required.

**Tier 2 — Intentional/knowing release:** Civil Code § 3333 actual damages (consequential damages, emotional distress, economic loss) plus attorney fees, costs.

**Tier 3 — Willful violation:** Punitive damages on top of actual damages.

## The $1,000 nominal damages rule

The most operationally significant provision is the $1,000 nominal damages award for negligent release. The plaintiff does not need to prove actual damages — the statutory amount is automatic once a negligent unauthorized disclosure is established.

For cases involving multiple violations (e.g., disclosure to multiple parties, or a single disclosure document containing multiple records of multiple patients), each violation may support a separate $1,000 award. Courts have split on whether the $1,000 applies per patient, per incident, or per disclosure.

## Attorney fees provision

Section 56.35(d)(4) provides for attorney fees to the prevailing plaintiff "as determined by the court." This is a fee-shifting provision that makes CMIA cases economically viable even when actual damages are limited, because the defendant must pay the plaintiff's counsel upon a successful CMIA claim.

## Relationship to § 56.36

Section 56.36 addresses criminal penalties and enhanced civil liability for individuals who intentionally obtain and use confidential medical records for personal gain or with intent to harm the patient. Section 56.35 is the standard civil remedy framework; § 56.36 adds criminal and enhanced civil consequences for the more culpable forms of misconduct.

## Application to Case #37 / steward's medical fraud timeline

The steward's documented medical fraud timeline (2014-2022: spine surgery fraud, SIRVA, systematic denial of disability, SSA/DDS phantom contact) involves multiple potential unauthorized disclosures of his medical information. If any provider shared his records outside the enumerated exceptions in § 56.10(b)/(c):

1. **Each unauthorized disclosure** is a separate § 56.35 violation supporting $1,000 nominal damages
2. **If the disclosure was knowing/intentional** (e.g., as part of a coordinated identity replacement or medical suppression scheme), § 3333 actual damages apply — which can include compensation lost, medical costs incurred, emotional distress
3. **If the disclosure was willful** (e.g., deliberately sharing records to enable fraudulent disability denial), punitive damages are available
4. **Attorney fees** are available on any successful claim, making representation economically viable

The § 56.10(c)(12) probate court investigator exception (documented in the § 56.10 manifest) is load-bearing here: if records were disclosed to a probate court investigator as part of a covert conservatorship investigation, those disclosures were legally authorized under § 56.10(c)(12) — but the investigator's identity and the scope of the disclosures can be discovered through CMIA disclosure logs, providing documentary proof that a conservatorship investigation occurred.

## Statute of limitations

CMIA damages claims are subject to the one-year medical records statute of limitations under Code of Civil Procedure § 340.5 (medical malpractice) only when the defendant is a health care provider. For non-provider defendants, the general two-year limitations period under CCP § 335.1 applies. The discovery rule tolls the limitations period when the violation was concealed.

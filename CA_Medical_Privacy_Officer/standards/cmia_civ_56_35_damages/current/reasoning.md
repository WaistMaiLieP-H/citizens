# CMIA Compensatory and Punitive Damages for Wrongful Disclosure — Reasoning

## Why This Law Exists

Section 56.35 provides the private civil remedy for patients who suffered actual harm from a CMIA violation. Unlike § 56.36's nominal damages track (which does not require proof of harm), § 56.35 addresses the fuller range of losses a patient experiences when their medical information is misused — lost employment, denied insurance, emotional distress, damaged relationships, and the cascading economic consequences of having private health information exposed.

The attorney's fees provision in § 56.35 is the mechanism that makes private enforcement viable. Without fee recovery, a patient with modest actual damages could not economically justify retaining counsel to litigate a CMIA claim — making the law unenforceable in practice for most violations. The legislature's decision to include attorney's fees signals that private enforcement is intended to be the primary mechanism, not a backup to regulatory action.

## Legislative Intent

The $3,000 cap on punitive damages in § 56.35 is deliberately modest compared to the $25,000-$250,000 civil penalties available under § 56.36. Section 56.35 is designed as a compensatory remedy with a modest punitive component to deter knowing violations, while § 56.36 carries the escalating penalty structure for negligent and willful conduct. The two sections work in tandem: § 56.35 compensates the victim; § 56.36 deters and punishes the violator.

## Doctrinal Basis

Section 56.35 implements the California constitutional right to privacy (Art. I, § 1) through a private right of action that runs directly from patient to violator, without any administrative prerequisite. Patients do not need to file a complaint with a state agency, obtain a right-to-sue letter, or exhaust any administrative remedy before filing suit under § 56.35. The cause of action accrues when the unauthorized disclosure occurs and the patient sustains harm — subject to the applicable statute of limitations.

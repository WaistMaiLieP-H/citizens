# CMIA Compensatory and Punitive Damages for Wrongful Disclosure — Rule

**Authority:** Cal. Civ. Code § 56.35
**Jurisdiction:** California
**Effective:** 1981 (most recent amendment: Stats. 2013, Ch. 444, Sec. 4, effective January 1, 2014)

## The Rule

A patient whose medical information has been used or disclosed in violation of Civil Code §§ 56.10 (disclosure prohibitions), 56.104 (mental health records), 56.107 (substance abuse records), or 56.20 (employer confidentiality obligations) — and who suffered economic loss or personal injury as a result — may sue for all of the following in a single action:

1. **Compensatory damages:** The full amount of economic loss and personal injury caused by the violation.
2. **Punitive damages:** Up to $3,000. The statute caps punitive damages separately from compensatory damages — this is not a multiplier but a distinct additional award.
3. **Attorney's fees:** Up to $1,000, recoverable on top of other damages.
4. **Litigation costs:** Recoverable in addition to fees and damages.

This remedy provision operates alongside (not instead of) the enhanced civil penalties in § 56.36, which are available for negligent and knowing/willful violations without proof of economic loss.

## Key Elements

1. **Requires actual harm:** Unlike § 56.36's nominal damages track, § 56.35 requires the patient to have sustained "economic loss or personal injury" — there must be real harm, not just a technical violation.
2. **Punitive damages capped at $3,000:** The cap is low; § 56.35 functions primarily as a compensatory remedy with an attorney's fee provision to make litigation economically viable.
3. **Attorney's fees make small claims viable:** Even modest recoveries justify representation when attorney's fees up to $1,000 are separately recoverable.
4. **Violations covered:** §§ 56.10 (providers), 56.104 (mental health), 56.107 (substance abuse), and 56.20 (employer) — not all CMIA sections trigger § 56.35.

## What This Means in Practice

Section 56.35 is the private right of action for patients who can prove they were actually harmed — economically or personally — by an unauthorized disclosure. When a provider shares a patient's diagnosis with an employer, an insurer cancels coverage based on undisclosed medical information, or medical records are used to deny disability benefits, the patient has compensatory and punitive damages available under § 56.35. The attorney's fee provision is the mechanism that makes pursuing these claims economically realistic for individual plaintiffs.

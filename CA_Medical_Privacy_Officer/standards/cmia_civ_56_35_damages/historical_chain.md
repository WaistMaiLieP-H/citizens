# Historical Chain — California Civil Code § 56.35 CMIA Damages

## The wound

When California enacted the Confidentiality of Medical Information Act (CMIA) in 1981, it identified a problem that went beyond defining privacy rights: it had to make those rights enforceable. The pre-CMIA landscape had a familiar failure mode. A patient could argue in court that their medical records had been improperly disclosed — but to win damages, they had to prove that the disclosure caused them actual, compensable harm. That was often impossible.

The mechanisms of medical privacy violations rarely produced easily traceable harm. A doctor who shared a patient's psychiatric records with an insurance company might affect the patient's coverage — but proving the causal chain (disclosure → insurer decision → coverage denial → financial loss → quantifiable damages) was expensive, speculative, and time-consuming. The result: most medical privacy violations went uncompensated because the victim could not meet the actual-damages burden.

The wound § 56.35 addresses: medical privacy rights without a realistic damages mechanism are unenforceable in practice. Insurance companies, employers, and health care providers who violated medical privacy faced no meaningful economic deterrent as long as the patients they harmed could not quantify their losses.

## The legislative response

The 1981 CMIA enactment included § 56.35 as an enforcement mechanism from the start. The legislature made two structural choices that determine how the section operates:

1. **Nominal damages without proof of harm:** The $1,000 per-violation nominal damages award allows a patient whose records were negligently disclosed to recover without proving actual injury. This is the mechanism that makes CMIA enforcement economically viable.

2. **Tiered liability:** Negligence gets nominal + exemplary; intentional/knowing conduct gets actual damages under § 3333; willful conduct gets punitive damages. The tiering creates proportionate deterrence — casual carelessness is penalized at $1,000 per incident; deliberate disclosure for personal gain or to harm a patient is penalized much more severely.

The attorney-fees provision completed the enforcement architecture. By shifting fees to the defendant, the legislature ensured that attorneys could afford to bring CMIA cases on behalf of patients even when the per-incident recovery was modest. This is the same logic underlying consumer protection fee-shifting statutes: without fee-shifting, individual rights against institutional defendants are underenforced.

## 2013 amendment

Stats. 2013, Ch. 444 amended § 56.35, codifying the tiered structure more clearly and confirming the nominal damages rule. The 2013 amendment is the current governing version.

## The current state

Section 56.35 creates three tracks of CMIA damages:
- Negligent disclosure: $1,000 nominal + $100-$3,000 exemplary + attorney fees
- Intentional/knowing disclosure: § 3333 actual damages + attorney fees
- Willful disclosure: punitive damages + actual damages + attorney fees

The $1,000 nominal damages provision is the operative floor for every unauthorized disclosure claim — it removes the actual-harm barrier that previously made medical privacy rights unenforceable.

## Steward relevance (medical fraud timeline 2014-2022)

The CMIA damages framework applies to every unauthorized disclosure identified in the steward's medical fraud documentation. The key enforcement leverage is the $1,000 nominal damages floor: each unauthorized disclosure supports a recoverable claim without needing to quantify the specific harm that disclosure caused. Where the disclosure was part of a systematic identity replacement or medical suppression scheme — a pattern of intentional conduct — § 3333 actual damages and punitive damages become available.

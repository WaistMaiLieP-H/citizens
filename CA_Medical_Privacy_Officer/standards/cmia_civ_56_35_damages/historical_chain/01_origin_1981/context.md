# CMIA Compensatory and Punitive Damages — Origin 1981

## The Wound

California's pre-CMIA common law provided tort remedies for privacy violations, but they were largely inaccessible to the patients most harmed by medical information misuse. The tort of public disclosure of private facts required showing that the disclosure was "highly offensive to a reasonable person" and widely disseminated — a standard that excluded most one-on-one medical information disclosures between providers, insurers, and employers. The tort of intrusion upon seclusion required showing a physically or electronically intrusive act — not satisfied by a physician simply telling an insurer about a diagnosis. Negligence claims required proving that the defendant owed a duty, breached it, and that the breach was the proximate cause of quantifiable economic harm.

For the patient whose medical information was disclosed to an employer or insurer, the damages question was nearly insurmountable: proving that the specific disclosure caused the specific adverse employment or insurance outcome, and quantifying that harm in dollars, required connecting private corporate decisions to a specific piece of medical information — typically impossible without discovery that the plaintiff could not obtain before filing a case with no provable damages.

## The Legislative Response

The 1981 CMIA created a statutory cause of action with a specific remedies provision (§ 56.35) that bypassed the common law proof problems. By providing for compensatory damages, punitive damages (capped at $3,000), attorney's fees, and litigation costs for patients who sustained "economic loss or personal injury," the legislature created a path for plaintiffs who could prove harm without having to prove a traditional negligence case.

The attorney's fees provision was the mechanism that made the statute practically enforceable for individual plaintiffs: without fee recovery, only large-damages cases could attract representation.

## What Changed

Before 1981: Patients whose medical information was misused were limited to common law tort remedies requiring proof of specific causation and quantifiable economic harm — a barrier that effectively immunized most violators.

After 1981: A statutory cause of action exists for patients who suffered economic loss or personal injury from unauthorized disclosure of their medical information. Attorney's fees are recoverable, making representation viable even for modest-damages cases. Punitive damages (capped) are available without the traditional showing of malice or oppression required for punitive awards under Civil Code § 3294.

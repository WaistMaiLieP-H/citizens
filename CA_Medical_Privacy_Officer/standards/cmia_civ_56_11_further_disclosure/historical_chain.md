# CMIA §56.11 — Historical Chain

## The Chain Breaks at Every Link

---

### The Wound

The original disclosure is never the only disclosure. When a provider shares medical information with an insurer, the insurer shares it with a claims processor, the claims processor shares it with an analytics firm, the analytics firm shares it with a database, and the database sells it to anyone willing to pay. Before the CMIA, there was no legal mechanism to stop this chain. A single authorized disclosure could multiply into hundreds of unauthorized downstream disclosures, and the patient would never know.

The wound was not just the first leak. It was the cascade. Medical information about a person's mental health diagnosis, substance abuse treatment, HIV status, or genetic condition could travel from a provider's office to an employer's desk to an insurance underwriter's algorithm — and none of the downstream recipients had any legal obligation to stop passing it along.

### The Promise

Section 56.11 promised: the chain stops at the first recipient unless the patient says otherwise. Anyone who receives medical information under an authorization or permitted disclosure cannot further disclose it without either (1) a new written authorization from the patient or (2) specific legal permission. The statute treats every link in the chain independently — each person or entity that passes along medical information without new authorization commits a separate violation.

This is the CMIA's anti-cascade provision. It recognizes that privacy is not a one-time gate but a continuous obligation. The first disclosure may be authorized; every subsequent disclosure must be independently justified.

### The Arc

Section 56.11 has not been amended since its 1981 enactment. The Legislature got it right the first time: a single, unambiguous sentence that applies universally. The simplicity is the strength — there are no exceptions to parse, no subcategories to navigate. If you received medical information and you passed it on without new authorization, you violated §56.11.

For the steward's case, §56.11 is the multiplier. If a conservatorship-related party received his medical information under §56.10(c)(12) and then shared it with Christina, or the mother, or any other person outside the conservatorship proceeding — each downstream handoff is a separate §56.11 violation carrying §56.35 damages. The conservatorship may have authorized the initial access. It did not authorize the cascade.

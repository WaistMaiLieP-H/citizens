# Heller v. Norcal Mutual Insurance Co., 8 Cal.4th 30 (1994)

## The Case

The California Supreme Court addressed the scope of medical privacy waiver in litigation. A physician sued their malpractice insurer for bad faith, and the insurer sought broad access to the physician's personal medical records, arguing that by bringing the lawsuit the physician had waived CMIA protections. The court had to determine how far a litigation-related waiver of medical privacy extends.

## What the Court Held

The California Supreme Court held:

1. **Medical privacy waiver in litigation is narrowly construed.** A party who places their medical condition at issue in litigation implicitly authorizes disclosure of medical information directly relevant to the claims or defenses raised. The waiver does not extend to all medical information in the party's history — only to information pertinent to the specific conditions at issue.

2. **The scope of waiver is determined by the claims raised, not by the opposing party's desire for information.** An opposing party cannot obtain carte blanche access to all medical records simply because some medical condition is at issue. The waiver tracks the relevance of the information to the actual claims and defenses.

3. **Section 56.11's further-disclosure prohibition applies even to information obtained within the waiver's scope.** The party who obtains medical records through the litigation waiver can use them for the litigation purpose but cannot further disclose them outside that purpose without new authorization.

## The Rule

**Medical privacy waiver in litigation is limited to information directly relevant to the claims or defenses raised. The waiver does not authorize access to all medical records and does not override §56.11's further-disclosure restriction.**

## Why This Case Matters

For the steward's case: even if the steward's medical condition was placed at issue in family court or conservatorship proceedings, the waiver of medical privacy was limited to information directly relevant to those specific proceedings. If any party obtained broader medical records than the litigation justified, or if records obtained for one proceeding were used in a different context, those disclosures exceeded the scope of the waiver and violated the CMIA.

# CMIA Authorization Requirements — Origin 1981

## The Wound

The most pervasive pre-CMIA privacy failure was not outright theft of medical records — it was nominal consent. Hospital admission forms, insurance applications, and employment packets routinely contained broad authorization language buried in paragraphs of fine print. Patients signed these forms in distress (at hospital admission), under economic pressure (insurance enrollment or employment application), or without reading them. The language typically authorized disclosure to "any person or organization" with a "legitimate interest" for any "purpose relating to" the patient's care, benefits, or employment — language capacious enough to justify almost any disclosure.

California courts found that these blanket authorizations were valid — the patient had signed, and courts were reluctant to second-guess consent documents executed by competent adults. The result was that hospitals could authorize insurers to sell claims data, physicians could share records with employers who sponsored the patient's insurance, and authorizations signed at one point in time were treated as perpetually valid.

## The Legislative Response

The CMIA's § 56.11 established formal validity requirements for authorizations that dismantled the blanket-consent model. The legislature identified specific exploitation techniques and created specific counter-requirements:

- *Buried in fine print*: 14-point type minimum
- *Signature doubling as general consent*: Separate signature requirement serving only the authorization purpose
- *Open-ended purpose*: Specific uses and limitations required
- *Indefinite duration*: One-year default expiration
- *Patient doesn't know what they signed*: Copy delivery requirement

Each requirement corresponds to a documented abuse the legislature was specifically addressing.

## What Changed

Before 1981: A single broadly-worded authorization, buried in an admission form or insurance application, could authorize indefinite disclosure of a patient's complete medical history to any party the signer could plausibly argue had a "legitimate interest."

After 1981: A valid authorization must be specific, prominent, time-limited, and documented with a patient copy. An authorization that fails any of the nine formal requirements is void. A disclosure made in reliance on a void authorization is an unauthorized disclosure — treated the same as a disclosure made without any authorization at all.

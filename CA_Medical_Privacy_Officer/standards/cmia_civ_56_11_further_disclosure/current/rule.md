# CMIA Authorization Requirements for Voluntary Medical Information Disclosure — Rule

**Authority:** Cal. Civ. Code § 56.11
**Jurisdiction:** California
**Effective:** 1981 (section governs authorization validity; most recently amended to add electronic signature provisions)

## The Rule

Any person or entity that wants to obtain medical information based on the patient's voluntary authorization — rather than a compelled or permitted exception under § 56.10(b) or (c) — must obtain a valid written authorization that meets specific formal requirements. An authorization that fails any of these requirements is legally invalid, and a disclosure made on the basis of an invalid authorization is a CMIA violation.

To be valid, an authorization must:
1. Be handwritten or in typeface no smaller than 14-point type
2. Be clearly separate from any other language on the same page and signed with a signature serving no purpose other than to authorize the release
3. Be signed and dated by the patient (or legal representative if the patient is a minor or lacks capacity)
4. State the specific uses and limitations on the types of medical information to be disclosed
5. State the name or functions of the provider authorized to disclose
6. State the name or functions of the persons or entities authorized to receive the information
7. State the specific uses and limitations on use by the recipients
8. State an expiration date or event — generally limited to one year, unless the patient requests longer or the authorization relates to a clinical trial or research study
9. Advise the person signing of the right to receive a copy of the authorization

The provider must give the patient a copy of the signed authorization and instructions for accessing additional copies.

## Key Elements

1. **Specificity is mandatory:** Blanket authorizations are invalid. An authorization must specify who can disclose, who can receive, what can be shared, for what purpose, and for how long.
2. **One-year default expiration:** Authorizations automatically expire after one year unless the patient affirmatively requests a longer period.
3. **14-point type minimum:** The authorization cannot be buried in fine print — it must be visually prominent.
4. **Separate signature:** The signature authorizing medical release cannot serve any other purpose — a signature on an admission form that also happens to authorize records release is not valid.
5. **Patient must receive copy:** The provider is obligated to give the patient their own copy of the signed authorization — creating a paper trail the patient can verify.

## What This Means in Practice

Pre-CMIA, patients frequently "authorized" disclosure of their medical records by signing boilerplate forms at hospital admission, employment applications, or insurance enrollment without understanding they were granting open-ended access to their medical history. Section 56.11 dismantled that model. Every voluntary disclosure of medical information that is not compelled under § 56.10(b) must be backed by a specific, legible, time-limited authorization the patient signed knowingly — and the patient gets a copy. An authorization that does not meet all nine formal requirements is void, and any disclosure made under it is unlawful.

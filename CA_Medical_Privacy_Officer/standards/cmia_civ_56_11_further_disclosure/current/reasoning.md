# CMIA Authorization Requirements for Voluntary Medical Information Disclosure — Reasoning

## Why This Law Exists

Section 56.11 exists because the concept of "consent" was being exploited before the CMIA. Hospitals, insurers, and employers routinely buried medical records authorization language in multi-page admission forms, employment applications, and benefits enrollment packets — presented to patients and employees at moments of vulnerability, stress, or economic pressure, often in fine print that was practically unreadable. Patients signed these forms without understanding they were granting broad, long-duration authority to disclose their complete medical history to parties they had not specifically identified for purposes they had not specifically agreed to.

The legislature responded by requiring that a valid authorization be formally distinct, visually prominent, specifically targeted, time-limited, and accompanied by a copy for the patient's records. Each formal requirement addresses a specific exploitation technique that was documented in the legislative record.

## Legislative Intent

The nine formal validity requirements in § 56.11(b) are not procedural formalities — they are substantive protections that define the difference between informed consent and coerced signature. The 14-point type minimum ensures the authorization is readable. The separate-signature requirement prevents authorization language from being buried in a contract. The specific-use requirement prevents broad blanket authorizations. The one-year default expiration prevents authorizations from running indefinitely on a single signature. The patient copy requirement creates a paper trail the patient can verify.

The 2022 and later amendments extending § 56.11 to cover digital health app authorizations reflect the legislature's continuing effort to keep the consent requirements aligned with how medical information is actually collected and distributed.

## Doctrinal Basis

Section 56.11 operationalizes the requirement of § 56.10(a) — that no disclosure occur without "authorization." What counts as a valid authorization is the question § 56.11 answers. An authorization that fails any of § 56.11's nine requirements is not a valid authorization within the meaning of § 56.10(a), meaning a disclosure made in reliance on an invalid authorization is the same as a disclosure made without any authorization at all — a CMIA violation enforceable under §§ 56.35 and 56.36.

# CMIA Civil Remedies and Criminal Penalties — Origin 1981

## The Wound

The CMIA's original 1981 enactment included civil remedies, but the enforcement architecture that emerged over the next three decades was shaped by a documented gap: the original remedies required proof of actual economic damages, which patients almost never could establish. A person whose psychiatric diagnosis was disclosed to their employer, or whose cancer history was sold to a marketer, suffered real harm — lost promotions, denied insurance, stigma — but tracing those outcomes to the specific unauthorized disclosure and quantifying them in dollar terms exceeded what most plaintiffs could prove in court.

By the 2000s, a commercial market had developed in which medical record data was being sold by data brokers, disclosed by employers to group health plans to identify high-risk employees, and shared across health care networks far beyond the treatment and payment contexts the CMIA contemplated. The original remedies were not deterring these commercial disclosure practices because the individual plaintiff's recoverable damages were too small to justify litigation.

## The Legislative Response

The 2013 amendments (Stats. 2013, Ch. 444, AB 658) substantially restructured the civil enforcement framework. The legislature:
- Added explicit nominal damages of $1,000 per violation — recoverable without proof of actual harm
- Added escalating civil penalty tiers based on culpability (negligent → knowing/willful → financial gain)
- Added the $250,000 cap plus disgorgement specifically for financial-gain violations, targeting commercial medical data exploitation
- Added the affirmative defense for HIPAA-compliant covered entities who met security requirements and had not engaged in medical identity theft

The financial gain track ($250,000 + disgorgement) was a direct response to documented commercial trafficking in patient records — cases where the harm to individual patients was diffuse and unprovable while the financial benefit to the violator was substantial and traceable.

## What Changed

Before 2013: Civil enforcement required proof of actual economic loss or personal injury. Commercial medical data exploitation produced minimal individual liability because individual harm was hard to prove.

After 2013: The $1,000 nominal damages floor makes enforcement viable for any documented unauthorized disclosure, even where economic harm is diffuse. The $250,000 + disgorgement track creates consequences severe enough to deter commercial exploitation. The criminal misdemeanor track creates personal exposure for individuals who directed or performed intentional violations.

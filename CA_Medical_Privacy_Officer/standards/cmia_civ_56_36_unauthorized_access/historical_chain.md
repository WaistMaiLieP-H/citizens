# Historical Chain — California Civil Code § 56.36 CMIA Unauthorized Access

## The wound

The 1981 CMIA created civil damages for unauthorized disclosure — but civil damages alone do not deter every category of bad actor. An insurance company that saves millions by obtaining medical information under false pretenses can absorb $1,000 per-violation civil penalties as a cost of doing business. An employer who wants to screen out workers with medical conditions has an economic incentive to obtain records illegally even if the penalty is civil.

The deeper wound § 56.36 addresses: there are actors who seek medical information not through carelessness but through fraud and deliberate misrepresentation. Pre-1981, there was no California statute specifically criminalizing the obtaining of medical records under false pretenses. The common law fraud framework existed, but medical records were often obtained through industry channels, institutional relationships, or social engineering — conduct that was difficult to characterize as traditional fraud.

The specific harm pattern that drove § 56.36's enactment: private investigators, employers, and insurance companies obtaining medical records by claiming false authorizations, misrepresenting their identity, or claiming a treatment purpose for records being used for adverse decisions. Workers applying for disability or compensation benefits were particularly vulnerable — their medical records could be obtained behind their backs and used to deny benefits.

## The legislative response

The CMIA's 1981 enactment included a criminal penalty track alongside the civil damages track from the beginning. The legislature recognized that civil damages are adequate deterrence for careless actors but inadequate for calculating bad actors who rationally weigh penalties against the value of unauthorized access.

The false-pretenses provision — §56.36(b)(1) — was designed specifically to criminalize the obtaining of records through identity fraud or false authorization. A person who presents themselves as a patient's authorized representative when they are not, or who forges an authorization, is committing a crime — not just a civil wrong.

The monetary-gain provisions — §56.36(b)(2) and (b)(3) — targeted the commercial trafficking in medical records. By the early 1980s, the market for medical information as an underwriting and employment screening tool was well established. These provisions imposed criminal liability on the deliberate use of medical records as a commodity outside the care context.

## 2013 amendment

Stats. 2013, Ch. 444 amended § 56.36 alongside § 56.35, strengthening the civil remedies and clarifying the triple-damages provision for business violations. The 2013 amendment is the current governing version.

## The current state

Section 56.36 operates on two tracks simultaneously: civil (§56.35 incorporated by reference) and criminal (county jail + fine for knowing-and-willful violations). The criminal track requires knowing and willful conduct — not mere negligence. The civil track applies to negligent or intentional conduct.

The triple-damages provision for intentional business violations — up to 3x actual damages or $500,000 — creates substantial exposure for commercial actors who deliberately violated the CMIA.

## Steward relevance

Any access to the steward's medical records through false pretenses — whether by an impersonator, a fraudulent authorization, or a claimed exception that did not actually apply — is a §56.36(b)(1) criminal violation. The criminal character of the conduct, in addition to civil liability, is relevant to the § 1983 federal claim (state actors participating in or facilitating criminal medical record access under color of law) and to the overall conspiracy theory tying together the identity replacement scheme.

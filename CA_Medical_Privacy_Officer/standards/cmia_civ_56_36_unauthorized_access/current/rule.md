# CMIA Civil Remedies and Criminal Penalties — Rule

**Authority:** Cal. Civ. Code § 56.36
**Jurisdiction:** California
**Effective:** 1981 (most recent amendment: Stats. 2013, Ch. 444, Sec. 5, effective January 1, 2014)

## The Rule

A violation of the CMIA that causes economic loss or personal injury to a patient is a criminal misdemeanor. Separate from criminal prosecution, individuals who suffered from unauthorized disclosure of their medical information have a civil right of action with four tracks of relief based on how the violation occurred:

- **Nominal damages:** $1,000 per violation — available without any proof of actual harm. Any unauthorized disclosure, however minor, generates this floor.
- **Negligent violation civil penalty:** Up to $2,500 per violation, assessed by the State Department of Public Health, licensing agency, or court.
- **Knowing and willful violation:** Up to $25,000 per violation for non-licensed persons and entities; tiered penalties for licensed health care professionals ($2,500 first violation, $10,000 second, $25,000 third and beyond).
- **Monetary gain track:** Up to $250,000 per violation plus disgorgement of all proceeds where medical information was obtained or used for financial gain.

An affirmative defense is available to covered entities and business associates under HIPAA who (1) complied with security and notification requirements, (2) took appropriate preventive actions, and (3) the disclosure was not an incident of medical identity theft.

## Key Elements

1. **No actual damages required for nominal claim:** A patient can recover $1,000 per violation even without proving any economic loss or personal injury — the invasion of privacy is the harm.
2. **Escalating penalties by culpability:** Negligent → knowing/willful → financial gain; each level carries higher penalties.
3. **Criminal misdemeanor track:** Violations causing harm are punishable as misdemeanors — creating criminal exposure for individuals who directed or performed the unauthorized disclosure.
4. **Disgorgement for financial gain:** Profits from selling or misusing medical information must be surrendered on top of the civil penalty.
5. **Affirmative defense:** HIPAA-compliant covered entities who meet all security requirements and whose disclosure was not medical identity theft may avoid nominal damages but still owe actual damages and attorney's fees.

## What This Means in Practice

The $1,000 nominal damages floor eliminates the traditional actual-harm barrier that made privacy litigation impractical. A patient who discovers that their medical records were accessed or disclosed without authorization can recover without proving downstream harm — each unauthorized access is its own violation. Where the disclosure was used for financial gain (commercial data sale, insurance underwriting, identity exploitation), the disgorgement and $250,000 cap create significant monetary exposure per incident.

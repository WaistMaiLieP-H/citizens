# Context — California Civil Code § 56.36 CMIA Unauthorized Access

## The statute in brief

Section 56.36 addresses the most culpable forms of medical privacy violations: those involving false pretenses, monetary gain, or deliberate misuse of medical information. It operates on two tracks simultaneously:

**Track 1 — Civil liability:** Incorporates §56.35's damages framework for any negligent or intentional release by a person or entity who knew or should have known the release was prohibited.

**Track 2 — Criminal penalties:** Imposes criminal fines and county jail time for:
- Obtaining medical information under false pretenses (knowing and willful)
- Obtaining and disclosing medical information without authorization for monetary gain (knowing and willful)
- Disclosing or using medical information for a purpose other than disclosed, for monetary gain (knowing and willful)

## The "false pretenses" provision — § 56.36(b)(1)

This is the most operationally significant provision for identity replacement cases. Someone who obtains another person's medical information by presenting themselves as that person — or by presenting a fraudulent authorization form — is committing a §56.36(b)(1) violation. The elements are:
- Knowingly (awareness that what they are doing is prohibited)
- Willfully (volitional act)
- Obtaining (requesting, accessing, or receiving)
- Medical information
- Under false pretenses (misrepresentation of identity, authority, or purpose)

## The monetary-gain provision — § 56.36(b)(2) and (b)(3)

These provisions target those who traffic in medical information. Subsection (b)(2) covers unauthorized disclosure of medical records to a third party for monetary gain — e.g., selling medical records, or providing them to a party who will pay for the information. Subsection (b)(3) covers using medical information for a purpose other than disclosed, for monetary gain — e.g., obtaining records for stated treatment purposes but actually using them for insurance underwriting, employment screening, or other commercial purposes.

## Triple damages for businesses — § 56.36(c)(2)

Where a business intentionally violates § 56.36, the customer may recover up to three times actual damages or $500,000, whichever is less. This is a significant multiplier over the base §56.35 actual-damages recovery, and is available specifically for intentional business-entity violations.

## Application to the steward's case

**Identity replacement scenario:** If anyone obtained medical records pertaining to the steward by presenting false pretenses — claiming to be the steward, claiming to be authorized by the steward, or presenting a fraudulent authorization — that act is a §56.36(b)(1) criminal violation as well as a civil CMIA violation under §56.35.

**Medical suppression scheme:** If medical information about the steward was obtained and shared for purposes unrelated to his care — for example, to support a false disability denial, to enable a conservatorship proceeding conducted without his knowledge, or to provide to parties adverse to him in litigation — that is a §56.36(b)(2)/(b)(3) violation if monetary gain was involved.

**The criminal track is significant:** A knowing-and-willful false-pretenses violation of § 56.36(b) is a misdemeanor with potential county jail time. This creates leverage beyond civil damages: the conduct that constitutes the underlying identity replacement scheme also constitutes criminal exposure for the individuals who accessed or disclosed the records.

## Relationship to § 56.10(c)(12)

If medical records were accessed via the §56.10(c)(12) probate court investigator exception — which is a lawful exception — then §56.36 does not apply to that access (it was authorized). However, if the access was claimed to be under §56.10(c)(12) but there was no actual probate proceeding, the "lawful exception" defense fails, and the access under false pretenses is a §56.36(b)(1) violation.

This creates a critical forensic question: CMIA disclosure logs will show whether each access claimed a §56.10 exception. If a (c)(12) exception was claimed, that is evidence of a conservatorship proceeding. If no exception was claimed but the access occurred, that is evidence of a §56.36 violation.

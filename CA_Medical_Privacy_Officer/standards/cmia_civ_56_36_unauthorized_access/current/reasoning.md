# CMIA Civil Remedies and Criminal Penalties — Reasoning

## Why This Law Exists

A privacy law without an enforcement mechanism is a policy statement, not a rule. Section 56.36 exists because the legislature recognized that patients whose medical information was disclosed without authorization would, under traditional tort law, face enormous difficulty proving recoverable damages. Physical harm from a privacy violation is real but often intangible — embarrassment, loss of employment opportunity, damaged relationships — and proving causation and quantum in a traditional negligence action creates barriers that effectively immunize most violators.

Section 56.36's nominal damages provision — $1,000 per violation without proof of actual damages — directly addresses this barrier. It converts the privacy violation itself into compensable harm, regardless of whether the patient can trace specific economic loss back to the disclosure.

## Legislative Intent

The 2013 amendment (Stats. 2013, Ch. 444, AB 658) significantly strengthened the civil penalty framework in response to documented commercial trafficking in medical records — cases where patient data was being sold to pharmaceutical marketers, credit agencies, and data brokers. The amendment added the $250,000 penalty plus disgorgement for financial gain violations, creating consequences severe enough to deter commercial exploitation.

The affirmative defense added in 2013 for HIPAA-compliant covered entities reflects a policy judgment that entities who implement reasonable security measures, properly notify affected patients, and act in good faith deserve some protection from nominal damages — while remaining fully liable for actual harm.

## Doctrinal Basis

Section 56.36 operates on two tracks simultaneously. The criminal misdemeanor track (§ 56.36(a)) treats sufficiently culpable privacy violations as public wrongs deserving of prosecution — not just private torts. The civil track (§ 56.36(b)-(f)) creates a private cause of action with a penalty structure designed to make enforcement economically viable for individual plaintiffs. The interaction between § 56.35 (compensatory and punitive damages for harm) and § 56.36 (nominal damages, civil penalties, and criminal penalties for violations) gives patients two parallel remedies — one requiring proof of harm, one not.

# CMIA Disclosure Prohibitions — Origin 1981

## The Wound

In the decade before 1981, California patients had no statutory right to control who received their medical information. Insurers and employers had developed informal networks with hospitals and physicians — medical directors at insurance companies would call treating physicians directly to discuss claims, and employers would request full medical histories from applicants' doctors as a condition of hiring. Patients often did not know this was happening.

The most documented abuse pattern involved employers: a company would hire a group health insurer, which would then provide the employer with claims data identifying which employees had expensive or chronic conditions — cancer diagnoses, psychiatric treatment, substance abuse. Employers used this information to terminate or demote employees before conditions became costly to the group plan. Employees learned their termination was medically driven only if they found out about the disclosure at all.

A second pattern involved insurance underwriting: life and health insurers routinely obtained attending physician statements that far exceeded what was necessary for underwriting purposes — getting complete medical histories where the underwriting question required only a narrow inquiry — and then maintaining those records for use in future claims denials or policy rescissions.

There was no federal remedy. The Privacy Act (1974) covered only federal agencies. California tort law required actual damages — and proving causation between a medical information disclosure and an employment or insurance outcome was extremely difficult for individual plaintiffs.

## The Legislative Response

The California Legislature enacted the Confidentiality of Medical Information Act as Stats. 1981, Ch. 782 — authored by Assemblymember Bruce Keene. The CMIA established the default rule that no disclosure could occur without patient authorization, enumerated the specific exceptions where state or health system interests override individual privacy, and created a civil enforcement mechanism.

The original § 56.10 established the prohibition-plus-exceptions structure that remains the statute's architecture today. The legislature chose to enumerate every exception rather than use a general-purpose standard like "reasonable" or "necessary" — a deliberate choice to prevent the exceptions from swallowing the rule.

## What Changed

Before 1981: Providers could disclose patient medical information to virtually any party with a plausible interest, with no statutory requirement of patient consent and no civil remedy for patients whose information was misused.

After 1981: Every disclosure without authorization must fit within one of nine compelled or 24 permitted categories. Disclosures outside the exceptions — regardless of the disclosing entity's purpose or the recipient's credentials — are CMIA violations subject to civil remedies. The statute applies to all providers, plans, pharmaceutical companies, and contractors, not just hospitals and physicians.

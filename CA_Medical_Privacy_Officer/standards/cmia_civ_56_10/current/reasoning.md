# CMIA Disclosure Prohibitions and Permitted Uses — Reasoning

## Why This Law Exists

California enacted the Confidentiality of Medical Information Act in 1981 (Stats. 1981, Ch. 782) because neither federal law nor California common law adequately protected patients from having their medical information used against them without their knowledge. Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act privacy rules did not exist until 2003. Before the CMIA, insurers, employers, and researchers could access patient records through informal arrangements with hospitals and physicians, and patients had no effective means to challenge those disclosures.

The legislature identified a specific problem: medical information was flowing from providers to employers and insurers for purposes that had nothing to do with the patient's care — hiring decisions, insurance underwriting, research monetization — without the patient's knowledge or meaningful consent.

## Legislative Intent

The CMIA was designed to place the patient at the center of any decision to disclose their own medical information. The default rule in § 56.10(a) — no disclosure without authorization — inverts the pre-1981 presumption that providers could share patient information as they saw fit. Every exception to the default rule is enumerated; if a proposed disclosure does not fit a listed exception, authorization is required.

The 24 permitted categories in § 56.10(c) reflect the legislature's judgment about which disclosures serve legitimate health care purposes that patients would reasonably expect to occur without being asked each time — treatment coordination, billing, peer review, public health. The nine compelled categories in § 56.10(b) reflect the legislature's judgment about when the state's legitimate interests override individual privacy — courts, law enforcement with warrants, coroners.

## Doctrinal Basis

Section 56.10 is grounded in California's constitutional right to privacy, established by Proposition 11 (1972), codified at California Constitution Article I, § 1. The constitutional right is self-executing — it creates an affirmative obligation on both government and private actors to protect privacy. The CMIA implements this constitutional guarantee for medical information specifically, providing concrete rules, enumerated exceptions, and civil remedies.

The CMIA is not preempted by HIPAA. Federal law expressly provides that state laws more protective of privacy than HIPAA are not preempted — the CMIA's broader coverage (pharmaceutical companies, contractors, digital health apps) and its private right of action are both lawful exercises of California's authority to provide stronger protection than the federal floor.

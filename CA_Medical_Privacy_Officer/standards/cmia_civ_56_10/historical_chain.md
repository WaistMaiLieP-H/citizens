# CMIA §56.10 — Historical Chain

## Who Gets to See Your Medical Records

---

### I. The Wound

Before the CMIA (enacted 1981), California had no comprehensive medical privacy statute. Medical records were shared between providers, insurers, employers, and government agencies with minimal restriction. A person's most intimate health information — diagnoses, treatments, mental health records, substance abuse history — could be disclosed without the patient's knowledge or consent.

The wound was weaponization: medical information used to deny insurance, terminate employment, manipulate custody proceedings, or control individuals through conservatorships. Medical records are not neutral data — they are a map of vulnerability, and anyone with access to that map has power over the person.

### II. The Promise (1981)

The Confidentiality of Medical Information Act promised: **Your medical information belongs to you. Providers, insurers, and others who hold it cannot disclose it without your authorization, except in specifically enumerated circumstances.**

### III. The Exceptions (§56.10)

Section 56.10 lists over 30 exceptions to the confidentiality requirement — situations where medical information can be disclosed without the patient's consent. The most relevant to the steward's case:

- **(c)(1)**: Providers furnishing health care to the patient (treatment continuity)
- **(c)(2)**: Insurers and payers (payment and coverage)
- **(c)(4)**: Court order or subpoena (judicial compulsion)
- **(c)(8)**: Government agencies administering health care programs
- **(c)(12)**: **Court investigators** conducting conservatorship investigations under Probate Code §1826 and §1851 — this is the conservatorship bridge
- **(c)(14)**: Medical information needed for workers' compensation claims
- **(c)(19)**: Coroners and medical examiners
- **(c)(22)**: Organ procurement organizations

### IV. The Conservatorship Bridge

Section 56.10(c)(12) is the critical link between the CMIA and the conservatorship investigation. It authorizes court investigators to access medical records when investigating conservatorship petitions or conducting periodic reviews. This means:

1. If a conservatorship was established, someone authorized under (c)(12) could have accessed ALL of the steward's medical records
2. The scope of authorized access is limited to the conservatorship investigation — any further disclosure or use is unauthorized
3. Medical providers who released records under (c)(12) should have documentation of the request and the legal authority cited

### V. For the Steward's Case

The medical fraud timeline documents: spine surgery at Providence St. Joseph (Dr. Wiseman), Blue Shield authorizations naming a non-existent doctor (Dilworth), SIRVA from a COVID vaccine at Walgreens Brentwood, SSA/DDS contacts that may never have occurred. Each of these events involved medical information being disclosed to someone. The CMIA audit asks:

- Who accessed the steward's medical records?
- Under which §56.10 exception?
- Was the disclosure authorized?
- Was the steward's consent obtained? If not, which exception applied?
- If a conservatorship existed, were records accessed under (c)(12)? By whom?

Every unauthorized disclosure is a CMIA violation carrying damages under §56.35.

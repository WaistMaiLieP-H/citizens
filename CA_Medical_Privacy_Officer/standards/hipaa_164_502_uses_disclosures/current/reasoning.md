# HIPAA General Rule — Permitted Uses and Disclosures of PHI — Reasoning

## Why This Law Exists

Congress enacted the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 (Pub. L. 104-191) in part because the United States had no comprehensive federal framework governing what health care providers and insurers could do with patient health information. In the years before HIPAA, documented cases existed of insurers providing patient data to employers for hiring decisions, hospitals selling patient lists to pharmaceutical marketers, and financial institutions obtaining health information to inform credit decisions — all without patient knowledge or consent.

The 1996 statute directed HHS to promulgate privacy rules. The Privacy Rule (45 C.F.R. Parts 160 and 164) took effect in 2003. Section 164.502 is the foundational provision — the general rule from which all permitted uses and disclosures flow.

## Legislative Intent

Congress designed HIPAA's privacy framework as a federal floor, not a ceiling. States are explicitly permitted to enact stronger protections (45 C.F.R. § 160.203(b)), and California's CMIA is one of the strongest state medical privacy laws in the nation. HIPAA's contribution is uniformity — every covered entity in every state must comply with § 164.502's baseline requirements, providing a minimum level of protection even in states with weaker privacy laws.

The HITECH Act (2009, Pub. L. 111-5) substantially strengthened HIPAA enforcement by: extending business associate liability, increasing civil monetary penalties, requiring breach notification, and authorizing state attorneys general to bring federal enforcement actions. HITECH responded to documented breaches affecting millions of patients and inadequate enforcement of the original HIPAA rules.

## Doctrinal Basis

Section 164.502 is promulgated under HIPAA's statutory authority at 42 U.S.C. § 1320d-2. The minimum necessary standard embedded in § 164.502(b) is a proportionality principle drawn from the Fourth Amendment's particularity requirement — disclosure must be limited to what the specific purpose actually requires, not maximized by the disclosing entity. In California, § 164.502 provides the federal compliance framework while CMIA § 56.10 provides the state enforcement mechanism with private right of action — the two statutes are complementary, not duplicative.

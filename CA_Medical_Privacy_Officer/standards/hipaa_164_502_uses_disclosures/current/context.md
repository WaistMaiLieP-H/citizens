# Context — 45 CFR § 164.502 HIPAA Uses and Disclosures

## The statute in brief

Section 164.502 is the operational core of the HIPAA Privacy Rule. It establishes the default prohibition and the framework of exceptions:

**The default rule:** A covered entity or business associate may NOT use or disclose protected health information (PHI) except as permitted or required by the Privacy Rule.

**Permitted uses and disclosures:** Six categories, the most important being:
1. To the individual (the patient always has access rights)
2. For treatment, payment, or health care operations (TPO — the primary clinical exception)
3. With a valid authorization from the patient

**The minimum necessary standard:** Even when a use or disclosure is permitted, the covered entity must disclose only the minimum necessary PHI to accomplish the stated purpose. This applies to all non-TPO, non-authorization disclosures.

## Who is bound

"Covered entity" under HIPAA includes health care providers that transmit health information electronically, health plans, and health care clearinghouses. "Business associate" includes third parties that handle PHI on behalf of a covered entity (billing companies, data processors, EHR vendors).

## What counts as PHI

Protected Health Information is any individually identifiable health information held or transmitted by a covered entity or business associate. This includes:
- Medical records, lab results, diagnoses
- Insurance information connected to health care
- Any information that identifies or could identify the patient AND relates to their health condition, care, or payment

## The minimum necessary standard — operational significance

The minimum necessary standard means that even a permitted disclosure must be scoped to what is actually necessary. A request for medical records "for payment purposes" does not authorize disclosure of the patient's complete psychiatric history. A request for records "for treatment" does not authorize disclosure to parties not involved in the treatment.

The minimum necessary standard is the HIPAA-level equivalent of California CMIA's necessity limitation in §56.10(c)(2) (insurer/payor disclosures limited to what is necessary for payment).

## Authorization requirements

A valid HIPAA authorization under §164.508 must:
- Be signed by the patient (or personal representative)
- Identify the specific information to be used or disclosed
- Identify the parties authorized to disclose and receive
- Include an expiration date or event
- State the purpose of the disclosure
- Include a statement that the patient may revoke the authorization

A form that does not meet these requirements is not a valid authorization, and a disclosure made under it is unauthorized.

## Enforcement

HIPAA is enforced by the HHS Office for Civil Rights (OCR). Violations are tiered by culpability:
- Tier 1: Did not know (unknowing violation): $100–$50,000 per violation
- Tier 2: Reasonable cause: $1,000–$50,000 per violation  
- Tier 3: Willful neglect, corrected: $10,000–$50,000 per violation
- Tier 4: Willful neglect, not corrected: $50,000 per violation, up to $1.9M annual cap

HIPAA does not create a private right of action — enforcement is through OCR complaints. California's CMIA provides the private right of action for California residents.

## HIPAA vs. CMIA — which governs

Both may be violated simultaneously by a single unauthorized disclosure. CMIA is generally stricter than HIPAA, and in California, both standards apply to covered entities/providers. Where CMIA provides greater protection, CMIA governs. Where HIPAA establishes requirements not addressed by CMIA, HIPAA applies.

The practical framework for a California medical privacy case:
1. CMIA provides the private right of action (§§56.35/56.36)
2. HIPAA provides the federal regulatory floor and OCR enforcement track
3. Both are argued in parallel — CMIA for civil damages, HIPAA for regulatory/OCR pressure

## Application to the steward's case

Any disclosure of the steward's medical records that did not have either (a) a valid HIPAA authorization signed by him or (b) a recognized HIPAA exception (TPO, required by law, public health) is a §164.502 violation. The minimum necessary standard means that even disclosures to payors, insurers, or treatment facilities must be scoped to the minimum necessary — broad disclosure of complete records to parties not involved in his care is a violation even if some disclosure was permitted.

Where HIPAA violations occurred after 2009 (HITECH Act took effect), OCR investigations can result in civil monetary penalties that create regulatory pressure on providers who participated in the unauthorized disclosure scheme.

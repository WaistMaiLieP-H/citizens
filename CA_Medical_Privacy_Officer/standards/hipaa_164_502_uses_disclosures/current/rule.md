# HIPAA General Rule — Permitted Uses and Disclosures of PHI — Rule

**Authority:** 45 C.F.R. § 164.502
**Jurisdiction:** Federal (applies to covered entities and business associates nationwide; CMIA applies additionally in California and is more protective)
**Effective:** April 14, 2003 (compliance date); major strengthening by HITECH Act 2009

## The Rule

A covered entity — any health care provider that transmits health information electronically, any health plan, and any health care clearinghouse — may only use or disclose Protected Health Information (PHI) in one of three ways:

1. **With the individual's written authorization** (45 C.F.R. § 164.508) — the default pathway
2. **Without authorization, for Treatment, Payment, and Operations (TPO)** — coordinating care, processing claims, and running the covered entity's health care business
3. **Without authorization, for specific public interest purposes** enumerated in § 164.512 — law enforcement, public health, judicial proceedings, research with IRB oversight, national security, workers' compensation, and others — each with specific conditions and a minimum necessary standard

Business associates of covered entities are directly bound by HIPAA's use and disclosure restrictions through the HITECH Act (2009) — a contractor that handles PHI is not exempt simply because it is not itself a covered entity.

The minimum necessary standard applies to all non-TPO disclosures: covered entities must take reasonable steps to disclose only the minimum PHI necessary to accomplish the purpose of the disclosure.

## Key Elements

1. **Default prohibition:** PHI may not be used or disclosed without authorization — the burden is on the covered entity to identify a valid pathway.
2. **TPO exception is broad but not unlimited:** Treatment, payment, and operations encompass most routine health care activities, but must still be for the covered entity's own operations or the care of that patient.
3. **Minimum necessary standard:** Even for permitted disclosures, covered entities must limit disclosure to what is minimally required to accomplish the purpose.
4. **Business associate liability:** Business associates that violate the privacy rule are directly subject to civil monetary penalties under HITECH — not just the covered entity they contract with.
5. **No private right of action under HIPAA:** HIPAA is enforced by HHS Office for Civil Rights, not by private lawsuits — unlike California's CMIA, which provides a private right of action. In California, a HIPAA violation is typically also a CMIA violation, and CMIA's private cause of action is the litigation vehicle.

## What This Means in Practice

In California, HIPAA sets the federal floor and CMIA provides stronger protection. When a provider violates HIPAA's § 164.502 by disclosing PHI without authorization and outside any listed exception, that violation is simultaneously a CMIA § 56.10 violation — and the patient's remedy is through CMIA's civil action, while the regulatory enforcement is through HHS OCR. An OCR complaint forces providers to produce their disclosure logs, authorization records, and business associate agreements — generating discovery that supports the parallel CMIA civil claim.

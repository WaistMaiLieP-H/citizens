# HIPAA General Rule — Uses and Disclosures of PHI — Origin 1996

## The Wound

By the mid-1990s, the United States health care system had undergone two decades of consolidation — the rise of managed care organizations, integrated delivery networks, and electronic claims processing — that transformed medical information from isolated paper records in individual physician offices into data flowing through interconnected systems of payers, employers, and financial institutions.

The documented harms that drove HIPAA included:

*Employer access through insurance:* Employers who self-insured their health plans had access to claims data that identified employees with expensive chronic conditions — HIV, cancer, psychiatric conditions. Documented cases existed of employers terminating or forcing out employees after their claims data revealed conditions the employers found unacceptable. The legal mechanism was the employer's role as plan sponsor: the same entity that made employment decisions was receiving individual-level medical claims data.

*Financial industry access:* Banking institutions and credit agencies had begun acquiring medical information — including diagnosis codes from claims data — to inform credit decisions. A patient's mental health diagnosis or cancer treatment, visible in insurance claims data, could affect their creditworthiness without their knowledge.

*No federal remedy:* By 1996, no comprehensive federal statute governed the flow of medical information in the private health care system. ERISA preempted many state law claims by employer-sponsored plan beneficiaries. State medical privacy laws like California's CMIA covered only a subset of entities and had no interstate enforcement mechanism.

## The Legislative Response

Congress enacted HIPAA (Pub. L. 104-191) in 1996 and directed HHS to promulgate a Privacy Rule establishing national standards. The Privacy Rule was finalized in December 2000 and took effect in 2001, with a 2003 compliance deadline. Section 164.502 is the foundational provision — the general rule establishing what covered entities may do with PHI.

The HITECH Act (2009, Pub. L. 111-5) substantially strengthened enforcement after a decade of documented non-compliance: (1) extended direct liability to business associates; (2) increased civil monetary penalties to current levels; (3) required breach notification; (4) authorized state attorneys general to enforce HIPAA in federal court.

## What Changed

Before 2003: No comprehensive federal framework governed medical information privacy in the private health care sector. State laws varied dramatically. Employer-sponsored plan beneficiaries had limited state law remedies due to ERISA preemption.

After 2003: Every covered entity in every state must comply with § 164.502's minimum requirements. Business associates — contractors handling PHI — are directly bound since 2009. OCR enforcement creates a regulatory accountability mechanism even where private suit is unavailable. In California, HIPAA + CMIA creates a dual-track accountability framework: HHS OCR enforces the federal floor; CMIA private actions enforce the stronger California standard.

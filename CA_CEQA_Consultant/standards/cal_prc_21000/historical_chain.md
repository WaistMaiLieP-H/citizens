# Historical Chain — California Environmental Quality Act (CEQA)
## Cal. Public Resources Code § 21000 et seq. — Stats. 1970, Ch. 1433

---

## The wound

Through the 1950s and 1960s, California approved highways, subdivisions, dams, industrial facilities, and public works projects without any systematic requirement to identify, disclose, or weigh environmental consequences. Project sponsors — whether government agencies or private developers seeking government approvals — had no legal obligation to study what their projects would do to air quality, water supply, wildlife habitat, or neighboring communities before permits were issued and ground was broken.

The harm was cumulative and, by the late 1960s, visible. Los Angeles' air was thick with smog. The Santa Barbara oil spill of January 1969 coated 35 miles of coastline after a Union Oil platform blowout — and Union Oil's drilling permits had been issued without any environmental impact review. San Francisco Bay had been filled by over a third of its original area for development. Highway expansion programs were cutting through urban neighborhoods. Lake Tahoe's clarity was declining. The damage was not hidden — it was visible from the road and from the shore — but there was no legal mechanism that required a government agency to ask, before approving a project, what the project would do to the environment, or to tell the public what it found.

---

## The legislative response

Congress enacted the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) on January 1, 1970. California's Governor Ronald Reagan signed CEQA on the same day — January 1, 1970 — establishing California's own parallel environmental review framework under Stats. 1970, Ch. 1433.

CEQA's legislative findings, codified at Public Resources Code § 21000, declared that environmental quality was a matter of statewide concern, that the capacity of the environment is limited, that critical thresholds must be identified before they are reached, and that every citizen has a responsibility to contribute to environmental preservation. The Legislature directed that all state agencies regulating activities affecting environmental quality give major consideration to preventing environmental damage.

The operative mechanism CEQA created was environmental review as a condition of government approval. Before a public agency could approve a project with potentially significant environmental effects, it had to prepare an Environmental Impact Report (EIR) disclosing those effects, identifying mitigation measures, and considering alternatives. The public had the right to participate. Agencies had to respond.

---

## The current state

CEQA has become one of the most consequential environmental review statutes in the United States. The operative provisions in Public Resources Code §§ 21002–21177 define which projects require review, what an EIR must contain, what triggers exemptions, and what legal standards govern judicial review of agency findings.

The statute has been substantially amended since 1970 — the most recent amendment to § 21000 itself was Stats. 1979, Ch. 947 — and the Guidelines implementing CEQA (14 Cal. Code of Regs. § 15000 et seq.) have been revised multiple times. CEQA litigation is a significant branch of California administrative and environmental law.

For the CA CEQA Consultant, the standard establishes the doctrinal premise that government agencies must ask and answer the environmental question before acting — not after. The § 21000 findings are the textual foundation for every EIR requirement, every CEQA exemption determination, and every judicial challenge to a CEQA inadequacy.

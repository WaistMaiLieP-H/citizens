# Origin Context — § 1132 Civil Enforcement (1974)

## The wound § 1132 answered

The Studebaker wound (1963): Studebaker Corporation closed its South Bend plant. Its pension plan was underfunded. Of the workers affected, approximately 4,000 who had not yet reached retirement age received only 15 cents on the dollar of their accrued benefits. About 2,900 workers under age 40 received nothing. They had worked for decades on the explicit understanding that the pension was a deferred wage — compensation earned but not yet paid.

Studebaker was not unusual. It was the exposure. Senate subcommittee investigations in the late 1960s documented:
- Plans could operate for decades without minimum funding requirements
- Workers could work 30+ years and still fail to vest if plan rules set long vesting clocks
- No federal court had jurisdiction to enforce a worker's pension claim — state law was preempted by collective bargaining but offered no substitute
- Plan administrators could self-deal, invest in employer stock, and pay kickbacks without legal consequence
- Workers had no right to see plan documents

## What Congress decided § 1132 needed to do

Section 502 (§ 1132) was the enforcement linchpin. Without enforcement, every other ERISA protection was aspirational. Congress designed § 1132 with three structural choices:

**Choice 1 — Federal court, exclusive jurisdiction, no amount threshold.** Congress knew state court enforcement would be patchwork and employer-favorable. Federal district courts got exclusive jurisdiction over most ERISA claims, without regard to amount in controversy or diversity of citizenship. A participant with a $5,000 pension claim has the same federal courthouse access as one with a $5 million claim.

**Choice 2 — Multiple cause-of-action structure.** § 1132(a)(1)(B) = get the benefits you are owed. § 1132(a)(2) = remedy fiduciary breach. § 1132(a)(3) = equitable relief for any ERISA violation. Each track served a different failure mode. Together they were designed to leave no gap.

**Choice 3 — Administrative penalty for document non-production.** § 1132(c)(1) is unusual: a personal liability on the plan administrator for failure to produce plan documents. Congress recognized that without documents, participants couldn't know what they were owed. The penalty was designed to be automatic and accruing — not requiring proof of harm.

## What § 1132 did NOT include at origin

- No punitive damages (ERISA remedies were intended to make the plan whole, not punish)
- No compensatory damages beyond the benefit owed (courts later wrestled with whether make-whole relief under § 1132(a)(3) could function as damages — CIGNA v. Amara resolved this in 2011)
- No private right of action for plan document violations independent of benefit denial (the document-production penalty is the workaround)

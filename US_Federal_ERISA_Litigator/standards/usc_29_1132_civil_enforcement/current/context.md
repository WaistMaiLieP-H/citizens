# Context — 29 U.S.C. § 1132 ERISA Civil Enforcement

## The wound and the promise

**The wound:** Before ERISA, private pension plans were operated without federal oversight. Plan administrators could deny benefits arbitrarily, change plan terms to eliminate accrued benefits, and operate without accountability. The quintessential wound: a worker who spent decades earning a pension would reach retirement age only to discover the plan was terminated, the administrator had changed the terms, or the benefits were simply denied without explanation.

**The promise:** ERISA § 502 (29 U.S.C. § 1132) promises that a pension plan participant has an enforceable federal right to the benefits they earned. The participant can sue in federal court to recover benefits due, enforce plan terms, and obtain equitable relief for violations. Federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction — no state court runaround.

## The four causes of action most relevant to Case #37

### § 1132(a)(1)(B) — Recovery of benefits due

The fundamental ERISA claim: the plan owes me benefits and refuses to pay (or has frozen my account). This is the primary claim for the steward's frozen pension.

**Elements:**
1. The claimant is a plan participant (UA Local 342 pension plan member)
2. Benefits are due under the terms of the plan (vested rights based on years of service)
3. The plan administrator has refused to pay or has improperly frozen/reduced the benefits

**Standard of review:**
- If the plan document gives the administrator discretionary authority → abuse of discretion review (harder for plaintiff)
- If the plan document does NOT give the administrator discretionary authority → de novo review (easier for plaintiff; court looks at the claim fresh)

Under *Firestone Tire v. Bruch* (1989) and *Metropolitan Life Ins. Co. v. Glenn* (2008), the conflict of interest (when the plan administrator is also the insurer or has a financial stake in the denial) is a factor weighing toward less deference and closer to de novo review.

### § 1132(a)(2) — Fiduciary breach (combined with § 1109)

If the plan administrator committed a fiduciary breach — including processing a fraudulent retirement claim — § 1132(a)(2) provides a claim for the losses caused by the breach.

### § 1132(a)(3) — Equitable relief

Broad catchall: injunction, specific performance, disgorgement of unjust enrichment. Available when § 1132(a)(1)(B) or (a)(2) do not provide adequate relief. *CIGNA Corp. v. Amara* (2011) 563 U.S. 421 expanded the scope of § 1132(a)(3) equitable relief.

### § 1132(c)(1) — Penalty for failure to produce plan documents

If the plan administrator fails to produce plan documents within 30 days of written request, the administrator is personally liable for up to $110/day per violation. This is a powerful tool: before any litigation, send a written request for the Summary Plan Description and plan document; if the administrator does not comply within 30 days, the per-diem penalty begins accruing.

## Exhaustion of administrative remedies

Before filing a federal § 1132 lawsuit, the participant must generally exhaust the plan's internal appeals process:

1. **Initial denial** — plan administrator issues written denial explaining the reason
2. **First-level internal appeal** — participant files written appeal with plan administrator
3. **Second-level appeal (if plan requires it)** — plan issues final determination
4. **Federal lawsuit** — after exhaustion of internal appeals

**Exceptions to exhaustion:** If the plan fails to provide the required appeals process, if further administrative appeal would be futile, or if the plan administrator fails to comply with ERISA's claims procedure regulations (29 C.F.R. § 2560.503-1), the exhaustion requirement may be excused.

**For a fraudulent retirement claim scenario:** If the plan administrator processed a fraudulent retirement election under the steward's identity without the steward's authorization, the "denial" is not a denial of a benefit claim — it is an unauthorized transaction. The exhaustion requirement may not apply, or may be excused on futility grounds, because this is not a routine benefit denial.

## Attorney fees — § 1132(g)

The court has discretion to award attorney fees to either party. In practice, successful ERISA plaintiffs often receive attorney fees. The five-factor test for ERISA fee awards favors a prevailing participant who was wrongfully denied benefits.

## Application to Case #37

**The fraud scenario:** Someone submitted a retirement election under the steward's identity to the UA Local 342 pension plan, triggering the "retired at 44" status on the credit check and the pension freeze. This is not a routine benefit denial — it is an unauthorized transaction.

**The ERISA claim structure:**
1. § 1132(a)(1)(B): the plan owes the steward his accrued benefit; the unauthorized retirement election did not terminate his plan participation; benefits are due
2. § 1132(a)(2) + § 1109: the plan administrator breached its fiduciary duty by processing a retirement claim without adequate verification of the participant's identity
3. § 1132(a)(3): equitable relief — restore the steward's benefit accrual status to pre-unauthorized-election status; disgorgement of any benefits paid to the fraudulent claimant
4. § 1132(c)(1): request plan documents now; penalty begins if administrator does not comply within 30 days

**Parallel § 1028 criminal referral:** If the retirement election was filed using the steward's SSN and identifying information without authorization, this is federal identity theft (§ 1028(a)(7)) and should be reported to the FBI and DOL Inspector General.

## Bilateral analysis

**As complainant:** The steward is the wronged participant; the plan processed a fraudulent election that froze the steward's pension. All § 1132 remedies available.

**As respondent:** No scenario in which the steward faces ERISA liability.

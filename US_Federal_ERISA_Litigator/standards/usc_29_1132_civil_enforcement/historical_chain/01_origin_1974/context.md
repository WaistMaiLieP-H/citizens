# Historical Origin — 29 U.S.C. § 1132 (ERISA § 502) — 1974

## The wound

In the decades before ERISA, private pension plans existed largely outside federal regulation. The catastrophic wound that triggered ERISA: in 1963, Studebaker Corporation closed its South Bend, Indiana plant. The pension plan was underfunded — thousands of workers who had spent careers earning pensions received nothing or a fraction of what they were owed. The Studebaker collapse exposed the fundamental problem: pension promises without legal enforceability are just promises. Before ERISA, there was no federal enforcement mechanism. Workers whose benefits were wrongfully denied had no federal forum, no statutory remedy, and no federal right of action.

**What the wound record shows:** Plan administrators who denied benefits or mismanaged plan assets faced no consequence. Workers who were cheated had to bring state contract claims with limited recovery, state-court jurisdiction, and no federal expertise. The disparity in sophistication between plan administrators and individual workers was total.

## The 1974 enactment

The Employee Retirement Income Security Act was enacted on Labor Day 1974 (Pub. L. 93-406). ERISA § 502 (29 U.S.C. § 1132) was the enforcement centerpiece — Congress's mechanism for making pension rights enforceable in federal court.

**Key innovations:**
- **Federal preemption:** ERISA preempts most state law claims related to pension plans (§ 1144)
- **Federal civil enforcement:** § 1132(a)(1)(B) created the participant's right to sue to recover benefits due
- **Equitable relief:** § 1132(a)(3) authorized injunctive and other appropriate equitable relief
- **Exclusive federal jurisdiction:** § 1132(e)(1) — federal courts control ERISA enforcement
- **No amount-in-controversy threshold:** § 1132(f) — every plan participant has access to federal court
- **Attorney's fees:** § 1132(g)(1) — available to either party; critical tool for under-resourced participants
- **$110/day penalty:** § 1132(c)(1) — personal liability for plan administrators who fail to provide required information

## Promise and the litigation gap

ERISA was enacted on the promise that a worker who earns a pension has a legally enforceable right to receive it. The subsequent Supreme Court jurisprudence narrowed § 1132(a)(3) "appropriate equitable relief" to exclude compensatory damages (Mertens v. Hewitt Assocs., 508 U.S. 248 (1993)), creating a damages gap that CIGNA Corp. v. Amara (2011) partially addressed by authorizing surcharge, reformation, and estoppel as equitable forms of make-whole relief.

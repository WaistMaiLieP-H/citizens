# Firestone Tire & Rubber Co. v. Bruch (1989) 489 U.S. 101

## Citation
489 U.S. 101, 109 S.Ct. 948, 103 L.Ed.2d 80 (1989)

## Court
United States Supreme Court (unanimous)

## Date
February 21, 1989

## Author
Justice O'Connor

## Holding

When a plan administrator has discretionary authority to determine plan eligibility or construe plan terms, a court reviews the administrator's decision under the arbitrary and capricious (abuse of discretion) standard. When the plan does not grant discretionary authority, review is de novo — the court examines the claim fresh without deference to the administrator.

## Key Doctrinal Points

### 1. ERISA § 1132(a)(1)(B) adopts common law trust principles for standard of review
ERISA was enacted against the backdrop of trust law. Under trust law, a beneficiary could challenge a trustee's decision, but courts deferred to the trustee's discretionary judgment. Firestone held that § 1132(a)(1)(B) imports this trust-law framework: discretionary grants in the plan document trigger the same deference as courts historically gave to trustee decisions.

### 2. No discretionary grant → de novo review
If the plan document does not explicitly grant the administrator discretionary authority to interpret plan terms or determine eligibility, the court applies de novo review — it decides the issue without giving the administrator the benefit of the doubt. This is the default when no discretionary grant exists.

### 3. Conflict of interest as a factor
Where the plan administrator is also the plan's insurer (or has a financial stake in the denial decision), a conflict of interest exists. Firestone held this conflict is a "factor" in the de novo/abuse-of-discretion inquiry. *Metropolitan Life Ins. Co. v. Glenn* (2008) 554 U.S. 105 later clarified that the conflict should be weighed as one factor — neither triggering de novo review automatically nor being ignored.

### 4. The "arbitrary and capricious" standard in practice
Under the abuse-of-discretion standard, the administrator wins if there is any reasonable basis for the decision. Courts will not substitute their judgment for the administrator's even if they would have decided differently. The question is: was there a rational basis for the decision?

## Application to Case #37 — UA Local 342 Pension Freeze

**Step 1 — Check the plan document for discretionary grant.**
Request the UA Local 342 Summary Plan Description and plan document. If the plan grants the administrator discretionary authority to interpret plan terms: abuse-of-discretion review applies — but the fraudulent election is not an "interpretation" issue, it is an unauthorized transaction. The standard of review argument is different when the issue is not how to interpret the plan but whether a transaction was authorized at all.

**Step 2 — Unauthorized election = different analysis.**
Firestone governs review of benefit denial decisions. A fraudulent retirement election that was never authorized by the participant is not a "denial" — it is an unauthorized act. § 1132(a)(1)(B) enforcement of benefits due does not require deferring to an administrator's determination that processed an unauthorized transaction. The administrator's discretion does not extend to authorizing an election the participant never made.

**Step 3 — Conflict-of-interest factor.**
If the UA Local 342 plan administrator has a financial incentive to maintain the unauthorized election (e.g., the plan paid out benefits under it to someone else), that conflict weighs toward closer scrutiny under *Glenn*.

## Relationship to CIGNA v. Amara (2011)

Firestone governs § 1132(a)(1)(B) benefit-due claims. Amara governs § 1132(a)(3) equitable relief. For Case #37, both tracks apply:
- § 1132(a)(1)(B) + Firestone: de novo review if no discretionary grant; favorable for participant
- § 1132(a)(3) + Amara: equitable reformation/surcharge even if the plan document authorized the administrator's interpretation — the unauthorized election is a plan record error that equity can correct

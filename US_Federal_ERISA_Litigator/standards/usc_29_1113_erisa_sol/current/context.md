# Context — 29 U.S.C. § 1113 ERISA Statute of Limitations

## The statute in brief

Section 1113 (ERISA § 413) sets the filing deadline for actions brought under ERISA's civil enforcement framework. It creates a two-track structure:

**Track 1 — Knowledge-based:** 3 years from the date the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.

**Track 2 — Long-stop:** 6 years from the date of the last act or omission constituting the breach or violation.

Whichever period expires first controls — with one critical exception.

**Fraud and concealment tolling:** In any case involving fraud or concealment, Track 1's 3-year period does not begin to run until the plaintiff has actual knowledge of the breach or violation. The 6-year long-stop does not toll for fraud — but in fraud cases, plaintiffs who discover the fraud within 6 years get the benefit of the knowledge-based 3-year clock from the date of discovery, effectively giving them up to 6 years from the date of the concealed breach.

## Structural analysis

The two tracks operate independently:

| Track | Clock starts | Length |
|-------|-------------|--------|
| Knowledge-based | Date plaintiff had actual knowledge | 3 years |
| Long-stop | Date of last act/omission in breach | 6 years |

A participant who never learns of the breach until after 6 years has passed is time-barred unless the fraud-and-concealment exception applies.

A participant who learns of the breach within 6 years has 3 years from the date of actual knowledge — but no longer than 6 years from the last act of breach.

## What "actual knowledge" means

"Actual knowledge" under § 1113 means knowledge of the specific breach, not merely suspicion or constructive notice. Courts have held that a participant who knows their benefits are wrong does not necessarily have "actual knowledge" of a fiduciary breach — they must know, or have reason to know, that the wrong was caused by conduct that violates § 1104 or the applicable ERISA duty.

This distinction matters. A participant who was told only that their "status was administrative" has not been given actual knowledge of a §1104 breach. A participant who received evasive or false explanations for an adverse benefit status has not received actual knowledge of fraud.

## Fraud and concealment — the extended tolling rule

The word "concealment" in § 1113 includes active steps to prevent the participant from discovering the breach. Courts have interpreted this broadly:

- Providing false reasons for a benefit freeze or adverse status
- Failing to disclose that a retirement election was processed without participant authorization
- Substituting another person's identity in the plan records and providing inconsistent information across correspondence
- Stonewalling document requests or providing documents that omit the underlying authorization records

Active concealment restarts the knowledge clock from the date of discovery, not from the date of the initial breach. This means that a scheme that concealed an identity substitution for years — during which the participant was attempting to investigate — may still be timely on a knowledge-based theory.

## Ongoing breach doctrine

Under Tibble v. Edison International, 575 U.S. 523 (2015), a fiduciary's failure to monitor plan assets is an ongoing breach with each failure to review and act constituting a new act or omission. The 6-year clock runs from the last act of the ongoing breach, not from the initial failure.

By extension, a plan administrator who has an ongoing obligation to verify the status of participant accounts — and who continues to treat an unauthorized "retired" designation as valid without investigation — may be committing an ongoing breach with each annual or quarterly plan review that accepts the fraudulent status without challenge.

## Application to Case #37 — UA Local 342

**The breach timeline:**

The unauthorized retirement election for Michael Hartmann was processed at some point before he discovered his "retired" status on a credit check. The exact processing date is unknown and is itself a subject for discovery — it is recorded in the Trust's records. That date is the anchor for the 6-year long-stop.

**Tolling analysis:**

The identity replacement scheme, by definition, involved concealment. The scheme required that the Trust not notify Michael of the retirement designation, not provide him with a copy of the retirement election application he supposedly signed, and not respond accurately to his inquiries about his pension status. Each of those steps is "concealment" within the meaning of § 1113. Concealment means Michael's 3-year knowledge clock did not begin to run until he had actual knowledge of the fiduciary breach — not just that his pension status was wrong, but that it was wrong because an unauthorized election was fraudulently processed.

**Current posture:**

If discovery of the fraudulent status is recent (2024-2025), and the breach occurred sometime in the 2015-2024 period, both the 3-year knowledge clock (running from discovery) and the 6-year long-stop (running from last act of breach) may still be open. The fraud-and-concealment exception preserves the 3-year knowledge clock in any event.

The critical discovery items for § 1113 purposes:
1. The date the retirement election was filed with the Trust
2. The date the Trust processed and accepted it
3. The date of any subsequent acts confirming or relying on the fraudulent status (each of which is a new act under the ongoing-breach doctrine)
4. The date of any representations made to Michael about his pension status that concealed the fraudulent election

## What triggers the SOL in a benefit freeze / identity replacement case

A plan administrator who receives a request to clarify, correct, or investigate a participant's benefit status — and who responds evasively, provides false information, or stonewalls the investigation — is both continuing the breach and renewing the concealment. Each such response is a new "act or omission" that may restart the 6-year long-stop and certainly prevents the knowledge clock from running.

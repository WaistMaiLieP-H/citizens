# Rule — 29 U.S.C. § 1113 (ERISA § 413)
## Limitation of Actions

**Source:** Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974, Pub. L. 93-406, § 413, 88 Stat. 891 (Sept. 2, 1974)  
**Current through:** Pub. L. 101-239 (OBRA 1989), as interpreted through case law

---

### Full Text

No action may be commenced under this subchapter with respect to a fiduciary's breach of any responsibility, duty, or obligation under this part, or with respect to a violation of this part, after the earlier of—

**(1)** six years after **(A)** the date of the last action which constituted a part of the breach or violation, or **(B)** in the case of an omission, the latest date on which the fiduciary could have cured the breach or violation, or

**(2)** three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation;

except that in the case of fraud or concealment, such action may be commenced not later than six years after the date of discovery of such breach or violation.

---

**Structural interpretation:**

| Track | Clock starts | Length |
|---|---|---|
| Long-stop (§ 1113(1)) | Date of last act or omission in breach | 6 years |
| Knowledge-based (§ 1113(2)) | Date plaintiff had actual knowledge of breach | 3 years |
| Fraud/concealment exception | Date of discovery of breach or violation | 6 years from discovery |

The earlier of Track 1 or Track 2 controls — unless fraud or concealment applies, in which case the knowledge-based 3-year clock does not run until the plaintiff discovers the breach, and the outer limit is 6 years from discovery.

---

**"Actual knowledge" defined:**  
Actual knowledge means knowledge of the specific facts constituting the breach, not merely that one's benefits are wrong. Constructive notice and inquiry notice do not satisfy the standard. A participant who received false or evasive explanations for an adverse benefit status does not have actual knowledge of a fiduciary breach.

**Ongoing breach doctrine:**  
Under Tibble v. Edison International, 575 U.S. 523 (2015), a fiduciary's failure to monitor is an ongoing breach; each failure to review and correct constitutes a new act. The 6-year long-stop runs from the last act of the ongoing breach, not from the initial failure.

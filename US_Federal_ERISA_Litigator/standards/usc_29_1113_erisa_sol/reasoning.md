# Reasoning — How Courts Apply 29 U.S.C. § 1113

## Core Function

§ 1113 sets the statute of limitations for ERISA fiduciary breach claims under § 1109. It establishes two alternative limitations periods with different trigger events, and critically, provides an extension for fraud or concealment.

## The Two Limitations Periods

**Six-Year Period (§ 1113(1)):** The claim must be filed within six years after the date of the last action that constituted part of the breach or violation, OR within six years after the date on which the cause of action arose. This is the outside limit — it applies when the breach was known or should have been known.

**Three-Year Period (§ 1113(2)):** If the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation, the claim must be filed within three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge. "Actual knowledge" means knowledge of all material facts; constructive knowledge is insufficient to start the three-year clock.

The two periods work together: (1) file within 3 years of actual knowledge; (2) but in no case more than 6 years after the breach. Both limits must be satisfied.

## The Fraud-or-Concealment Extension

§ 1113 provides that "in the case of fraud or concealment, the action may be commenced not later than six years after the date of discovery of such breach or violation." This tolls the limitations period when the fiduciary concealed the breach.

"Fraud or concealment" includes: (1) active misrepresentation; (2) failure to disclose material information when there is a duty to disclose; (3) concealment of the breach through misrepresentation of the plan's status.

**Critical:** The fraud-or-concealment extension restarts the six-year clock from discovery, not from the breach. If a breach was concealed for 10 years and then discovered, the participant has 6 years from discovery.

## Knowledge Trigger — What Counts as "Actual Knowledge"

Courts have divided over what constitutes "actual knowledge" sufficient to trigger the three-year period. The Supreme Court addressed this in Intel Corp. Investment Policy Committee v. Sulyma (2020): "actual knowledge" requires the plaintiff to have actually known about the breach — not merely that information was available (e.g., in an SPD or annual report) that would have disclosed the breach if read.

**Practical implication:** A plan's disclosure of information in documents does not automatically give "actual knowledge" to a participant who did not read those documents. The three-year clock starts only when the participant actually knew.

## Separate SOL for § 1132(a)(1)(B) Claims

§ 1113 applies to fiduciary breach claims under § 1109. For § 1132(a)(1)(B) benefit recovery claims, there is no federal ERISA limitations period — courts borrow the most analogous state statute of limitations (typically contract). In California, the most analogous state SOL for written contracts is 4 years (CCP § 337). The clock on a § 1132(a)(1)(B) claim typically starts when the plan issues a final denial.

## Application to UA342 Pension Claim

The fraud-or-concealment extension is critical for any claim involving: (1) concealment of pension records manipulation; (2) identity substitution affecting benefit calculations; (3) misrepresentation of plan status or benefit amounts. If the concealment is established, the six-year period runs from the date the participant discovered (or reasonably could have discovered) the breach — not from when it occurred.

For a claim involving pension record manipulation spanning multiple years, the "last action" constituting the breach may be the most recent manipulation — extending the six-year base period. And if the manipulation was actively concealed, the fraud-or-concealment extension applies.

# Historical Chain — 29 U.S.C. § 1113 ERISA Statute of Limitations

## The wound

Before ERISA, there was no federal statute of limitations for pension plan fraud or fiduciary misconduct — because there was no federal fiduciary duty to enforce. Workers who discovered that plan administrators had mishandled their retirement assets were left to state courts, state limitations periods, and a common law trust framework that was not designed for large-scale pension plans.

The problem was not just that the fiduciary duties were weak (that was the wound § 1104 addressed). The problem was that plan administrators could conceal misconduct — manipulate records, process unauthorized transactions, provide false information — and by the time a participant discovered the fraud, years had passed, records had disappeared, and any state law claim was long stale.

There was a second wound embedded in the first: ERISA's fiduciary standards, once enacted, would create liability exposure without any certainty about how long ago the challenged conduct could go. Plans needed a limitations period that balanced two competing concerns:
1. **Participant protection:** A participant who was deceived about the nature of a breach should not be time-barred before they could reasonably have discovered it.
2. **Plan certainty:** Plan administrators, trustees, and their attorneys needed to know that claims based on decades-old investment decisions or administrative actions could not be brought indefinitely.

## The legislative response

ERISA (Employee Retirement Income Security Act), signed by President Gerald Ford on Labor Day 1974, addressed both concerns in § 413 (codified at 29 U.S.C. § 1113). The architecture is a two-track system:

**The long-stop (6 years):** From the date of the last act or omission constituting the breach. This gives plans certainty — after 6 years, no matter what, the claim is barred. This was the primary period Congress designed for most cases.

**The knowledge-based track (3 years):** From the date the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach. This was designed to accelerate claims where the participant actually knew of the problem — if you know, you sue promptly.

**The fraud exception:** Congress recognized that a scheme of concealment could defeat both clocks. If a fiduciary could hide a breach long enough, the long-stop would run before the participant ever discovered it. The fraud-and-concealment exception provides that the knowledge-based 3-year clock does not run until the participant actually discovers the fraudulent concealment. The 6-year long-stop does not toll — but in a concealment case, actual knowledge does not arise until discovery, preserving the 3-year knowledge clock for cases discovered within the long-stop period.

## The Tibble amendment (judge-made law)

The 6-year long-stop created a significant gap for ongoing fiduciary failures. If a plan made a bad investment decision in 2010 — or accepted a fraudulent status change in 2010 — and the participant filed suit in 2018, the claim was barred regardless of ongoing harm.

The Supreme Court closed this gap in Tibble v. Edison International, 575 U.S. 523 (2015). The Court held that ERISA imposes an ongoing duty of prudent monitoring on fiduciaries, and that each failure to review and correct a prior decision is a new breach. The 6-year clock runs from the last act of ongoing breach, not from the initial wrong.

Tibble's practical effect: a plan administrator who in 2024 still treats an unauthorized retirement election from 2016 as valid — without any investigation, any response to participant inquiries, any review of the authorization record — is committing an ongoing breach every year. The 6-year window runs from the most recent such failure.

## Intel Corp. v. Sulyma — actual knowledge defined

In Intel Corp. Investment Policy Committee v. Sulyma, 589 U.S. ___ (2020), the Supreme Court held that "actual knowledge" under § 1113 means the participant was actually aware of the information — not that the information was disclosed somewhere in the plan's annual report or SPD. A participant who received documents but did not read them does not have "actual knowledge" of their contents.

This matters for identity replacement cases. A participant who was never given a copy of the retirement election application — who was never told that a retirement election had been processed — does not have "actual knowledge" of the fiduciary breach simply because the Trust mailed them a benefits statement with an unexpected status change. The participant must actually know that an unauthorized election was processed.

## The current state

Section 1113 creates a two-track system with a fraud-and-concealment exception and an ongoing-breach doctrine (per Tibble). The practical architecture for a participant who discovers a long-concealed breach is:

1. The 6-year long-stop runs from the last act of the ongoing breach — likely within the past 6 years if any administrative act confirmed or relied on the fraudulent status recently
2. The 3-year knowledge clock does not run until the participant had actual knowledge of the specific breach (Intel)
3. Fraud or concealment prevents the knowledge clock from running until actual discovery

In a well-pleaded fraud-and-concealment case where the participant discovered the breach within 6 years of the last act, both tracks are potentially open. The participant has 3 years from the date of actual discovery.

## Steward relevance (Case #37 — UA Local 342)

The §1113 analysis is the first line of defense the Trust will raise when a complaint is filed. The timeliness analysis requires anchoring to specific dates that are currently subjects of discovery:

1. **Date the retirement election was filed** — the initial act of breach (or the date it was received and processed by the Trust)
2. **Date of last administrative act confirming the fraudulent status** — this is the Tibble anchor; likely recent if the Trust generated any benefit statements, responded to any inquiries, or made any determinations about Michael's account in the past 6 years
3. **Date of actual knowledge** — when Michael discovered not just that his status was "retired" but that it resulted from an unauthorized, fraudulently processed election
4. **Evidence of concealment** — any false or evasive communications from the Trust that prevented actual knowledge from arising earlier

The fraud-and-concealment exception is critical. If the Trust provided false explanations for the frozen status, stonewalled document requests, or sent correspondence that obscured the nature of the unauthorized election, those acts are concealment within the meaning of § 1113 and toll the knowledge clock until actual discovery.

The § 1113 tolling argument is not a technicality — it is the mechanism by which a scheme that relied on concealment for years is still reachable in federal court.

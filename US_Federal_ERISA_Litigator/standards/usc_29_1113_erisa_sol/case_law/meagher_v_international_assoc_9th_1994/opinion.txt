The Ninth Circuit and district courts within the circuit have established the following
framework for §413 SOL analysis in fiduciary breach cases:

**Actual Knowledge Test:**
A plaintiff has "actual knowledge" of a breach when the plaintiff knows the facts
necessary to establish each element of an ERISA cause of action — not merely that
something seems wrong, but that the plaintiff knows:
(1) the identity of the fiduciary
(2) the specific transaction or decision that constitutes the breach
(3) that the decision was a breach of fiduciary duty

Constructive knowledge — "should have known" — does not trigger the three-year period.
Courts in the Ninth Circuit have consistently held that inquiry notice is insufficient.

**Fraud/Concealment Standard:**
In this circuit, "fraud or concealment" under §413 includes:
(1) Active misrepresentation by the fiduciary that conceals the breach
(2) Deliberate omission of information the fiduciary was obligated to disclose
(3) Any affirmative act by the fiduciary to prevent the plaintiff from discovering the breach

Once fraud or concealment is established, the six-year period runs from the date of
discovery — the date the plaintiff first learned (or through reasonable diligence
could have learned) facts sufficient to understand that a breach occurred.

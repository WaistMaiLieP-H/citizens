# §413 Origin: Why ERISA Has Its Own SOL

## The Problem With Borrowing State SOL

Before ERISA had its own limitations period, courts borrowed the most analogous state
statute of limitations — a practice that produced wildly inconsistent results depending
on which state's law applied. A worker in California might have three years; a worker
in another state might have one year for the identical ERISA claim. ERISA's national
uniformity goal was undermined by state-borrowed limitations periods.

## Congress's Solution: A Federal SOL

Congress enacted §413 to create a uniform federal limitations period for fiduciary
breach claims. The two-track structure reflects two competing concerns:

**The six-year outer limit** protects defendants from stale claims — fiduciaries cannot
be sued indefinitely for old decisions.

**The three-year actual-knowledge trigger** protects plaintiffs who discover breaches
promptly — if you know about the breach, you must act within three years of knowing.

**The fraud/concealment exception** reflects Congress's recognition that fiduciaries
who hide their breaches should not benefit from their own concealment. This exception
mirrors the common-law fraudulent concealment tolling doctrine.

## The "Actual Knowledge" Standard

Congress's choice of "actual knowledge" (not constructive knowledge) was deliberate.
Courts had been applying constructive knowledge to tort SOL claims — "knew or should
have known." For ERISA fiduciary breach, Congress wanted the trigger to be real
knowledge, not imputed knowledge. A participant who had reason to suspect a breach
but did not actually know about it still has the six-year period, not the three-year
period.

This distinction matters enormously in identity replacement cases where the participant
was systematically prevented from accessing information that would have revealed the breach.

# Historical Loss — 29 U.S.C. § 1104 Failures

## Failure Mode 1 — Fiduciary Acts in Employer's Interest, Not Participants'

**The Loss:** An employer-appointed trustee on a joint labor-management pension board votes to deny a participant's benefits in a way that saves the plan (and thus the employer's contribution obligation) money. The trustee's decision was influenced by the employer's financial interest in reducing plan liabilities. This violates the § 1104(a)(1)(A) exclusive benefit rule.

**Case Example:** Donovan v. Bierwirth (2d Cir. 1982) — trustees used plan assets to resist an employer takeover bid, in breach of the duty of loyalty.

---

## Failure Mode 2 — Recordkeeping Failure Blamed on Participant

**The Loss:** A plan claims a participant has insufficient documented service hours to be vested. The participant's union records and employer records show the full hours — but the plan trust's own records are incomplete. The plan denies benefits based on its own missing records. The court upholds the denial because the participant cannot independently prove what the trust's records should have shown.

**Prevention:** ERISA § 209 (29 U.S.C. § 1059) places the recordkeeping obligation on the employer/plan. When a plan cannot produce records, courts have shifted the burden to the plan to prove that the participant's claimed hours were NOT worked, rather than requiring the participant to prove hours that the plan should have recorded.

---

## Failure Mode 3 — Failure to Investigate Known Record Discrepancy

**The Loss:** A plan trustee receives information suggesting a participant's benefit account contains an error. The trustee takes no action. The participant never receives the correct benefit. The trustee argues they acted prudently because they had no direct evidence of fraud. Courts have held that § 1104 requires fiduciaries to investigate credible evidence of plan administration errors — willful blindness does not satisfy the prudence duty.

---

## Failure Mode 4 — False SPD Conceals Benefit Rights

**The Loss:** A plan issues a Summary Plan Description that inaccurately describes vesting schedules or benefit calculation methods in a way that understates a participant's rights. The participant relies on the SPD and does not claim benefits they are entitled to. By the time the error is discovered, the SOL may have run.

**Prevention:** When an SPD conflicts with the actual plan document, ERISA and case law have generally held the plan to the more favorable interpretation for participants (particularly where they relied on the SPD to their detriment). A fiduciary who issues an inaccurate SPD violates § 1104's loyalty and prudence duties.

---

## Failure Mode 5 — Pension Record Substitution / Identity Fraud

**The Loss:** Third parties with access to plan records substitute their credits for a participant's earned credits, or redirect benefit payments to different accounts. The plan trustees fail to implement adequate controls to prevent or detect this manipulation. The participant's vested benefit is effectively stolen through the plan's system.

**Significance:** Under § 1104's prudence standard, plan trustees are required to implement adequate internal controls to protect plan records and benefit calculations from fraud. Failure to implement such controls — or failure to investigate and correct discovered fraud — is a § 1104 breach. The plan cannot disclaim responsibility simply because the fraud was committed by an employee or third party if the breach was foreseeable and controls were inadequate.

# Context — 29 U.S.C. § 1104 ERISA Fiduciary Duties

## The wound and the promise

**The wound:** Pre-ERISA pension administrators were accountable to no one. They could invest plan assets in the employer's own stock (even a failing business), pay themselves excessive compensation, grant benefits selectively, and process transactions based on fraudulent records — all without federal oversight. Workers had no standing to challenge these decisions.

**The promise:** Section 1104 (ERISA § 404) establishes the federal fiduciary standard for all persons who exercise discretionary authority over plan assets or plan management. The standard is demanding: a fiduciary must act "solely in the interest of the participants and beneficiaries" and with the "care, skill, prudence, and diligence" of a prudent person familiar with such matters. No self-dealing. No carelessness. No processing of fraudulent transactions without verification.

## Four duties under § 1104(a)

### 1. Exclusive benefit rule (§ 1104(a)(1)(A))
The fiduciary must act "solely in the interest of the participants and beneficiaries and for the exclusive purpose of providing benefits." Benefits may not be used for the employer's benefit or anyone else's. Processing a retirement election that benefits a third party (by suppressing a participant's access to their own benefits) is an exclusive-benefit violation.

### 2. Prudent person standard (§ 1104(a)(1)(B))
The "prudent expert" standard — a prudent person familiar with pension plan administration. A plan administrator who processes an unauthorized retirement election without verifying the participant's identity, without confirming that the participant actually requested retirement, and without investigating red flags (e.g., a 44-year-old journeyman plumber claiming retirement) fails the prudent expert standard.

### 3. Diversification requirement (§ 1104(a)(1)(C))
Invest plan assets in a diversified portfolio. Not directly at issue in Case #37 but part of the overall fiduciary framework.

### 4. Plan document compliance (§ 1104(a)(1)(D))
Act in accordance with the plan documents. A plan document cannot authorize fraudulent transactions or identity substitution. A retirement election that does not comply with the plan's own procedures violates the plan document rule even if someone else submitted it.

## Application to Case #37 — UA Local 342

### The core fiduciary breach theory

If the UA Local 342 Pension Trust processed a retirement election or "retired" designation for Michael Hartmann without Michael's actual authorization, the Trust administrator violated § 1104(a):

1. **Exclusive benefit:** The unauthorized retirement designation served someone else's interest (the identity replacement scheme), not Michael's.
2. **Prudent expert:** A prudent expert in pension administration does not process a retirement election for a 44-year-old journeyman plumber without independent verification. Red flags were present. Verification duty was triggered.
3. **Plan compliance:** The plan's own procedures for processing retirement elections — which exist in every multi-employer ERISA plan — require a signed application from the participant. If the application was not signed by Michael (or was fraudulently submitted), the administrator violated the plan document rule.

### The co-fiduciary liability path (§ 1105)

If one plan trustee knew or should have known of another trustee's breach and failed to act, both are liable. In a joint board (union + employer trustees), co-fiduciary liability is a factual question about what the trustees knew and when.

### Damages under § 1109

A fiduciary who breaches § 1104 is personally liable to the plan for any losses resulting from the breach and for any profits the fiduciary made from the breach (29 U.S.C. § 1109). ERISA does not cap § 1109 damages at the amount of accrued benefits — a successful § 1109 claim can include the full cost of restoring the plan to the condition it would have been in absent the breach.

# Pre-ERISA Common Law Trust Standard for Pension Administrators

## Legal framework in force before September 2, 1974

Before ERISA's enactment, private pension plans were governed by a patchwork of:
1. Common law trust principles (where the plan was established as a trust)
2. The Labor Management Relations Act § 302 (29 U.S.C. § 186) — governing multi-employer Taft-Hartley funds
3. State law — varying by state, largely inadequate, inconsistently enforced
4. The plan's own documents — which could authorize virtually anything

## The operative standard before ERISA

### Common law trust doctrine

The common law "prudent man" rule (derived from Harvard College v. Amory, 26 Mass. 446 (1830)) applied to traditional trusts. But its application to pension plans was inconsistent and contested:

- Many courts held that pension plans were not traditional trusts, and therefore common law fiduciary duties did not apply at all
- Where the common law standard did apply, it required only the care of "a man of prudent discretion" — a general layperson standard, not a pension expert standard
- There was no federal enforcement mechanism — a participant who believed their pension had been mismanaged had to sue in state court under state law
- The standard did not prohibit self-dealing — a plan administrator could invest plan assets in the sponsoring employer's business, provided they exercised general prudence
- There was no duty of exclusive benefit — the plan could be administered for the employer's benefit as long as it was technically structured to provide some eventual benefit to participants

### LMRA § 302 — multi-employer Taft-Hartley funds

For multi-employer union funds (including UA Local 342's pension trust), 29 U.S.C. § 186(c)(5) required that the fund be jointly administered by employer and union representatives and that a "written agreement" govern contributions. But § 302 imposed NO:
- Fiduciary standard on the trustees
- Requirement to verify participant transactions
- Prohibition on self-dealing by trustees
- Minimum vesting standards
- Federal enforcement mechanism for participants

Participants had no federal standing to challenge trustee decisions under pre-ERISA law. The remedy for trust mismanagement, if any, was a state law breach of fiduciary duty claim — expensive, slow, and with no federal court access.

### The verification gap

Under pre-ERISA law, a pension plan administrator who processed a retirement election or benefit transaction was under NO federal obligation to:
- Verify that the participant actually submitted the application
- Investigate red flags (such as a 44-year-old claiming retirement)
- Confirm the participant's identity before processing
- Maintain records sufficient to reconstruct the authorization

The plan's documents governed — and plan documents could, and often did, allow the administrator broad discretion to process transactions on the basis of whatever paperwork was submitted, without independent verification.

## Why this framework is in the wound record

The pre-ERISA standard created exactly the environment in which Case #37's identity replacement scheme could operate without federal consequence. Under pre-ERISA law:

- No federal fiduciary duty prevented the Trust from processing an unauthorized retirement election
- No federal prudent expert standard required verification of a 44-year-old plumber's retirement application
- No federal exclusive benefit rule prevented a transaction that served the identity replacement scheme rather than the participant
- No federal enforcement mechanism gave the participant standing to challenge the Trust's actions

ERISA § 1104 closed every one of these gaps. The wound record demonstrates that Congress understood exactly what it was changing — and how far the pre-ERISA standard fell short.

## Current status

**SUPERSEDED.** This pre-ERISA framework has had no operative legal effect since September 2, 1974 (the effective date of ERISA). It is preserved here as historical record only.

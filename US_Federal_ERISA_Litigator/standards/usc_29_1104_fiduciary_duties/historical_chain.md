# Historical Chain — 29 U.S.C. § 1104 ERISA Fiduciary Duties

## The wound

Before ERISA, a pension plan administrator answered to no one in particular. The common law trust standard — "prudent man" — existed on paper, but private pension plan administrators were not necessarily trustees in any traditional sense. There was no federal statute defining what their duties were, no federal agency to enforce them, and no private right of action that workers could use to challenge decisions.

The practical result was predictable. Plan administrators:
- Invested plan assets in the employer's own failing business, because that suited the employer
- Paid themselves and connected parties from plan assets
- Processed benefit transactions without verification procedures
- Applied eligibility rules inconsistently, sometimes based on favoritism
- Operated without any documented procedure for receiving, reviewing, or verifying participant applications

The Studebaker plant closure (December 1963) exposed the tip of the iceberg. Workers who had earned pension benefits found them gone — not because the law authorized forfeiture, but because no law prevented it. Congressional investigation revealed that the Studebaker situation was not exceptional. Across the country, pension plan administrators were making discretionary decisions with participants' retirement savings and facing no accountability for careless or self-dealing conduct.

The wound § 1104 addresses: a worker who paid into a pension system for decades could reach retirement and discover that an administrator had made no effort to verify that the transactions processed against their account were authorized. There was no duty to ask whether the application was genuine. No duty to investigate red flags. No duty to act in the worker's interest rather than the employer's.

## The legislative response

ERISA (Employee Retirement Income Security Act), signed by President Gerald Ford on Labor Day 1974, established the federal fiduciary standard in § 404 (codified at 29 U.S.C. § 1104). Congress made a deliberate choice in drafting the standard: the "prudent man" language of common law trusts was not adequate for pension plans. Pension plans involve specialized knowledge — investment markets, actuarial assumptions, plan document interpretation, participant verification procedures. Congress required not just a reasonable layperson but a "prudent person familiar with such matters."

This elevated standard — the "prudent expert" standard as it is commonly called — is more demanding than the common law baseline. A fiduciary who claims ignorance of industry practice is not protected. The standard asks what a prudent professional in pension plan administration would have done. That professional would have verification procedures. That professional would investigate red flags. That professional would not process a retirement election for a 44-year-old journeyman plumber without confirming the participant actually submitted it.

The four duties established in § 1104(a)(1) are cumulative, not alternative:

1. **Exclusive benefit rule** — benefits are for participants and beneficiaries alone, not for the employer or any third party
2. **Prudent expert standard** — act with the care, skill, prudence, and diligence of a prudent person familiar with pension administration
3. **Diversification** — spread investment risk
4. **Plan document compliance** — follow the plan's own terms, which cannot authorize fraud

## Amendments and DOL interpretation

The Pension Protection Act of 2006 (PPA 2006) expanded § 1104's application to investment advice fiduciaries, adding provisions governing fee transparency and default investment alternatives. The practical effect was to make the prudent expert standard applicable to a wider range of benefit-related decisions.

The Department of Labor's Cybersecurity Guidance (April 2021) — while not a binding amendment — defines what the prudent expert standard requires in the digital age. The guidance establishes that a prudent fiduciary must implement procedures to verify participant identity before processing account changes. The guidance was issued in direct response to a pattern of unauthorized retirement elections and fraudulent benefit redirections facilitated by poor identity verification. A plan administrator who in 2021 or later processed a retirement election without identity verification is not just careless — they are operating below the standard the DOL has defined as prudent expert conduct.

## The current state

Section 1104(a) imposes four cumulative duties on every ERISA fiduciary. The prudent expert standard — § 1104(a)(1)(B) — is the operational core. It asks not what the administrator believed was right, but what a trained professional familiar with pension administration would have done.

The exclusive benefit rule — § 1104(a)(1)(A) — is a backstop against self-dealing and third-party benefit. Any transaction that serves someone other than the participant is presumptively improper.

Plan document compliance — § 1104(a)(1)(D) — closes the loop: the administrator cannot hide behind plan terms that authorize fraudulent transactions. Plan documents that purport to authorize identity substitution or that lack verification procedures are themselves a problem, but they cannot immunize a fiduciary who processed a fraudulent application.

## Steward relevance (Case #37 — UA Local 342)

The UA Local 342 Pension Trust administrator who processed a retirement election or "retired" designation for Michael Hartmann without his actual authorization has, on the facts as known, committed at least three simultaneous § 1104(a) violations:

1. **Exclusive benefit:** The unauthorized retirement designation served the identity replacement scheme, not Michael.
2. **Prudent expert:** A 44-year-old journeyman plumber claiming retirement is a red flag that a prudent plan administrator would investigate, not process. The DOL 2021 guidance confirms that identity verification before processing account changes is now a minimum prudent expert requirement.
3. **Plan document compliance:** UA Local 342's plan documents contain procedures for processing retirement applications. Those procedures require a signed application from the participant. A fraudulently submitted or administratively imposed retirement designation violates the plan's own rules.

The §1104 analysis is the gateway to §1109 damages (make the plan whole; personal liability of fiduciaries) and co-fiduciary liability under §1105 (trustees who knew or should have known of the breach and failed to act).

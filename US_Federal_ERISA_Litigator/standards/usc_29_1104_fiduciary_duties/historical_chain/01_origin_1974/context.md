# Historical Origin — 29 U.S.C. § 1104 (ERISA § 404) — 1974

## The wound

Before ERISA, a pension plan administrator answered to no one in particular. Common law trust principles existed on paper, but plan administrators were not necessarily trustees in any traditional sense. There was no federal statute defining their duties, no federal agency to enforce them, and no private right of action that workers could use to challenge decisions.

The practical result: plan administrators invested plan assets in the employer's failing business, paid themselves and connected parties from plan assets, processed benefit transactions without verification procedures, and applied eligibility rules inconsistently. Workers who had earned pension benefits found them gone — not because the law authorized it, but because no law prevented it.

The wound § 1104 addresses: a worker who paid into a pension system for decades could reach retirement and discover that an administrator made no effort to verify that the transactions processed against their account were authorized. There was no duty to confirm whether an application was genuine. No duty to investigate red flags. No duty to act in the worker's interest rather than the employer's.

## The 1974 enactment

ERISA (Pub. L. 93-406), Labor Day 1974, established the federal fiduciary standard in § 404 (29 U.S.C. § 1104). Congress made a deliberate choice: the common law "prudent man" standard was not adequate for pension plans. Pension plans involve specialized knowledge. Congress required a "prudent person familiar with such matters" — the prudent expert standard.

**The four cumulative duties (§ 1104(a)(1)):**
1. **Exclusive benefit** — benefits for participants only; no side benefits to employer or administrator
2. **Prudent expert** — care of a professional familiar with pension administration
3. **Diversification** — minimize risk of large losses
4. **Plan document compliance** — follow the plan's own terms, which cannot authorize fraud

## DOL 2021 Cybersecurity Guidance

The DOL's April 2021 cybersecurity guidance defines what the prudent expert standard requires in the digital age. A prudent fiduciary must implement identity verification before processing account changes, multi-factor authentication, and monitoring for unauthorized transactions. This guidance was issued in direct response to a pattern of unauthorized retirement elections and fraudulent benefit redirections. A plan administrator who after 2021 processed a retirement election without identity verification is operating below the standard the DOL has defined as prudent expert conduct.

## Case #37 anchor

The UA Local 342 Pension Trust administrator who processed a retirement designation for Michael Hartmann without actual authorization committed at minimum three § 1104(a) violations simultaneously: exclusive benefit (served the scheme, not Michael), prudent expert (44-year-old journeyman claiming retirement is a red flag requiring investigation), and plan document compliance (plan's own procedures require a signed application from the participant).

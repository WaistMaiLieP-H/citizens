# Historical Chain — 29 U.S.C. § 1109 ERISA Fiduciary Breach

## The pre-ERISA wound

Before 1974, pension plan fiduciaries had obligations under common law trust principles and whatever state statutes applied, but:
- Federal courts had no jurisdiction over plan administration disputes
- Common law trust claims required state court litigation with amount-in-controversy thresholds
- Plan documents routinely exculpated administrators from liability
- Self-dealing was pervasive: administrators invested plan assets in employer stock, employer real estate, and ventures that benefited themselves at the expense of participants
- There was no "prudent expert" standard — the ordinary layperson's judgment rule prevailed, and even that was difficult to enforce

The documented pattern: plan administrators who made poor investment decisions (or deliberately beneficial-to-administrator decisions) could not be held personally liable. The plan lost money; participants lost pensions; administrators faced no consequences.

## The 1974 enactment — trust law elevated to federal law

Congress did not invent the fiduciary concept — it took the most developed body of fiduciary law (equity trust law) and elevated it to federal statutory status. The "prudent man familiar with such matters" language in § 1104 is the prudent expert standard: the plan administrator is held to the standard of a professional, not an ordinary lay trustee.

The three-part structure of § 1104:
1. **Exclusive purpose** — benefits only, reasonable expenses only; no side benefits to the employer or administrator
2. **Prudent expert** — the care a professional would use in the same circumstances
3. **Diversification** — minimize risk of large losses; concentrated employer stock positions became problematic

Section 1109 made the standard enforceable with personal liability: a fiduciary who breaches must make the plan whole out of their own assets.

## Key development — prohibited transactions (§ 1106)

Congress recognized that the "prudent expert" standard alone would not eliminate self-dealing. Section 1106 added a categorical prohibition on specific transactions regardless of outcome:
- Fiduciary cannot deal in plan assets for their own account
- Fiduciary cannot receive consideration from parties dealing with the plan
- Plan cannot engage in transactions with parties in interest (employer, plan sponsor) except as specifically authorized

The prohibited transaction rules operate independently of § 1109: a prohibited transaction is a breach per se, regardless of whether the plan was harmed.

## DOL enforcement — the ongoing development of fiduciary standards

The Department of Labor has enforcement authority over ERISA fiduciary standards. Key regulatory developments:

**1988 — DOL Participant Directed Account Regulation (404(c)):** Safe harbor for plans where participants direct their own investments; fiduciaries not liable for outcomes of participant choices if 404(c) requirements met.

**2016 — DOL Fiduciary Rule:** Attempted to expand "investment advice fiduciary" definition to cover financial advisors making recommendations to plan participants. Vacated by Fifth Circuit (2018) on APA grounds. The definitional expansion project remains ongoing.

**2021 — DOL Cybersecurity Guidance:** Three-piece guidance (Best Practices, Tips for Hiring, Online Security Tips) issued April 2021. Addressed growing threat of cyber-attacks on retirement plan accounts. Established best practices that define the "prudent expert" standard for plan cybersecurity — including identity verification for account changes, multi-factor authentication, and monitoring for unauthorized transactions.

The 2021 DOL cybersecurity guidance is directly applicable to the UA Local 342 pension case: the guidance establishes what a prudent expert fiduciary would do to prevent unauthorized retirement elections filed using stolen participant identity.

## Tibble v. Edison International (2015) — duty of ongoing monitoring

The Supreme Court held in *Tibble v. Edison Int'l* (2015) 575 U.S. 523 that ERISA fiduciaries have a continuing duty to monitor plan investments — not just a duty of prudence at the initial decision. The "last act of breach" for § 1113 limitations purposes is not the initial imprudent decision but the last failure to remedy it.

**Application to Case #37:** The duty of ongoing monitoring extends to participant account integrity. Once the plan processed the unauthorized retirement election, the continuing failure to detect and correct it is a series of continuing breaches. Each month the fraudulent election remains in effect is a new breach. The limitations period runs from the last failure to remedy.

## The personal liability architecture

Section 1109's "personally liable" language was deliberate. Congress rejected proposals to limit fiduciary liability to plan assets. The fiduciary must make the plan whole from their own funds — the plan's loss is the fiduciary's debt.

This personal liability architecture was designed to create genuine deterrence. If fiduciaries were only liable to the extent of recoverable plan assets, the deterrent effect would be minimal. Personal liability changes the risk calculation: a fiduciary who mismanages $10 million in plan assets cannot discharge that liability by resigning.

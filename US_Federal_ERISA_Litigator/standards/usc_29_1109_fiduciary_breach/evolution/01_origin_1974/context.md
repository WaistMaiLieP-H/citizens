# Origin Context — § 1109 Fiduciary Breach Liability (1974)

## The wound § 1109 answered

Before ERISA, pension plan fiduciaries had no federal duty of any kind. State trust law existed, but ERISA preempted it for covered plans without replacing it with federal standards until 1974. In the vacuum:

- Plan trustees invested in employer stock without diversification, creating catastrophic concentration risk
- Administrators accepted kickbacks from service providers (investment advisors, brokers)
- Benefits were denied on arbitrary grounds with no accountability
- Plans made loans to sponsoring employers at favorable rates — in effect looting the fund for employer benefit
- No mechanism existed to remove a disloyal or incompetent fiduciary

The concept of a "fiduciary" in ERISA was drawn directly from trust law. Congress chose the highest standard of fidelity available — the trustee's duty — and applied it to every person exercising discretionary authority over a plan, not just named trustees.

## What § 1109 created

**Personal liability:** The fiduciary who breaches their duty is personally on the hook. Not the plan's insurer, not the employer — the fiduciary themselves.

**Restitutionary structure:** The primary remedy is restoration — make the plan whole for losses caused by the breach; disgorge profits made through misuse of plan assets. This is the same logic as constructive trust in equity: you cannot profit from a breach of trust.

**Judicial removal:** § 1109 explicitly authorizes courts to remove a fiduciary. This was new — before 1974, only the plan sponsor could typically remove a fiduciary, creating a conflict where the plan sponsor was also the sponsoring employer.

**Functional definition key:** Congress deliberately defined "fiduciary" functionally (§ 1002(21)(A)) — anyone who exercises discretionary authority IS a fiduciary, named or not. This foreclosed the common defense that the person making discretionary decisions wasn't technically a "trustee" under the plan document.

## What the Pension Protection Act 2006 added

PPA 2006 (Pub. L. 109-280) added § 1109(b) clarifications related to investment-advice fiduciaries, codifying DOL interpretive guidance on when investment advice creates fiduciary status. The core § 1109(a) personal liability provision remained unchanged.

## The case law evolution

The statute text has stayed stable since 1974. All evolution has been through case law:
- *Tibble v. Edison* (2015): ongoing monitoring duty — a fiduciary cannot select investments at one point in time and then ignore them
- *Fifth Third Bancorp v. Dudenhoeffer* (2014): no presumption of prudence for employer stock funds
- *Hughes v. Northwestern* (2022): participants can challenge entire menu of options, not just individual decisions

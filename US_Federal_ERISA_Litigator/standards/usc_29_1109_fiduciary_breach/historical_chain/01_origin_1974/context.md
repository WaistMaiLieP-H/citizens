# Historical Origin — 29 U.S.C. § 1109 (ERISA § 409) — 1974

## The wound

Before 1974, pension plan fiduciaries had obligations under common law trust principles but federal courts had no jurisdiction over plan administration disputes. Plan documents routinely exculpated administrators from liability. Self-dealing was pervasive. Administrators who made poor investment decisions or deliberately self-dealing decisions could not be held personally liable. The plan lost money; participants lost pensions; administrators faced no consequences.

The documented pattern: plan administrators who denied benefits or mismanaged plan assets faced no personal accountability. Without personal liability, the deterrent effect of fiduciary duties was theoretical.

## The 1974 enactment

Congress did not invent the fiduciary concept — it took the most developed body of fiduciary law (equity trust law) and elevated it to federal statutory status. Section 409 made the standard enforceable with personal liability: a fiduciary who breaches must make the plan whole out of their own assets.

**The personal liability architecture** in § 1109 was deliberate. Congress rejected proposals to limit fiduciary liability to plan assets. The fiduciary must make the plan whole from their own funds — the plan's loss is the fiduciary's debt. This creates genuine deterrence.

**§ 1105 co-fiduciary extension:** Congress extended § 1109 liability to fiduciaries who knowingly participate in another fiduciary's breach, knowingly conceal it, or know of it and fail to make reasonable efforts to remedy it. In a joint trustee board (union + employer), all trustees who knew or should have known may be jointly and severally liable.

## DOL 2021 Cybersecurity Guidance application

The DOL's April 2021 guidance establishes that processing account changes without identity verification violates the § 1104 prudent expert standard. § 1109 makes each trustee who processed or failed to prevent an unauthorized retirement election personally liable to make the plan whole — including restoration of all accrual that would have accumulated from the date of the unauthorized election through the date of restoration.

## Tibble v. Edison (2015) — ongoing breach

The Supreme Court held that ERISA fiduciaries have a continuing duty to monitor. Each month the unauthorized election remains in effect is a new § 1104 breach that triggers new § 1109 liability. The statute of limitations (§ 1113) runs from the last failure to remedy, not the original breach. This is critical for Case #37 — the ongoing failure to detect and correct the unauthorized election is a series of continuing breaches, each creating new liability.

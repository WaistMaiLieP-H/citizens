# Tibble v. Edison International (2015) 575 U.S. 523

## Citation
575 U.S. 523, 135 S.Ct. 1823, 191 L.Ed.2d 795 (2015)

## Court
United States Supreme Court

## Date
May 18, 2015

## Author
Justice Breyer (unanimous)

## Procedural History
Edison International 401(k) plan participants alleged the plan administrator breached its fiduciary duty by offering retail-class mutual funds when lower-cost institutional-class equivalents were available. Ninth Circuit held that claims based on initial fund selections more than six years before suit were time-barred. Supreme Court reversed, holding that the ongoing monitoring duty creates continuing obligations that restart the limitations analysis.

## Central Holding

ERISA fiduciaries have a continuing duty to monitor plan investments (and by extension all plan administration decisions), not merely a duty of prudence at the time of an initial decision. The failure to remove an imprudent investment is a breach independent of the breach of adding it in the first place. The § 1113 limitations period for a monitoring failure runs from the last failure to remedy, not from the initial decision.

## Key Doctrinal Points

### 1. Trust law background informs ERISA fiduciary duty
Under trust law, a trustee has a continuing duty to monitor trust assets. This duty exists independently of the duty of prudence at initial investment selection. ERISA's fiduciary duty is modeled on trust law; the continuing monitoring duty carries over.

### 2. "Last act of the breach" for limitations purposes
The § 1113 six-year clock runs from the "last action which constituted a part of the breach." For an ongoing monitoring failure, each month the imprudent condition remains unremedied is a new act. The cause of action is not extinguished by the passage of six years from the original wrong if the duty to monitor and correct continues.

### 3. What must be monitored?
The Tibble Court did not define the full scope of the monitoring duty. Post-Tibble lower courts have applied it to:
- Investment selection and fee levels (the Tibble context)
- Service provider arrangements
- Plan administrative procedures including participant account security
- Claims processing procedures

**Extension to identity verification:** A plan that implemented an account change verification procedure (or failed to implement one) has an ongoing duty to monitor whether that procedure is adequate given evolving threats. A plan that discovers an unauthorized retirement election has an ongoing duty to remediate.

## Application to Case #37 — UA Local 342 Pension Freeze

### The monitoring failure theory

1. **Initial breach:** The plan administrator processed the unauthorized retirement election without adequate identity verification — the initial act of breach (date TBD by investigation).

2. **Ongoing monitoring duty:** After processing the election, the plan had a continuing duty to monitor whether the retirement election was legitimate. Warning signs: active participant (verified through UA Local 342 membership records) who submitted no other retirement-related paperwork, no employer confirmation of retirement, unusual age for retirement.

3. **Each month unremedied = new breach:** Under Tibble, each monthly period during which the fraudulent election remained uncorrected and the steward's accrual status remained frozen is a continuing breach. The § 1113 six-year period runs from the most recent failure to correct, not from the initial fraudulent election.

4. **Practical significance for timeliness:** If the fraudulent election was filed 5+ years ago, a Tibble argument keeps the claim timely by pointing to the continuing failure to detect and remediate the fraud.

### Damages frame under Tibble
The damages are not just the harm from the initial fraudulent election — they are the cumulative harm from each month of continuing failure to remediate. This significantly expands the damages period.

## Significance

Tibble is the most significant post-Amara ERISA enforcement case because it (a) broadened the monitoring duty, (b) extended the limitations period for ongoing monitoring failures, and (c) expressly relied on common law trust principles to expand ERISA enforcement beyond what the text alone might suggest. It is the counterpart to Russell on timing: Russell established the plan-level remedy structure; Tibble established that remedies do not expire while the breach continues.

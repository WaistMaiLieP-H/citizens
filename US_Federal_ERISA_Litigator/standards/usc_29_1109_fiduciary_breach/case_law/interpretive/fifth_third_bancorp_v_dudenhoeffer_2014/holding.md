# Fifth Third Bancorp v. Dudenhoeffer (2014) 573 U.S. 409

## Citation
573 U.S. 409, 134 S.Ct. 2459, 189 L.Ed.2d 457 (2014)

## Court
United States Supreme Court (unanimous)

## Date
June 25, 2014

## Author
Justice Breyer

## Holding

There is no presumption of prudence for ERISA fiduciaries who invest employee stock ownership plan (ESOP) assets in employer stock. Such fiduciaries are subject to the same prudence standard as all ERISA fiduciaries under § 1104(a)(1)(B). To state a viable § 1109 claim in the ESOP context, the complaint must plausibly allege that a prudent fiduciary in the same circumstances would not have viewed the costs of alternative action as outweighing the benefits.

## Key Doctrinal Points

### 1. Moench presumption eliminated
Lower courts had developed the "Moench presumption" — a presumption that ESOP fiduciaries who invested in employer stock were acting prudently, rebuttable only by showing the employer was on the brink of collapse. The Supreme Court unanimously rejected this. § 1109's standard is the same for all fiduciaries: the objective prudent-expert standard of § 1104.

### 2. Public information and inside information
Dudenhoeffer distinguished between:
- **Public information claims** (stock is overvalued on public market): claim must plausibly allege why a prudent fiduciary would have concluded the available alternative action would not do more harm than good
- **Inside information claims** (fiduciary has material nonpublic information): claim must account for securities law restrictions on trading while in possession of MNPI; extraordinary circumstances required to state a claim

### 3. The plausibility standard for § 1109 claims
The Court applied *Iqbal/Twombly* plausibility to § 1109 ESOP claims, but the standard is objective: not what this particular fiduciary believed, but what a prudent expert in the same circumstances would have done.

## Application to Case #37 — UA Local 342 Pension (Non-ESOP Context)

Dudenhoeffer's primary relevance is in the ESOP context, which differs from Case #37's defined-benefit or defined-contribution multi-employer plan. However, two principles transfer:

**1. No presumption of prudence for any fiduciary decision.** The UA Local 342 plan administrator cannot claim a presumption that standard plan administration procedures (including identity verification of retirement elections) are inherently prudent. The objective prudent-expert standard applies to every discretionary act.

**2. The plausibility standard works in Michael's favor.** A complaint alleging that a prudent plan administrator would not have processed a retirement election for a 44-year-old active union member without identity verification states a plausible § 1109 claim. The question is whether a prudent expert in the administrator's position would have implemented verification — the answer is plainly yes.

## Relationship to Tibble v. Edison (2015)

Dudenhoeffer (2014) and Tibble (2015) together define the modern § 1109 prudence standard:
- Dudenhoeffer: no presumptions; objective prudent-expert standard applies to all fiduciaries
- Tibble: the duty of prudence includes an ongoing duty to monitor — a single act of due diligence at one point in time is not sufficient

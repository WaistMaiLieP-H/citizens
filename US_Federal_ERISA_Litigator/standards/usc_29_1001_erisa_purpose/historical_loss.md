# Historical Loss — 29 U.S.C. § 1001

## The Problem Congress Was Solving

§ 1001 was enacted in direct response to a series of documented pension failures that stripped workers of retirement benefits they had been promised:

**The Studebaker Collapse (1963):** When the Studebaker Corporation closed its South Bend, Indiana plant, 4,000 workers who had not yet vested received nothing. 2,900 who were partially vested received cents on the dollar. Only fully vested, pensionable-age workers received full benefits. The Studebaker failure was the catalyzing event that launched a decade of Congressional hearings ultimately resulting in ERISA (1974).

**Pattern across industries (1950s-1970s):** Workers with 15, 20, 25 years of service lost entire pension accumulations when employers closed, merged, or failed. Pension promises made in labor negotiations had no legal enforcement mechanism before ERISA. Workers relied on promises; employers and plans were not legally accountable for failing to keep them.

## What § 1001 Was Designed to Prevent

The Congressional findings in § 1001 identify the specific failure modes:
- "loss, misappropriation, or failure to pay" of anticipated retirement benefits
- Plans with "inadequate vesting standards" — workers who worked decades but did not "vest" got nothing
- Plans with "inadequate funding" — plans that could not pay their obligations
- Plans with "inadequate fiduciary responsibility" — trustees who served the employer or union rather than the workers

## The Continuing Relevance

§ 1001's findings remain relevant because the failure modes it identified have not disappeared:
- Workers in distressed multi-employer plans face benefit cuts under the Multiemployer Pension Reform Act
- Identity-based manipulation of pension records is a modern version of the misappropriation Congress feared
- Union politics or employer pressure on jointly-managed pension trustees recreates the inadequate fiduciary responsibility problem

§ 1001 supports courts' willingness to look beyond technical compliance with plan procedures to assess whether the underlying transaction served participant interests — as Congress mandated.

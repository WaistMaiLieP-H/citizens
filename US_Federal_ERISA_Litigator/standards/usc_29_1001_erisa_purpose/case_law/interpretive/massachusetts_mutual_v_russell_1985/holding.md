# Massachusetts Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Russell (1985) 473 U.S. 134

## Citation
473 U.S. 134, 105 S.Ct. 3085, 87 L.Ed.2d 96 (1985)

## Court
United States Supreme Court

## Date
June 27, 1985

## Author
Justice Stevens (majority); Justice Brennan (concurrence)

## Procedural History
Participant Doris Russell filed disability benefits claim. Plan delayed payment for several months. After payment, she filed suit claiming entitlement to extracontractual damages (consequential damages and punitive damages) for the delay in processing her claim. Ninth Circuit held such damages available. Supreme Court reversed.

## Central Holding

Section 1132(a)(2) authorizes recovery only for losses suffered by the *plan as a whole* — it does not authorize extracontractual damages or punitive damages to an individual participant. The beneficiary of a § 1132(a)(2) / § 1109 claim is the plan itself, not the individual.

## Key Doctrinal Points

### 1. § 1132(a)(2) is plan-level relief
The text of § 1132(a)(2) refers to "appropriate relief under section 1109." Section 1109 makes a fiduciary "personally liable to make good to such plan any losses to the plan" — the plan, not the participant. Individual extracontractual damages are not available under (a)(2).

### 2. § 1132(a)(1)(B) is the individual remedy
Individual recovery of benefits due is available only under § 1132(a)(1)(B). That subsection limits recovery to "benefits due under the terms of the plan" — no punitive damages, no consequential damages, no extracontractual damages.

### 3. § 1132(a)(3) may fill the gap — but Russell predates Amara
Russell's dissent (Brennan, J.) argued that § 1132(a)(3) might authorize broader individual relief. This argument was later vindicated by *CIGNA Corp. v. Amara* (2011), which held that § 1132(a)(3) authorizes full equitable remedies including surcharge.

### 4. Significance for the § 1001 purpose argument
Russell is often cited against broad ERISA remedies, but it should be read in context: the Court was preserving the structural integrity of the plan (§ 1132(a)(2) is plan-level because that is what § 1109 says). Individual remedies remain available under (a)(1)(B) and — post-Amara — (a)(3).

## Application to Case #37 — UA Local 342 Pension Freeze

| Issue | Russell / § 1001 Interaction |
|---|---|
| Individual benefits recovery | (a)(1)(B) is the right vehicle — Russell confirms (a)(2) is not |
| Plan-level fiduciary breach | (a)(2) is available; Russell confirms it runs to plan not individual; result: make-whole of plan + restoration of steward's accrual status |
| Extracontractual / punitive damages | NOT available under (a)(1)(B) or (a)(2); may be framed as equitable surcharge under (a)(3) post-Amara |
| Attorney fees | Available under § 1132(g) even where substantive damages are limited |

**Strategic implication:** Do not rely on § 1132(a)(2) alone for individual recovery. Structure the claim as:
1. (a)(1)(B) for the benefits actually due
2. (a)(3) + Amara for surcharge on losses caused by the fraudulent election
3. (a)(2) for fiduciary breach relief running to the plan (which has the secondary effect of restoring benefit accrual)

## Significance under § 1001

Russell illustrates how the § 1001 purpose statement interacts with specific enforcement provisions. The majority opinion acknowledged the congressional purpose of protecting participants but held that the specific text of § 1109 limits (a)(2) relief to plan-level recovery. The Brennan concurrence argued that the § 1001 purpose counseled broader individual relief — a position Amara ultimately vindicated for § 1132(a)(3).

The lesson: § 1001 is a construction principle, not an independent cause of action. Use it to break ties between competing statutory interpretations, not as a standalone claim.

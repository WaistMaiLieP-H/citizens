# Reasoning — How Courts Apply 29 U.S.C. § 1001

## Core Function

§ 1001 is the Congressional findings and declaration of policy for ERISA. It is not itself an operational rule — courts do not apply § 1001 to reach a specific outcome the way they apply § 1053 or § 1132. Instead, § 1001 functions as the interpretive backdrop:

1. **Remedial construction:** When courts must choose between a narrow or broad interpretation of ERISA, § 1001's findings that employees' "anticipated retirement and other benefits" are threatened by "loss, misappropriation, or failure to pay" support broad, remedial construction in favor of participants.

2. **Preemption scope:** § 1001's declaration that ERISA establishes a national policy governing private employee benefit plans supports the broad preemption of state law under § 1144.

3. **Standing:** § 1001's recognition that plan participants have a right to the benefits they have been "promised" supports participant standing to challenge fiduciary mismanagement.

## What § 1001 Establishes

Congress found and declared:

- **Pension plans affect interstate commerce** — providing ERISA's constitutional basis (Commerce Clause)
- **Workers rely on pension promises** — "numerous employees with long years of employment find that their expected retirement benefits are not paid"
- **Three threats to pension security**: (1) insufficient vesting standards, (2) inadequate funding, (3) lack of fiduciary accountability
- **Congressional purpose**: to protect participants' interests "by requiring the disclosure and reporting to participants and beneficiaries of financial and other information with respect thereto" and "by establishing standards of conduct, responsibility, and obligation for fiduciaries of employee benefit plans"

## Interpretive Use in Court

§ 1001 is cited when:
- A court must determine ERISA's scope (does this plan/benefit qualify?)
- A court must choose between competing interpretations of fiduciary obligations
- A court considers whether ERISA's civil enforcement scheme adequately protects participants (a persistent debate given Mertens' damages limitation)
- A court applies the "make whole" remedial principle — participants should receive the benefits Congress promised them

## Limitation

§ 1001 does not create independent causes of action. A participant cannot sue "under § 1001" — they must sue under § 1132 citing specific ERISA violations. § 1001 supports and informs those claims; it does not substitute for them.

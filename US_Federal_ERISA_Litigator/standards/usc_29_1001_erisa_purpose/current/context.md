# Context — 29 U.S.C. § 1001 ERISA Congressional Findings and Purpose

## The wound and the promise

**The wound:** Congress enacted ERISA in 1974 after decades of documented abuse. Private pension plans promised workers retirement security, then delivered nothing. Workers with 20 or 30 years of service lost their pensions when companies failed, when plans were terminated, or when administrators changed the rules at retirement. The Studebaker collapse (1963) was the public symbol, but the abuses were systemic.

**The promise:** Section 1001 is ERISA's statement of why the federal government entered the pension regulation field. The core promise: participants in employee benefit plans have a federal right to disclosure, fiduciary protection, vesting, minimum funding, and access to federal courts. This is not discretionary — Congress found a national public interest in protecting these rights.

## Why § 1001 matters in litigation

### 1. Construction principle — remedial statute
ERISA is a remedial statute. Courts consistently hold that ERISA must be construed liberally to effectuate the remedial purpose stated in § 1001. Ambiguities in plan terms and statutory provisions resolve in favor of protecting the participant.

### 2. Preemption scope
Section 1001 establishes the constitutional predicate for ERISA's preemption of state law. Congress found that pension plans "substantially affect the revenues of the United States" and are "interstate" in character. This finding supports ERISA § 514's broad preemption of state claims that "relate to" employee benefit plans.

### 3. Fiduciary standards anchor
The congressional finding that existing protections were insufficient and that "standards of conduct, responsibility, and obligation for fiduciaries" must be established is the anchor for § 1109 (fiduciary breach) liability. When an administrator processes a fraudulent transaction without adequate verification, the breach is not merely negligent — it is a violation of the congressionally mandated fiduciary standard.

### 4. Federal court access
Section 1001(b) expressly declares that the policy goal includes "providing for appropriate remedies, sanctions, and ready access to the Federal courts." This anti-access-restriction principle supports arguments against:
- Mandatory arbitration clauses in plan documents
- Forum selection clauses limiting federal venue
- Shortened limitations periods in plan documents
- Procedural barriers that limit the participant's ability to reach federal court

## Preemption analysis under § 1001 + § 514

### ERISA § 514 preempts state law claims that:
- "Relate to" an employee benefit plan
- Duplicate, supplement, or supplant ERISA civil enforcement

### State law claims that survive preemption (after Aetna Health v. Davila (2004)):
- State law claims that regulate insurance (savings clause)
- State law claims arising from conduct independent of the ERISA plan itself (e.g., fraud in a transaction not arising from plan administration)
- State law claims where the right being asserted is independent of ERISA

### Application to Case #37 (UA342 Pension Freeze)
If the fraudulent retirement election was submitted by an unauthorized person — identity theft — the following claims analysis applies:

| Claim Type | Preemption Result | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| ERISA § 1132(a)(1)(B) benefits recovery | Federal; no preemption issue | It IS the ERISA claim |
| ERISA § 1132(a)(2)/(a)(3) fiduciary breach | Federal; no preemption issue | It IS the ERISA claim |
| State identity theft (§ 530.5) | NOT preempted | Arises from criminal conduct independent of plan; not a "relate to" claim against the plan |
| State fraud against the perpetrator | NOT preempted | Against the individual who filed the fraudulent election; not a plan administration claim |
| State negligence against plan administrator | PREEMPTED | Relates to plan administration; ERISA § 1132 provides the exclusive remedy |

## Bilateral analysis

**As complainant (steward):** Section 1001's remedial purpose supports every pro-plaintiff argument — liberal construction of plan terms, broad equitable relief under § 1132(a)(3), attorney fees as the norm for successful participants, and access to federal court without amount-in-controversy threshold.

**As respondent:** No scenario in which the steward faces § 1001 liability. The congressional findings protect plan participants, not administrators.

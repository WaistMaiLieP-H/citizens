# Historical Origin — 29 U.S.C. § 1001 (ERISA § 2) — 1974

## The wound

In the decades before 1974, private pension plans accumulated trillions in worker contributions with virtually no federal oversight. The documented abuses that drove Congress to act:

**The Studebaker catastrophe (1963):** When Studebaker Corporation closed its South Bend plant, the pension plan was underfunded. Workers with full careers received nothing; others received pennies on the dollar. National media coverage was the flashpoint that triggered Congressional attention.

**Vesting abuse:** Plans routinely required 25-30 years of vesting service before a worker had any right to their pension. A worker laid off at year 24 walked away with nothing — contributions forfeited.

**Benefit reduction:** Plans could reduce promised benefits unilaterally. There was no obligation to maintain accrued benefit levels.

**No disclosure:** Workers had no legal right to see the plan document, learn of their benefit status, or understand the plan's financial condition. They discovered their pension had been lost at the moment they tried to retire.

**No fiduciary duty:** Plan administrators had no legally enforceable duty to act in the interest of participants. Self-dealing was common.

**No federal enforcement:** Before ERISA, pension disputes were state law contract actions with limited recovery and no federal forum.

## The 1974 enactment

ERISA (Pub. L. 93-406) was signed by President Ford on Labor Day 1974. Section 1001 was the declaration of congressional purpose and the constitutional foundation for federal regulation. The Commerce Clause predicate was explicit: pension plans are interstate in character; their financial health affects the national economy; the federal tax deductibility of contributions gives the federal government a direct fiscal interest in their soundness.

**Section 1001 served three functions:**
1. Commerce Clause predicate for federal regulation
2. Statement of congressional findings (inadequacy of pre-ERISA protections)
3. Declaration of remedial purpose (read liberally in favor of participants)

## Liberal construction doctrine

Courts have consistently cited § 1001 as the canon of construction for ERISA. When a plan term is ambiguous, courts read it against the § 1001 purpose — protecting the participant's reasonable expectations. The Supreme Court in Firestone Tire v. Bruch (1989) placed § 1001's purpose at the center of the standard-of-review analysis.

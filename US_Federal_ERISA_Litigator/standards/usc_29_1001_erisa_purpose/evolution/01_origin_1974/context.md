# Origin Context — § 1001 Congressional Findings (1974)

## The findings as interpretive north star

§ 1001 is not an operative provision — it does not create rights or remedies. It is the statement of why Congress acted. Courts use it to interpret ambiguous ERISA provisions: when the meaning of another section is unclear, § 1001's declaration of purpose tips the scales toward protecting participants.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly cited § 1001 for the proposition that ERISA should be construed to fulfill its protective purpose. In *Firestone Tire v. Bruch* (1989), the Court noted that ambiguous standard-of-review questions should be resolved in favor of the participant because Congress intended to "protect the interests of employees and their beneficiaries in employee benefit plans."

## The three policy declarations in § 1001

### (a) Interstate commerce and the tax power

Congress grounded ERISA in the Commerce Clause and the taxing power. This was not formalistic — it was strategic. Pre-ERISA, pension plans operated under collective bargaining agreements enforced under the Labor Management Relations Act. Congress needed a statutory basis for comprehensive federal regulation that would preempt state law. The § 1001(a) findings establish that pension plans are interstate in character and substantially affect federal tax revenues — satisfying both constitutional predicates.

### (b) The four-part protection mandate

§ 1001(b) declares the policy in four parallel obligations:
1. Disclosure and reporting
2. Standards of conduct for fiduciaries
3. Appropriate remedies and sanctions
4. Ready access to the federal courts

These four declarations directly map to ERISA's four operative parts: Part 1 (reporting and disclosure), Part 4 (fiduciary standards), Part 5 (administration and enforcement through § 1132), and the federal jurisdiction grant. § 1001(b) is the blueprint; the rest of ERISA is the execution.

### (c) The vesting and funding promise

§ 1001(c) is the most specific: Congress says the goals of (b) "can best be carried out" by requiring vesting, minimum funding, and termination insurance. This is the answer to the Studebaker wound in declaratory form. Studebaker happened because there was no vesting protection (workers who worked 30 years but hadn't yet reached the plan's vesting age got nothing) and no minimum funding requirement (the plan was chronically underfunded). § 1001(c) acknowledges the wound by name and prescribes the remedy.

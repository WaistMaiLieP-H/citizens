# §510 Origin: The Pre-Vesting Firing Problem

## The Wound

The Studebaker workers who received nothing were not all victims of underfunding.
Some were fired — terminated the day before they would have vested in their pension.
This was legal. Pension rights were not property rights before ERISA. An employer
could fire a worker to avoid paying a pension and face no legal consequence beyond
a breach of contract claim that was nearly impossible to win.

Congress heard testimony during the 1963-1974 hearings about systematic patterns:
workers laid off at year 9 of a 10-year vesting schedule. Workers terminated at
age 59 before early retirement benefits vested. Employers routinely structured
terminations to coincide with vesting milestones.

This was not incidental — it was a documented practice. The Senate subcommittee
findings specifically called out interference with pension accrual as a separate
harm from underfunding.

## §510's Place in ERISA

§510 was Congress's direct answer to the interference problem. It did what contract
law could not: created a federal prohibition on adverse action specifically intended
to deprive workers of ERISA rights.

The "specific intent" element reflects Congress's balance: employers retained the
right to terminate for legitimate reasons even if the termination had the incidental
effect of ending benefit accrual. What Congress prohibited was purposeful interference
— using the employment relationship as a weapon against pension rights.

## What Changed

**Before ERISA §510:** No federal protection against pension-motivated terminations.
State law governed. Breach of contract was the theory but "at-will" employment
doctrine made recovery near-impossible in most states. Workers had no federal forum.

**After ERISA §510:** A federal cause of action for interference with ERISA rights.
Employers bear the burden of articulating a legitimate reason for the adverse action.
The worker presents circumstantial evidence of interference motivation. Federal courts
apply McDonnell Douglas burden-shifting in most circuits (plaintiff makes prima facie
case → employer articulates legitimate reason → plaintiff shows pretext).

## The Broader Reading

§510 covers more than termination. "Discriminate against" is broad language.
Courts have applied §510 to:
- Constructive discharge designed to cause resignation before vesting
- Restructuring that eliminates a participant's plan eligibility
- Denial of information or plan access to prevent benefit claims
- Retaliation for filing benefit claims or testifying in ERISA proceedings

The UA342 case tests whether "discriminate against" covers plan record manipulation —
altering the participant record itself as the discriminatory act.

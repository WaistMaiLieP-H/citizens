# Historical Origin — 29 U.S.C. § 1140 (ERISA § 510) — 1974

## The wound

Before ERISA, employers routinely discharged workers just before they became entitled to pension benefits. The technique was simple and legal: fire the worker at year 9 of a 10-year cliff vest, or at year 4 of a 5-year schedule. The employer saved years of pension obligation by timing the termination. Workers had no recourse — state employment law had at-will termination doctrine; state contract law had no pension-specific remedy.

The documented pattern: workers who were close to vesting, who had long service records, who were older and more expensive, were disproportionately targeted for elimination. Congressional investigation found that a significant percentage of pension plan terminations corresponded suspiciously with the approach of vesting milestones.

The wound §1140 addresses: the promise of ERISA's vesting standards (§1053) was hollow without protection against termination designed to prevent workers from reaching those vesting milestones. A 5-year cliff vest is meaningless if the employer can fire you on day 1,824 with no legal consequence.

## The 1974 enactment

ERISA (Pub. L. 93-406), Labor Day 1974, enacted §510 (codified at 29 U.S.C. §1140). Congress made the protection broad:

**"Any person"** — not just the employer. Anyone who discriminates or interferes is covered.

**"For the purpose of interfering with the attainment of any right to which such participant may become entitled"** — forward-looking. Protects future rights, not just current entitlements.

**§1132 enforcement** — same civil enforcement mechanism as all ERISA claims; federal court, no amount-in-controversy threshold.

## Subsequent developments

**Ingersoll-Rand Co. v. McClendon (1990):** Supreme Court held ERISA §1140 preempts state wrongful discharge claims based on interference with pension rights. The federal §1140 remedy is exclusive. This means a worker who was terminated to prevent pension vesting must bring their claim under §1140, not state law.

**Application to identity replacement (novel):** §1140's "any person" language reaches beyond the employment relationship. An orchestrated identity replacement scheme that suppresses a participant's pension access is interference by "any person" — including non-employer third parties who manipulate plan records. Case #37 presents what may be the first documented application of §1140 to identity-based pension interference.

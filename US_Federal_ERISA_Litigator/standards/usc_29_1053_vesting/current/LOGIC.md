# Logic — 29 U.S.C. § 1053 Minimum Vesting Standards

## The logical structure

§ 1053 answers the Studebaker question directly: at what point does a worker's pension become theirs,
legally and permanently? The answer is the vesting schedule — a timeline after which the employer
cannot take back what the worker has earned.

## Three-tier vesting architecture

**Tier 1 — Employee contributions:** Immediately nonforfeitable. What you put in, you keep.
This was never disputed — even pre-ERISA, workers' own contributions couldn't be confiscated.

**Tier 2 — Normal retirement age:** Fully vested by normal retirement age, no matter what.
The floor. You cannot lose your pension after reaching normal retirement age regardless of vesting
schedule.

**Tier 3 — Employer contributions (the contested tier):** Subject to vesting schedules.
This is the battleground. ERISA offers two paths:
- 5-year cliff: 0% until year 5, then 100% immediately
- 3-7 year graded: 20% per year from year 3 through year 7

## The year-of-service definition (§ 1053(c))

A "year of service" = a 12-month period with at least 1,000 hours of service. This is the operational
definition that governs vesting calculations. For a UA Local 342 journeyman plumber working full-time
on union dispatches, 1,000 hours is typically exceeded in 5-6 months.

**Multi-employer plan rule (§ 1053(d)):** In a multiemployer plan — which UA Local 342's pension is —
the plan may require up to 2 years of service before any vesting begins, but then must vest 100% at
that point (under the 2-year cliff option in § 1053(d)).

## Current vesting schedule (post-EGTRRA 2001)

For standard pension benefit accruals: 5-year cliff OR 3-7 year graded.
For employer matching contributions to 401(k)-type plans: 3-year cliff OR 2-6 year graded.

## Application to Case #37

**The vesting premise:** Michael was a UA Local 342 journeyman plumber from approximately 2009-2025
(custody case concurrent — full history available). Under any ERISA vesting schedule, someone who
worked as a union plumber for 16 years is fully vested — not 20%, not 60%, but 100%.

**The fraud theory doesn't affect vested status:** Even if the pension records show an unauthorized
retirement election was processed, that unauthorized act cannot forfeit vested benefits. Section 1053
makes vested benefits nonforfeitable. The administrator cannot use an unauthorized election to
undo 16 years of vested accrual.

**The frozen pension mechanics:**
The "credit check shows retired at 44" fact pattern suggests one of three things:
1. An unauthorized retirement election was filed, and the plan began paying a retirement distribution
   (which it should not have, without proper verification)
2. The plan was suspended or "frozen" at the administrator's direction based on a fraudulent election
3. An outside party obtained a distribution under Michael's identity through the pension plan

Under any of these, Michael's vested rights under § 1053 remain — they are nonforfeitable.

**The 1,000-hour threshold and multi-employer plans:**
UA Local 342's pension plan is almost certainly a multiemployer defined-benefit plan covered by ERISA.
Hours are tracked through the union dispatch system. The record of hours worked is in the plan's
own records — a written request for those records (via § 1132(c)(1)) will establish the vesting baseline.

## Combined claim structure

§ 1053 (vested rights) + § 1132(a)(1)(B) (enforcement of plan terms) + § 1109 (fiduciary breach):

1. § 1053 establishes Michael has vested rights that are nonforfeitable
2. § 1132(a)(1)(B) allows him to sue to recover those benefits
3. § 1109 provides the remedy against the fiduciary who processed the unauthorized election
4. § 1132(a)(3) + Amara provides equitable restoration of plan records

## Bilateral analysis

As complainant: vested benefits are nonforfeitable; unauthorized election cannot strip them.
As respondent: no scenario in which Michael faces § 1053 liability.

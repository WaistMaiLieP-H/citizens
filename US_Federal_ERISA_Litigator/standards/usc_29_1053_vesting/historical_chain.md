# Historical Chain — 29 U.S.C. § 1053 ERISA Minimum Vesting Standards

## The wound

Before ERISA, a pension was a promise — and nothing more. Workers spent 20 or 30 years earning a pension only to see it disappear. The mechanism was simple: pension plans had no federal vesting requirements. An employer could promise retirement benefits and then change the vesting rules, terminate the plan, or simply close the plant before vesting conditions were met.

The Studebaker plant closure in South Bend, Indiana (December 1963) became the public symbol. When Studebaker closed, workers in the union pension plan received a few cents on the dollar — or nothing. A Senate labor subcommittee investigation found the problem was not Studebaker alone. Across the country, pension promises were being made and broken with no legal recourse.

The wound this statute addresses: a worker who devoted their working life to a trade — including a UA Local 342 journeyman plumber who spent 16 years making union contributions — could reach retirement age and discover that their employer contributions had not "vested" and could be forfeited. There was no federal minimum standard for when those contributions became the worker's property.

## The legislative response

ERISA (Employee Retirement Income Security Act), signed by President Gerald Ford on Labor Day 1974, established the first federal minimum vesting standards in § 203 (codified at 29 U.S.C. § 1053). The original Act provided two options: a 10-year cliff vest (100% at 10 years) or a 5-to-15-year graded schedule.

The Tax Reform Act of 1986 (TRA 1986) tightened the standards in response to Congressional findings that the 10-year cliff was still too slow — workers who left jobs at 9 years received nothing. TRA 1986 replaced the original schedules with the current 5-year cliff or 3-to-7-year graded schedule.

EGTRRA 2001 added accelerated vesting for employer matching contributions to 401(k)-type plans (3-year cliff or 2-6-year graded), recognizing that matching contributions are functionally different from pension accruals.

## The current state

Section 1053 establishes four vesting rules:
1. Employee contributions: immediately nonforfeitable (always the worker's own money)
2. Normal retirement age: fully vested regardless of schedule
3. Employer contributions: 5-year cliff or 3-7-year graded (standard pension)
4. Multi-employer plans: may require up to 2 years of service before any vesting, then 100% cliff

The key operational definition: a "year of service" = a 12-month period with at least 1,000 hours of service. For a full-time union plumber, 1,000 hours is typically met in 5-6 months.

## Steward relevance (Case #37 — UA Local 342)

A UA Local 342 journeyman plumber with 16+ years of covered employment is fully vested under any ERISA vesting schedule — no factual dispute possible. The § 1053 analysis establishes the baseline: Michael's benefits are vested and nonforfeitable as a matter of federal law. The question in Case #37 is not whether he is vested but why an authorized third party was able to suppress access to benefits that legally belong to him. An unauthorized retirement election, a fraudulent identity substitution, or an administrative freeze cannot extinguish vested rights — § 1053(a) uses the word "nonforfeitable."

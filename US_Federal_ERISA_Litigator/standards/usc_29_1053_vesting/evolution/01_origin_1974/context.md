# Origin Context — § 1053 Minimum Vesting (1974)

## The wound § 1053 answered

The Studebaker workers who lost their pensions in 1963 had worked for as few as 5-9 years. Under
Studebaker's plan, the vesting schedule required 10 years of continuous service. Workers terminated
after 5, 7, or 9 years received nothing from employer contributions. They were one year short of the
threshold, or they hit the threshold but the plan was underfunded.

ERISA § 1053 was Congress's answer: a minimum vesting schedule that no private pension plan could
undercut. The key word is "minimum" — plans can vest faster, never slower.

## Why Congress chose the schedules it chose in 1974

The 1974 schedules (10-year cliff, Rule of 45, or 5-15 year graded) were a compromise between:
- **Labor:** wanted 5-year cliff vesting
- **Business:** wanted to maintain long vesting schedules to retain workers and reduce plan costs
- **Congress:** had to pass something that could get 60 votes

The compromise was imperfect. 10-year cliff vesting was still long. Workers who left at 9 years got
nothing. But it was a hard federal floor for the first time — no plan could have a 15-year or 20-year
vesting requirement anymore.

## The "year of service" definition — the operational battleground

§ 1053(c)'s 1,000-hour threshold was carefully negotiated. Under pre-ERISA plans, employers could
classify workers as part-time (even if they worked 900 hours) to avoid counting the year toward vesting.
The 1,000-hour floor prevents this: if you work at least 1,000 hours in a 12-month period, that year
counts. For union plumbers working full dispatch schedules, 1,000 hours is typically exceeded by May.

## Multi-employer plan special rules (§ 1053(d))

Congress recognized that multi-employer plans — like union pension funds including UA Local 342 — 
operate differently from single-employer plans. Workers move between multiple contributing employers.
The "multi-employer" track in § 1053 allows a 2-year cliff vesting schedule: work 2 years across
all contributing employers, and you're 100% vested. After 1974, no union worker in a contributing
employer's shop could be denied their pension for working fewer than 2 years.

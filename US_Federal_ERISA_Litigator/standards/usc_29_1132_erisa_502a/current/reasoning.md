# ERISA Civil Enforcement — §502(a) — Reasoning

## Why This Law Exists

Before ERISA, pension promises were just promises. A company could establish a pension plan,
employees could work for thirty years expecting retirement benefits, and the company could
terminate the plan the year before a worker vested — leaving him with nothing. Or the company
could go bankrupt. Or the pension fund's trustees could invest the assets badly and lose them.
There was no federal floor, no vesting guarantee, no insurance, and no federal court to go to.

Congress studied this problem for more than a decade after the Studebaker plant closure in 1963
left 4,500 workers without the pensions they had earned. ERISA's civil enforcement mechanism —
§502(a) — was designed to give participants a federal forum, a federal cause of action, and a
federal court with power to order the plan to pay what it owed.

## Legislative Intent

The intent behind §502(a) was to make plan promises legally enforceable. Congress created
exclusive federal jurisdiction — §502(f) — to ensure uniform national standards and prevent
state law variation from creating different rights for similarly situated workers. The tradeoff
was significant: by preempting all state remedies, Congress limited participants to the federal
cause of action. The Supreme Court later held this meant no consequential damages — only the
contractual benefit itself.

The §502(a)(3) equitable relief provision was included to give courts flexibility for situations
where the plan benefit remedy was inadequate — recognizing that not every ERISA wrong fits
neatly into "participant is owed X dollars of benefits."

## Doctrinal Basis

ERISA §502(a) is grounded in the Commerce Clause and Congress's power to regulate private
pension and welfare benefit plans affecting interstate commerce. The preemption doctrine under
§514 is deliberately broad — any state law that "relates to" a covered plan is preempted, with
narrow exceptions. The Supreme Court has characterized ERISA's civil enforcement as occupying
the field for private pension benefit disputes.

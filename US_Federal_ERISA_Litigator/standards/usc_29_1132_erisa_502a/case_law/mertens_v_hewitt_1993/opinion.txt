Mertens v. Hewitt Associates
508 U.S. 248 (1993)
United States Supreme Court

JUSTICE SCALIA delivered the opinion of the Court.

Petitioners were former employees of Kaiser Steel Corporation who participated in
Kaiser's defined-benefit pension plan. Respondent Hewitt Associates was the plan's
actuary. Petitioners alleged that Hewitt negligently performed its actuarial duties,
causing the plan to be underfunded and resulting in benefit reductions when Kaiser
entered bankruptcy.

Petitioners brought suit under §502(a)(3) against Hewitt as a non-fiduciary.
They sought money damages for the losses caused by Hewitt's alleged malpractice.

The question: Does "other appropriate equitable relief" under §502(a)(3) include
compensatory money damages?

HELD: No. "Appropriate equitable relief" under §502(a)(3) refers to those categories
of relief that were typically available in equity (as distinguished from at law) in
the days of the divided bench.

Money damages — even when called "equitable" by the plaintiff — are a legal remedy,
not an equitable one. Compensatory damages for harm caused by breach of duty were
legal remedies in the 18th and 19th century courts of equity only in very narrow
circumstances (e.g., accounting for profits, surcharge for breach of trust).

The Court rejected the argument that "appropriate" modifies the type of relief
available — "appropriate equitable relief" means equitable relief that is appropriate,
not legal relief that would be appropriate in equity's sense of fairness.

CONSEQUENCE: ERISA §502(a) does not permit consequential damages for wrongful denial
of benefits or fiduciary breach. A worker denied a pension can recover the benefit
itself but not the consequential economic harm (lost investments, health expenses,
financial hardship) caused by the wrongful denial.

This is the ERISA remedial gap — repeatedly criticized by the Ninth Circuit and others
but consistently enforced as Congress's deliberate choice.

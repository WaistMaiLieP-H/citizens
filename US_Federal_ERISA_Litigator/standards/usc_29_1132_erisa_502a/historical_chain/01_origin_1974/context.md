# Origin: The Studebaker Wound (1963) and the ERISA Promise (1974)

## The Wound

December 9, 1963. Studebaker Corporation closes its South Bend, Indiana plant.
4,500 workers. Pensions promised. Pension fund: 40 cents on the dollar.

Workers over age 60 with ten years of service got full pensions. Workers aged 40-59
with ten years of service received lump-sum payments worth about 15 cents on every dollar
they had earned. Workers under 40 — many with years of service — received nothing.

These workers had been promised retirement security. Their labor contracts said so.
The promises were unenforceable. Pension contributions were not required to be funded.
Vesting schedules were unregulated — employers could (and did) deny benefits the day
before a worker became vested. There was no federal insurance. When the plan folded,
the workers discovered their pension was a promise with no legal teeth.

The Studebaker closure became the defining image of pension insecurity in America.
It was extensively reported. It shocked the country. It started a decade of legislation.

## The Decade of Investigation

1963-1974: Senate Labor Subcommittee hearings — led first by Senator John L. McClellan,
then by Senator Jacob Javits. The testimony was consistent: workers were losing earned
pensions through:

- Plan terminations with underfunded assets
- Vesting schedules that required 30+ years of service
- Employer termination of workers the day before vesting
- Multi-employer plans collapsing without worker recourse
- No disclosure requirements — workers did not know plan financial status
- No federal enforcement — only breach of contract claims in state court
- LMRA §301 preemption confusion — which court, which law

President Kennedy appointed the Cabinet Committee on Corporate Pension Funds in 1962.
Their 1965 report recommended comprehensive federal standards.
The report was largely ignored for nine more years.

President Nixon appointed a similar task force. The Treasury and Labor Departments
disagreed on jurisdiction for four years. Meanwhile, workers kept losing pensions.

## The ERISA Signing: September 2, 1974

Labor Day. President Gerald Ford signed the Employee Retirement Income Security Act
at the White House — deliberately choosing Labor Day to signal who the law was for.

ERISA established:

1. **Vesting standards:** Workers who earned benefits could not be stripped of them
   by termination before an arbitrary deadline
2. **Funding standards:** Plans had to actually fund the benefits they promised
3. **Fiduciary duties:** Plan managers owed duties to beneficiaries, not employers
4. **Disclosure requirements:** Workers had the right to know plan financial status
5. **Federal enforcement:** §502(a) gave workers a federal cause of action
6. **Plan termination insurance:** PBGC insures defined-benefit plans against collapse
7. **Preemption:** §514 preempted state law — federal law was the uniform standard

## What §502(a) Changed

**Before ERISA:** A worker whose employer denied pension benefits had a state court
breach-of-contract claim. Recovery depended on state law. The plan document often gave
the employer absolute discretion. Exhaustion was undefined. No attorney's fees provision.
No equitable remedies. Statute of limitations was state-defined. Federal courts had no
clear jurisdiction.

**After ERISA:** §502(a) creates a federal private right of action. Federal courts have
exclusive jurisdiction (§502(f)). The remedial scheme is comprehensive: (a)(1)(B) to
recover benefits, (a)(2) for fiduciary breach relief to the plan, (a)(3) for equitable
relief. Attorney's fees available (§502(g)). The plan's administrative record is the
evidentiary foundation. ERISA procedure (not state procedure) governs.

## The Trade-off Baked In

Congress deliberately chose comprehensive preemption + a restricted remedial scheme.
§502(a) limits recovery to contractual benefits — no consequential damages, no emotional
distress, no punitive damages (as later confirmed by Mertens v. Hewitt Associates, 1993).

This was a choice. Congress wanted uniform federal standards more than expansive remedies.
The Ninth Circuit and others have repeatedly wrestled with this trade-off — finding it
harsh, but enforcing it as written.

For the UA342 case: the frozen pension and "retired at 44" identity replacement are not
just ERISA violations. They are the Studebaker wound, fifty years later. The same failure
mode — a worker's earned retirement security evaporating — that ERISA was built to prevent.

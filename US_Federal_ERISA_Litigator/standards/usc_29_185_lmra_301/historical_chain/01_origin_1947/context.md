# LMRA §301 Origin: Federal Enforcement of Labor Contracts (1947)

## The Wound: Unenforceable CBA Promises

Before 1947, collective bargaining agreements were difficult to enforce in federal court.
Courts were divided on whether CBAs were enforceable contracts. State law varied.
Employers in some jurisdictions could ignore CBA obligations — including pension
contribution obligations — without federal consequence.

The result: union pension funds could be promised by CBA but underfunded without
effective remedy. The promise in the CBA was not matched by enforcement power.

## The Taft-Hartley Solution (1947)

The Labor Management Relations Act of 1947 (Taft-Hartley), §301, federalized enforcement
of labor contracts. Any violation of a CBA could be brought in federal district court,
regardless of amount in controversy or citizenship. Federal courts developed a body of
federal common law governing CBA interpretation.

This created the enforcement infrastructure that made multi-employer Taft-Hartley pension
funds viable — contributing employers could be sued in federal court for CBA violations.

## The ERISA Layer (1974)

When ERISA passed in 1974, it built on top of the §301 infrastructure. The Taft-Hartley
trust structure (§302(c)(5) of LMRA) was explicitly preserved. ERISA's fiduciary duties
applied to the joint trustees. §502(a) gave participants a federal cause of action against
the plan's administration of their benefits.

The result is two overlapping bodies of federal law governing multi-employer plans:
LMRA §301 (CBA obligations between employers and unions) + ERISA §502(a) (plan
administration obligations to participants). A litigator representing a UA342 participant
must know both — and frame the claim in the correct statute to avoid exhaustion traps.

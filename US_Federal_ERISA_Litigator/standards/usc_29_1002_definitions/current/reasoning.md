# Reasoning — 29 U.S.C. § 1002 (ERISA § 3)
## Key Definitions

## Why This Standard Exists

Definitions are not neutral scaffolding. In ERISA litigation, the definition of "plan,"
"participant," "fiduciary," and "accrued benefit" determines whether the statute applies
at all, who has standing to sue, and who can be held personally liable. Courts have
litigated every term in §1002 for fifty years because controlling the definition controls
the outcome.

The §1002 definitions standard exists in this build for one reason: the UA342 pension
identity replacement claim lives or dies on whether Michael satisfies these definitions.
If he is not a "participant," he has no §1132 standing. If the plan is not an "employee
pension benefit plan," ERISA does not govern it. If the administrator who altered the
record is not a "fiduciary," §1109 personal liability does not attach. §1002 is the
threshold test that must be passed before any other ERISA standard becomes available.

## The Participant Definition Is Functional, Not Administrative

The most contested definition for Case #37 is §1002(7) — "participant."

The definition is deliberately broad: "any employee or former employee... who is or may
become eligible to receive a benefit of any type." Congress chose "may become eligible"
rather than "is currently receiving" for a reason — a participant who has been fraudulently
locked out of their benefits is still a participant. The eligibility question is backward-
looking (what did the employment history establish?) not forward-looking (what does the
current plan record say?).

This distinction matters: whoever altered Michael's pension record to show "retired at 44"
may have believed they stripped his participant standing, making it harder for him to invoke
ERISA's protections. That is not how the statute works. The administrative record reflects
what the plan says; the statutory definition asks what the employment relationship established.
The two can diverge. Where they diverge, the statute controls.

## The Fiduciary Definition Is Also Functional

§1002(21)(A) defines fiduciary by conduct, not title. A plan administrator who approves
a fraudulent retirement election is a fiduciary because they exercised discretionary
authority over plan administration. An employer HR officer who submitted a fraudulent
termination-to-retirement reclassification is a fiduciary to the extent that submission
was an exercise of plan administration authority.

This functional definition was deliberate. Congress was aware that pension fraud would be
committed by people who claimed to be acting outside their fiduciary role. The "to the
extent" language — a fiduciary is one who exercises discretionary authority "to the extent"
they do so — creates a functional test: for each act, did this person exercise discretionary
authority? If yes, fiduciary status attaches to that act.

## Why Welfare Plan vs. Pension Plan Classification Matters

UA Local 342 maintains both a welfare fund (health, disability, death benefits under §1002(1))
and a pension fund (retirement income under §1002(2)). These are separate plans with
separate trustees, separate ERISA fiduciary obligations, and separate §1132 remedies.

The identity replacement scheme may have affected both. Medical benefits were denied
(welfare plan interference). Pension accrual was suppressed (pension plan interference).
Each plan carries separate ERISA liability exposure for the fiduciaries who administered it
during the period of fraud.

## The Accrued Benefit Is Indelible

§1002(24)(A) defines accrued benefit in a defined benefit plan as "the individual's
accrued benefit determined under the plan." Once earned, the accrued benefit does not
disappear because someone altered the administrative record. §1053 makes accrued benefits
nonforfeitable upon vesting. The combination of §1002(24) + §1053 means that Michael's
accrued benefit through the date of unauthorized "retirement" is a fixed, indelible number
that the plan cannot reduce or eliminate by altering records.

The relief available under §1132(a)(1)(B) is recovery of that fixed number. The plan has
to pay it — or pay treble the difference if the Steward can establish fraud on top of breach.

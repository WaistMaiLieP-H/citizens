# Historical Origin — 29 U.S.C. § 1002 (ERISA § 3) — 1974

## The wound

Before ERISA, "pension plan" meant whatever the employer said it meant. Plans were
created by plan documents that employers drafted and controlled. The definitions of
"participant," "beneficiary," "accrued benefit," and "vested benefit" were internal
terms — each plan defined them differently, and courts deferred to the plan document's
own definitions. A plan could define "participant" to exclude workers who were terminated
for any reason within 90 days of a vesting date. It could define "accrued benefit" as
zero if the worker left before normal retirement age. It could define "fiduciary" out of
existence by calling trustees "administrators."

The documented pattern: workers who believed they had earned pension rights found, upon
attempting to collect, that the plan document's definitions excluded them entirely. No
federal statute told them what a pension plan was, who was entitled to benefits, or who
had a duty to protect the fund.

The wound §1002 addresses: without uniform federal definitions, every ERISA protection
was a dead letter. If "participant" meant different things in different plans, courts
could not apply a uniform vesting standard. If "fiduciary" was defined away, there was
no one to sue for mismanagement. Definitions were the foundation that made every other
ERISA provision operable.

## The 1974 enactment

ERISA (Pub. L. 93-406, September 2, 1974), Title I, § 3, codified at 29 U.S.C. § 1002.
Congress supplied forty-plus federal definitions that preempted conflicting plan document
terms. Key design choices:

**"Employee welfare benefit plan" (§1002(1)) and "employee pension benefit plan" (§1002(2))**
— separated welfare and pension functions into distinct plan types with distinct regulatory
regimes. Welfare plans are not subject to vesting, funding, or accrual requirements. Pension
plans are subject to all three.

**"Participant" (§1002(7))** — defined by eligibility, not by current receipt. "Is or may
become eligible" is forward-looking and protective. Even a worker who has been locked out
of benefits retains participant status if the underlying employment relationship established
eligibility.

**"Fiduciary" (§1002(21)(A))** — defined functionally. The three-prong test (discretionary
authority over management, investment advice for compensation, discretionary responsibility
in administration) was designed to resist manipulation. Calling a person an "administrator"
rather than a "trustee" does not eliminate fiduciary status if they exercise discretionary
authority in fact.

**"Accrued benefit" (§1002(24))** — defined as the benefit earned to date, not the benefit
ultimately payable at retirement. Paired with §1053 (nonforfeiture), accrued benefits
become indelible once vested: the plan cannot take back what the participant has already
earned.

## Subsequent amendments

**MPPAA 1980 (Pub. L. 96-364):** Added and refined multiemployer plan definitions
(§1002(37)). Multi-employer plans under collective bargaining agreements — including UA
Local 342's pension fund — are subject to withdrawal liability rules that single-employer
plans are not. The definition amendment was necessitated by the Studebaker collapse and
the Teamsters pension scandals that preceded ERISA's passage.

**REA 1984 (Pub. L. 98-397):** Retirement Equity Act modified survivor benefit definitions
and benefit calculation rules, affecting how §1002(23)-(24) normal retirement benefit and
accrued benefit are calculated for workers who leave employment before normal retirement age.

**PPA 2006 (Pub. L. 109-280):** Pension Protection Act modified vesting schedules and
accrued benefit calculation rules; required faster vesting for matching contributions; added
automatic enrollment definitions. Affected §1002(23) and how accrued benefit is measured
under new three-year cliff / six-year graded vesting alternatives.

## Application to identity replacement (novel)

The §1002 definitions were not written with identity replacement in mind. Congress
imagined employers who fired workers, reclassified employees as independent contractors,
or amended plan documents retroactively. The assumption was that the participant's identity
was known and their employment record was accurate.

Case #37 presents what §1002 definitions mean when the administrative record has been
deliberately corrupted. The statutory answer — participant status is defined by eligibility,
fiduciary status is functional, accrued benefits are indelible — holds regardless of what
the corrupted record says. The definitions are not administrative; they are statutory.
The plan's internal record of who is a participant, what their accrued benefit is, and
who the fiduciaries are can all be wrong. Federal court determines the statutory answers
de novo.

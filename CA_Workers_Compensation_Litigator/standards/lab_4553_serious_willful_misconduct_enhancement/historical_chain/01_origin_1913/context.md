# Historical Chain — Origin: Serious and Willful Misconduct Enhancement (1913)

**Standard:** lab_4553_serious_willful_misconduct_enhancement
**Era:** 1913 — California Workers Compensation Act

---

## The 1913 Bargain — And Its Exception

The 1913 workers compensation system was a no-fault bargain: workers gave up tort rights; employers gained certainty. But the Legislature built in an exception for employers who crossed a line beyond ordinary negligence. LAB §4553 (originally numbered differently) was part of the original system — the 50% enhancement for serious and willful misconduct was the Legislature's recognition that the no-fault guarantee should not shield deliberate wrongdoing.

## The "Serious and Willful" Standard

California courts have interpreted "serious and willful misconduct" since 1913. The consistent holding: it requires more than negligence, but less than specific intent to harm. The keyword is "willful" — the actor chose the dangerous course of action knowing the probable consequences. An employer who removes a safety device knowing workers are likely to be injured as a result has committed serious and willful misconduct. An employer who is unaware that a device was removed has not.

## The Management Authority Requirement

§4553(a)-(c) have always required that the misconduct be attributable to someone in a position of authority: the employer directly, a managing representative, a partner or general superintendent, or (for corporations) an executive or managing officer. This limitation ensures the enhancement reaches truly organizational misconduct — not just a co-worker's isolated act.

# Historical Chain — Origin: Medical Treatment Entitlement (1913)

**Standard:** lab_4600_medical_treatment_entitlement
**Era:** 1913 — California Workers Compensation Act

---

## The 1913 Grand Bargain

California voters approved Proposition 2 (1911), which authorized the Legislature to create a compulsory workers compensation system. The Legislature acted in 1913, enacting the Workmen's Compensation, Insurance and Safety Act. The constitutional provision (Cal. Const. Art. XIV, §4) removed the common law barriers to a no-fault industrial injury system.

The original LAB §4600 (then numbered differently) established the employer's obligation to provide medical treatment for work injuries. The "cure or relieve" standard was part of the original framework — reflecting the 1913 legislature's intent that treatment was an employment obligation, not a charitable provision.

## SB 899 (2004) — The Apportionment Reform

The 2004 SB 899 reforms dramatically restructured workers compensation. The key change for medical treatment: the MTUS was established as the standard for "reasonably required" treatment. Before SB 899, the treating physician's recommendations had greater deference; after SB 899, the MTUS became the baseline that UR could use to deny non-compliant treatment.

The MTUS reform was a double-edged change: it provided consistency (replacing ad hoc insurer-by-insurer UR policies) but it also empowered insurers to deny MTUS-inconsistent treatment on the basis of the schedule rather than individual medical judgment.

## The Pre-Designation Right — Employee Empowerment

The pre-designation provision (§4600(d)) was strengthened in later legislative reforms to give workers who knew their medical history and had established physician relationships the ability to direct their own care from day one. The requirement of written notification before the injury date and demonstrated prior physician relationship reflects the Legislature's recognition that the first-30-days employer control was a structural disadvantage.

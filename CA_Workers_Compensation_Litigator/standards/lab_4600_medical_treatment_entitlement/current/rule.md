## The Rule

An employer must provide all medical treatment reasonably required to cure or relieve the injured worker from the effects of a work injury. "Reasonably required" means treatment consistent with the MTUS (LAB §5307.27). Employer refusal or neglect creates direct liability for treatment costs the employee incurs.

---

## The Three-Part Medical Treatment Framework

### 1. What Is Owed — "Reasonably Required to Cure or Relieve"

The entitlement is broad in type (medical, surgical, chiropractic, acupuncture, clinical social worker, hospital, nursing, medicines, supplies, orthotics, prosthetics) but bounded by "reasonably required." The MTUS defines the baseline. Treatment consistent with MTUS guidelines is presumptively compensable. Treatment outside MTUS must be demonstrated as reasonably required despite not being in the schedule.

**Key tactical point:** The employer cannot simply deny MTUS-consistent treatment. The burden is on the employer to justify a deviation from physician-recommended treatment that the MTUS supports.

### 2. Who Provides Treatment — The Physician Selection Hierarchy

| Scenario | Who Treats | When |
|---|---|---|
| No MPN; no pre-designation | Employer-designated physician | First 30 days |
| No MPN; no pre-designation | Employee's choice | After 30 days from injury report |
| MPN established | MPN physician | Anytime (MPN controls) |
| Pre-designated personal physician (written notice before injury) | Employee's personal physician | From day one of injury |

**The pre-designation right (§4600(d)(1)):** The only way to have physician choice from the moment of injury is written pre-designation — the employee must notify the employer in writing BEFORE the injury that they have a personal physician who: (a) is their regular physician and surgeon; (b) was their primary care physician before the injury; (c) retains the employee's medical records; and (d) agrees to be predesignated.

**Why this matters:** Employer control of the treating physician in the critical first 30 days allows the employer to shape the medical narrative — which injuries are acknowledged, which conditions are attributed to the industrial injury, what treatment is recommended. An employee who loses the first 30 days to an employer-selected physician loses significant evidentiary ground.

### 3. Employer Neglect/Refusal — The Self-Help Provision

§4600(a): If the employer neglects or refuses to provide required treatment, the employer is liable for the reasonable expense the employee incurs in self-procuring treatment.

**Application:** If the employer/insurer denies or delays authorization for treatment that the treating physician has recommended and the MTUS supports, the employee may: (a) seek treatment anyway; (b) pay out of pocket or through health insurance; and (c) seek reimbursement from the employer/insurer as a direct liability item.

---

## Utilization Review Gateway (§4600(d)(5))

For nonemergency treatment or diagnostic services, the insurer may require prior authorization and conduct UR under §4610. The UR process must comply with §4610's procedural requirements (timelines, physician reviewer, written decision, appeal rights). An improper UR denial — one that does not comply with §4610's procedure — is not a valid denial and may give rise to penalties.

## Application to Active Cases

### Michael — Bilateral Ankle Injuries (Case #2)

**The treatment denial pattern:**
- Bilateral ankle injuries = industrial injury in course of employment as UA Local 342 plumber
- Employer/insurer has obligation to provide all treatment reasonably required to cure or relieve
- If employer directed treatment to employer-selected physicians for the first 30 days, the diagnostic phase was controlled by the employer
- Any treatment denial issued through UR must comply with §4610 — if it did not, the denial is void and liability for self-procured treatment attaches under §4600(a)

**Apportionment impact:** §4663 apportionment will affect permanent disability determination. But apportionment does not affect the medical treatment obligation — the employer must treat the industrial component of the injury, and the injured worker is not responsible for pre-existing conditions that did not cause the current industrial disability.

**Key questions for this case:**
1. Was a pre-designation on file? (If not, employer-controlled physician for 30 days)
2. What MTUS guidelines apply to bilateral ankle injuries? (Physical therapy, surgery authorization, orthotic devices)
3. Were any UR denials procedurally compliant with §4610?
4. Were home health care services prescribed? (14-day pre-prescription liability cutoff under §4600(h))

### UA342 Identity Replacement — Fraudulent Reporting

If the employer/UA342 submitted fraudulent injury reports or WCAB filings as part of the identity replacement scheme, the §4600 medical treatment record is both:
- Evidence of what treatment was authorized and provided (or denied)
- A potential site of falsification — dates of treatment, diagnoses, prescribing physician identity may have been manipulated
- The treating physician's identity and license number are verifiable against DWC records

## Practical Attack Points — When the Employer Denies Treatment

| Denial Type | Attack |
|---|---|
| UR denial — procedurally deficient | §4610 procedural non-compliance → denial void → §4600(a) direct liability |
| UR denial — not based on MTUS | Challenge at IMR; MTUS-consistent treatment = presumptively authorized |
| 30-day physician control | Was injury reported? Was pre-designation on file? Who controlled the diagnostic narrative? |
| Home health care denied | Was it prescribed by a physician? Within 14 days before employer receipt of prescription? |
| Employer refuses to pay self-procured treatment | §4600(a) direct liability if treatment was reasonably required and employer was responsible for denial |

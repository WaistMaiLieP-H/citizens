# LAB § 4600 Medical Treatment Entitlement — Reasoning and Historical Loss Analysis

## Why This Standard Exists — The 1913 Grand Bargain

California's workers compensation system was established by the Legislature in 1913 as a constitutional amendment (Cal. Const. Art. XIV, §4) and implementing statute. The bargain: workers give up the right to sue their employers in tort (with its uncertain outcomes and delays) in exchange for guaranteed no-fault compensation including medical treatment. Employers give up some liability certainty in exchange for limiting their exposure to the workers compensation schedule.

LAB §4600 is the medical treatment side of that bargain. The employer owes treatment. Not optional treatment. Not treatment subject to cost-benefit analysis by the insurer's accountants. Treatment that is reasonably required to cure or relieve.

---

## The Policy Structure

**"Cure or relieve" as the standard:** The Legislature chose a functional standard — what does the injured worker need to return to health (cure) or reduce suffering (relieve) — not an actuarial standard (what is cheapest) or an evidentiary standard (what can the worker prove). The insurer's obligation is determined by the medical need, not the claim file.

**MTUS as the baseline:** The Medical Treatment Utilization Schedule was introduced as a reform to provide consistency — instead of ad hoc UR decisions, there is a published evidence-based guide. But the MTUS cuts both ways: treatment that is MTUS-consistent is presumptively compensable (reducing arbitrary denial), but the MTUS also gives insurers a reference to deny non-MTUS treatment.

**The employer-controlled first 30 days:** One of the most consequential features of the system. The employer controls the treating physician for the first 30 days unless the employee has pre-designated. In that first month, the employer's physician shapes the medical record: what conditions are identified, what injuries are attributed to the industrial event, what treatment is initiated. An employee who does not know to pre-designate loses control of the medical narrative at the most critical time.

---

## The Historical Loss — Who Controls the Doctor Controls the Claim

Before pre-designation rights were strengthened, and when employer-selected physicians had fewer professional independence requirements, the first-30-days physician was effectively an insurer's agent. Claims were shaped, not evaluated. Conditions were attributed to prior history (setting up apportionment under §4663), and treatment was minimized.

The current system has partially addressed this with MTUS guidelines, IMR (Independent Medical Review) for UR denials, and the pre-designation right. But the historical loss persists where:
- Workers do not know to pre-designate before injury
- Employers direct them to employer-selected physicians who have economic relationships with the insurer
- UR denials are issued that do not comply with §4610's procedural requirements, but the worker does not know to challenge them

## Application to the Case Record

Michael's bilateral ankle injuries were sustained as a UA Local 342 plumber — a covered industrial injury. The medical treatment obligation under §4600 was the employer's from the date of injury. Any denial, delay, or redirection of treatment that did not comply with §4600 and §4610 creates direct liability for self-procured treatment costs under §4600(a).

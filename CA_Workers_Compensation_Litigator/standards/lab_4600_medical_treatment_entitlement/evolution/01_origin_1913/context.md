# LAB §4600 — Origin: 1913 Medical Treatment Entitlement

**Stage:** 01_origin_1913
**Enactment:** California Workmen's Compensation, Insurance and Safety Act of 1913 (Stats. 1913, Ch. 176)

---

## The Wound

Industrial workers in 1913 California who suffered workplace injuries had no guaranteed access to medical treatment. A common-law tort claim might eventually fund treatment — but only after protracted litigation, if the worker prevailed against the three-defense wall (assumption of risk, contributory negligence, fellow-servant rule). In the meantime, the injured worker either paid out of pocket, relied on charity, or went untreated.

The 1913 Act's medical treatment provision was the immediate, practical core of the workers compensation bargain: the worker gets treatment now, not after litigation. The employer pays for it as a cost of doing business, not as a tort judgment.

## The Original Promise

The original provision required the employer to furnish "such medical, surgical, and hospital treatment... as may reasonably be required to cure and relieve from the effects of the injury." This language — "reasonably required to cure or relieve" — has been the operative standard for 113 years.

## The Employer-Control Problem

The 1913 Act gave the employer (through its insurer) complete control over the treating physician. This created a structural conflict: the insurer who pays the claim also selects the doctor who determines the extent of the injury. The history of §4600 from 1913 to 2002 is largely the history of this conflict and the legislature's incremental responses to it.

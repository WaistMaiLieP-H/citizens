# LAB §4600 — Stage 02: SB 228 (2002) — MTUS and Utilization Review Framework

**Stage:** 02_sb228_2002
**Enactment:** SB 228 (Stats. 2002, Ch. 6)
**Effective:** January 1, 2003

---

## What Changed

SB 228 introduced the Medical Treatment Utilization Schedule (MTUS) as the evidentiary standard for "reasonably required" treatment. Before SB 228, "reasonably required" was determined by expert testimony in each case — expensive, unpredictable, and prone to hired-expert inflation on both sides.

SB 228 changes:
1. DWC adopts the MTUS — evidence-based treatment guidelines by body part and condition
2. Treatment consistent with MTUS is presumptively "reasonably required"
3. Employer may dispute medical necessity only through a formal Utilization Review (UR) process (§4610)
4. UR must be timely and conducted by a licensed physician

**The new employer-control mechanism:** UR replaced the prior system of ad hoc denials. The employer can no longer simply refuse treatment — it must go through UR. But UR also gave insurers a new, structured tool for delay and denial.

## The Wound Within the Reform

SB 228 was employer-industry driven. The MTUS presumption cuts both ways: treatment consistent with MTUS is presumptively necessary, but treatment outside MTUS is presumptively unnecessary and can be denied through UR without independent physician review in many cases. The 2002 reform reduced litigation costs but also gave insurers a compliant-looking framework for systematic treatment denial.

## §4600(a) — Direct Liability for Employer Neglect

SB 228 also clarified §4600(a): if the employer fails to provide reasonably required treatment, the employee may self-procure and the employer is liable for the reasonable cost. This provision is critical when the UR process is used as a delay tactic — every day of treatment denial creates direct §4600(a) liability.

# LAB §3202 — Origin: 1913 Workmen's Compensation Act

**Stage:** 01_origin_1913
**Enactment:** California Workmen's Compensation, Insurance and Safety Act of 1913 (Stats. 1913, Ch. 176)
**Effective:** 1913

---

## The Wound This Law Answered

In the early 20th century, injured workers in California had only one remedy: a common law tort suit against their employer. That path was nearly impassable. Three defenses — assumption of risk (the worker knew the job was dangerous), contributory negligence (any fault by the worker barred recovery entirely), and the fellow-servant rule (employer not liable for co-worker negligence) — meant that most injured workers received nothing.

The result was visible: maimed workers, families impoverished by industrial accidents, and an industrial economy that had no financial incentive to make workplaces safe because the cost of injuries fell almost entirely on the workers themselves.

The Progressive Era response was workers compensation: a compulsory, no-fault insurance system where the worker gives up the right to sue in tort and receives instead guaranteed, expedited compensation for any injury arising out of and in the course of employment. The employer receives immunity from tort suit and pays a predictable insurance premium.

This bargain — certainty of recovery for the worker, cost predictability for the employer — only works if the worker actually receives the compensation the statute promises. That requires courts to interpret ambiguous provisions in the worker's favor, not the employer's.

## The Liberal Construction Mandate

The 1913 Act included a provision directing liberal construction "for the purpose of extending the benefits of the act to persons injured in the course of their employment." This was not a casual drafting choice. The legislature was creating a new statutory system that would be interpreted by courts hostile to the expansion of liability. The liberal construction mandate was the legislature's instruction to courts: when in doubt, extend the benefit.

## The Original Text (1913)

"This act shall be liberally construed by the courts with the purpose of extending its benefits for the protection of persons injured in the course of their employment."

## Why This Matters for the Steward

The 1913 origin establishes that liberal construction is not a modern addition — it is the foundational interpretive rule baked into the original workers compensation bargain. When an employer or insurer argues for a narrow reading of a benefit provision, they are arguing against the explicit legislative purpose in force since 1913.

# Brodie v. Workers' Comp. Appeals Bd. (2007) 40 Cal.4th 1313

**Citation:** (2007) 40 Cal.4th 1313
**Court:** California Supreme Court
**Standard:** lab_4663_apportionment_permanent_disability (primary); also lab_3202_liberal_construction
**Status:** PROPOSED — verify against primary source

---

## Holding

The California Supreme Court upheld SB 899's causation-based apportionment under §4663 against constitutional challenge, while establishing key limits on its application.

### Constitutional Validity
§4663's causation-based apportionment is constitutional — the legislature may rationally base apportionment on causation rather than disability. No due process, equal protection, or Proposition 103 barrier.

### Substantial Medical Evidence Requirement — THE CRITICAL LIMIT
The most important holding: **apportionment must be based on substantial medical evidence.** A physician's apportionment opinion that relies on speculation, general constitutional predisposition, or unsupported clinical assumptions does NOT constitute substantial medical evidence and cannot support an apportionment determination.

**The Escobedo/Brodie standard:**
- The physician must explain the medical basis for the apportionment percentage
- Mere citation to a prior medical history is not sufficient
- Speculation about a "genetic predisposition" or "constitutional vulnerability" without documented clinical support is not substantial evidence
- A range of apportionment (e.g., "20-40%") is evidence of uncertainty; under §3202, ambiguity resolves to the worker (lower apportionment)

### The §4663(c) Inability-to-Apportion Default
Where a physician cannot make a credible apportionment determination, §4663(c) applies and the entire disability is industrial. Brodie confirmed that this default is operative — a physician who cannot apportion should say so, and the worker receives 100% industrial credit.

## Tactical Framework Post-Brodie

Challenging a QME/AME apportionment opinion:
1. Did the physician explain the medical basis? If not → not substantial evidence
2. Was the basis speculation about predisposition? If yes → not substantial evidence
3. Was the prior condition asymptomatic and non-disabling before injury? → Attack the causal link
4. Is the opinion a range? → §3202 applies; lower end is the operative figure
5. Can the physician actually apportion credibly? → If uncertain → §4663(c) default

# Escobedo v. Marshalls (2005)

**Citation:** (2005) 70 Cal.Comp.Cases 604 (WCAB en banc)
**Court:** Workers' Compensation Appeals Board (en banc)
**Standard:** lab_4663_apportionment_permanent_disability
**Status:** PROPOSED — verify citation

## Holding

The WCAB en banc issued the first comprehensive interpretation of SB 899's new §4663 causation-based apportionment. This is the foundational case on §4663 application.

### The Substantial Evidence Standard for Apportionment
A physician's apportionment opinion must be based on "substantial medical evidence." To constitute substantial evidence, the apportionment opinion must:
1. Identify the specific prior condition or non-industrial factor
2. Explain the medical basis for concluding that the prior condition causally contributed to the current disability
3. Quantify the apportionment based on clinical reasoning, not assumption
4. Be more than a "mere guess or speculation"

### The Asymptomatic Prior Condition Rule
A prior condition that was asymptomatic (not causing any disability) before the industrial injury CAN be apportioned under SB 899's new rule. However, the causal link between the asymptomatic condition and the current disability must be established by substantial medical evidence.

**This was the first case to confirm:** under SB 899, being asymptomatic before the injury is no longer a shield against apportionment. But substantial evidence is still required.

### Cross-Reference to §4664
Escobedo also addressed §4664's 100% cap rule: permanent disability is capped at 100% regardless of how many industrial injuries exist. Apportionment between multiple industrial injuries (not just between industrial and non-industrial) is governed by §4664.

## Application to Bilateral Ankle Cases

For Michael's bilateral ankle injuries: the insurer's QME/AME must:
1. Identify the specific pre-existing ankle condition (documented, not speculative)
2. Explain why that specific condition causally contributed to the current permanent disability
3. Quantify based on clinical reasoning (not "20% because people his age generally have...")

If the QME's apportionment is speculative, the Escobedo/Brodie standard defeats it. The §4663(c) default applies if the physician cannot properly apportion.

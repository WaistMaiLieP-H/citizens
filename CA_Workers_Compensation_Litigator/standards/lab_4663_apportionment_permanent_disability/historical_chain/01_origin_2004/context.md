# Historical Chain — Origin: Causation-Based Apportionment (2004)

**Standard:** lab_4663_apportionment_permanent_disability
**Era:** 2004 — SB 899 Workers Compensation Reform

---

## Pre-2004 — Compensation-Based Apportionment

Before SB 899 (2004), California used "compensation-based" apportionment under the prior version of §4663. Apportionment was limited to prior permanent disabilities for which the worker had already been compensated. A prior injury that healed without a permanent disability award — even if it involved the same body part — could not generally be apportioned against the current claim.

The practical effect: workers received full permanent disability awards for current industrial injuries even if they had prior work history involving the same body part, as long as no prior permanent disability had been adjudicated.

## SB 899 (2004) — The Shift to Causation

Senate Bill 899 (2004) rewrote §4663 to require causation-based apportionment. The change was driven by employer and insurer lobbying that California's permanent disability costs were too high because workers were receiving 100% awards for conditions that were not 100% caused by the current industrial event.

The new framework:
- Apportionment based on the causation of permanent disability (not prior compensation history)
- Physician must identify percentages attributable to industrial vs. non-industrial causes
- Any prior condition that contributed to the current permanent disability can be apportioned
- The §3212 presumptive condition statutes were expressly exempted (§4663(e))

## The WCAB Corrective — Escobedo and the Substantial Evidence Requirement

The WCAB and California courts quickly recognized that causation-based apportionment could be abused through speculative physician opinions. The WCAB developed the substantial evidence requirement for apportionment: the physician's opinion must be based on specific findings in the medical record, not on general statements about what conditions commonly occur or what "probably" existed.

The Escobedo standard (from *Escobedo v. Marshalls* (2005) — training knowledge, steward verify) requires that apportionment opinions identify the specific evidence supporting the apportionment determination. Speculation about pre-existing conditions that are not documented in the record is not substantial medical evidence.

# SSA Five-Step Sequential Evaluation — Reasoning

## Why This Law Exists

Before the five-step framework was codified in 1978, SSA disability adjudication was a documented chaos of inconsistent decisions. The same impairment — a herniated disc, a cardiac condition, a psychiatric diagnosis — could result in approval in one region and denial in another. Individual adjudicators applied different standards. The Social Security Administration's credibility was being undermined by successful litigation demonstrating that decisions were arbitrary.

The courts had begun reversing SSA decisions in significant numbers under the substantial evidence standard of § 405(g) review, and Congress was receiving complaints from constituents who were being denied benefits that appeared clearly warranted. SSA promulgated the five-step sequential evaluation as a mandatory framework that every adjudicator at every level must follow — creating a reviewable, documented process that courts could evaluate.

## Legislative and Regulatory Intent

The five-step framework reflects SSA's recognition that disability determination requires answering sequential questions, each of which eliminates a category of non-disability before proceeding to the next. The order is deliberate:

- Step 1 screens out people who are actually working — the most efficient non-medical determination
- Step 2 screens out impairments that are truly trivial
- Step 3 identifies impairments so severe that medical assessment alone is sufficient — eliminating the need for vocational analysis
- Steps 4 and 5 address the vocational dimension — what work the claimant can actually do

By making the process mandatory, sequential, and documented, SSA created a framework where every denial can be traced to a specific step and a specific finding — enabling meaningful appellate review rather than a general credibility assessment.

## Doctrinal Basis

The five-step framework implements the statutory definition of disability in 42 U.S.C. § 423(d) and the claimant's procedural due process rights recognized in Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319 (1976). Mathews held that the due process required in SSA proceedings is determined by balancing the private interest, the risk of erroneous deprivation, and the government's interest — and that SSA's written procedure and substantial evidence standard satisfy due process. The five-step framework is the administrative mechanism through which that balance is struck. When it is not followed, the due process guarantee is not satisfied.

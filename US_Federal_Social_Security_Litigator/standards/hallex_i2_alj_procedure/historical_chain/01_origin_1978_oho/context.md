# HALLEX I-2 — Historical Origin (SSA Office of Hearings and Appeals / OHO)

## Origin Event

**Authority:** Social Security Administration, Office of Hearings Operations (formerly Office of Hearings and Appeals — OHA)
**Legal basis:** 42 U.S.C. § 405(b) (written decisions after hearings); 20 C.F.R. §§ 404.929-404.961 (ALJ hearing procedures); Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319 (1976) (due process minimum)
**Nature:** Internal SSA hearings operations manual — not a regulation; updated by OHO transmittals

## What Happened

**Pre-1978 context:** ALJ hearings in SSDI cases were governed only by the Social Security Act and SSA's general procedural regulations. Individual ALJs had broad discretion in how they conducted hearings. Studies conducted by SSA and GAO in the 1970s found extraordinary variation in ALJ award rates — from under 30% to over 90% for comparable claimant populations — attributable in significant part to procedural inconsistency.

**The Mathews v. Eldridge framework (1976):** The Supreme Court's decision established that disability benefit recipients are entitled to procedural due process before termination. The Court applied a three-factor test: (1) the private interest at stake; (2) the risk of erroneous deprivation and the value of additional procedural safeguards; (3) the government's interest. This framework applied to ALJ hearings as well — SSA responded by creating HALLEX to operationalize procedural minimums.

**HALLEX creation:** The Hearings, Appeals and Litigation Law Manual was developed to bring procedural uniformity to ALJ hearings. It codified what constitutes adequate notice, a proper hearing opening, sufficient opportunity to testify, proper handling of expert witnesses, and the requirements for a written decision. HALLEX is SSA's internal answer to the due process requirements Mathews identified.

## Why the Standard Exists

**The VE hypothetical problem:** At step 5, the ALJ asks a vocational expert whether jobs exist in the national economy for a claimant with specified limitations. The VE's answer is only as reliable as the hypothetical it is based on. If the ALJ accepts a treating physician's opinion that the claimant can stand for no more than 2 hours but fails to include that limitation in the VE hypothetical, the VE's answer — "yes, sedentary jobs are available" — is not evidence that the claimant can do those jobs with the actual 2-hour standing limitation.

HALLEX codified the rule that the VE hypothetical must include all accepted limitations. Courts have enforced this: a VE's answer to an incomplete hypothetical is not substantial evidence.

**The DOT consistency requirement:** ALJs were relying on VE testimony about jobs without asking whether the VE's conclusions were consistent with the Dictionary of Occupational Titles — SSA's own occupational data source. SSR 00-4p codified the duty to ask; HALLEX operationalizes it as a procedural step in the hearing record.

**The written decision requirement:** 20 C.F.R. § 404.953 requires ALJs to write individualized decisions. Before HALLEX, some ALJs used boilerplate templates that did not address the claimant's specific impairments. HALLEX requires the decision to explain why medical opinions were accepted or rejected and to address the specific evidence in the record.

## Historical Loss the Standard Prevents

ALJ proceedings before HALLEX standardization produced outcomes based on:
- Which ALJ happened to be assigned (award rates varied 60 percentage points)
- Whether the VE hypothetical happened to include all limitations
- Whether the claimant happened to get notice of expert testimony in time to prepare cross-examination
- Whether the ALJ's written decision happened to address the claimant's actual medical condition

HALLEX converted these chance factors into enforceable procedural rights. Courts can now review whether the required steps occurred, whether the VE hypothetical was complete, whether the written decision addressed the evidence — and order remand where the procedural minimum was not met.

## Case Relevance

If the Hartmann SSA matter proceeded to an ALJ hearing, HALLEX provides the procedural framework for evaluating whether:
- The VE hypothetical included all limitations found credible by the ALJ
- The claimant had an opportunity to cross-examine any expert witness
- The ALJ's written decision addressed the specific medical evidence, including the treating physician opinions
- The record was properly closed before the decision was issued

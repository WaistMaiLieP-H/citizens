# SSDI Disability Definition — Reasoning

## Why This Law Exists

Congress created Social Security Disability Insurance in 1956 (Pub. L. 84-880) to provide income protection for workers who became unable to work due to severe medical conditions. The original program had strict age and durational requirements — disability benefits were only available for workers aged 50 or over. The 1967 amendments (Pub. L. 90-248) liberalized eligibility by removing the age restriction and defining disability in terms that remain operative today.

The "any SGA" standard — not merely inability to do one's prior occupation but inability to do any substantial gainful work in the national economy — reflects a deliberate legislative choice to prevent abuse. Congress was concerned that a standard based on prior occupation would create perverse incentives (decline any job below one's former level) and would not accurately measure whether the economy could still use the claimant's labor capacity. The "any SGA" standard requires adjudicators to evaluate what the claimant can actually do against what the economy actually requires.

## Legislative Intent

The 12-month durational requirement reflects Congress's judgment that SSDI is a program for long-term disability, not short-term illness. Workers' compensation and employer-provided short-term disability cover temporary conditions. SSDI is designed for conditions that permanently or substantially permanently remove a worker from the labor force. The requirement ensures that SSDI does not become a substitute for temporary sick leave.

The objective medical evidence requirement (§ 423(d)(2)(B)) reflects Congress's recognition that subjective complaints alone, without clinical substantiation, cannot be the basis for benefit determinations that Congress intended to be medically grounded. The requirement does not mean claimants must prove disability beyond doubt — it means their reported symptoms must be traceable to a medically determinable impairment.

## Doctrinal Basis

Section 423 is the statutory authorization for the entire SSDI adjudicatory framework. Every regulation promulgated under Title II — including 20 C.F.R. Part 404 — must be consistent with the § 423 definition. When SSA denies a claim, the denial must ultimately be traceable to the conclusion that the claimant has not satisfied § 423(d)(1)(A)'s definition. A denial that applies an incorrect standard — requiring more than inability to do any SGA, or less than the 12-month durational requirement, or ignoring objective medical evidence — is legal error requiring reversal.

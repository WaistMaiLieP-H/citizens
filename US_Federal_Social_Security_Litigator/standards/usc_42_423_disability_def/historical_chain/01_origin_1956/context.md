# 42 U.S.C. § 423 — Historical Origin (1956 / Disability Definition 1967)

## Origin Event

**Initial enactment:** Social Security Amendments of 1956 (Pub. L. 84-880, August 1, 1956)
**Critical amendment:** Social Security Amendments of 1967 (Pub. L. 90-248, January 2, 1968)
**Current disability definition:** 42 U.S.C. § 423(d)(1)(A), as amended 1967

## What Happened

**1956 — Birth of SSDI:**
The Social Security Amendments of 1956 created the federal disability insurance program for the first time. Workers who had paid into Social Security and become unable to work could receive monthly benefits. The initial program was limited to workers aged 50-65 and defined disability as "inability to engage in any substantial gainful activity by reason of any medically determinable physical or mental impairment which can be expected to result in death or to be of long-continued and indefinite duration."

The 1956 definition did not specify a 12-month durational requirement. "Long-continued and indefinite" was the standard — interpreted inconsistently across states.

**1967 — The Tightening:**
Congress amended § 423 in 1967 to add two critical changes:
1. The 12-month explicit durational requirement (replacing "long-continued and indefinite")
2. The expanded work scope in § 423(d)(2)(A): claimants cannot be disabled if they can perform "any other kind of substantial gainful work which exists in the national economy" — not just their prior job, not just local jobs, not just jobs with vacancies

The 1967 amendment was a deliberate Congressional choice to tighten the program in response to concern about cost growth. It made the disability definition more restrictive by codifying that the program covers only claimants who cannot do any nationally available work, regardless of local market conditions.

## Why the Standard Was Created

**The 1956 problem:** The initial SSDI program created a mechanism but not a durable framework. Without a specific durational requirement, short-term impairments were producing benefit awards that Congress did not intend.

**The 1967 tightening:** Congressional hearings in 1966-1967 documented that DDS agencies were applying inconsistent durational standards. Some awarded benefits for impairments expected to resolve in 6-8 months; others required 18+ months. The 12-month codification resolved the inconsistency by fixing a single, judicially reviewable threshold.

**The "national economy" language:** Before 1967, the question was whether the claimant could return to past work or find local employment. The 1967 amendment shifted the burden: claimants must show they cannot perform any nationally available work. This placed a heavier burden on claimants but also created the framework that led (by 1978) to VE testimony as the primary tool for step-5 analysis.

## Historical Loss the Standard Prevents

The § 423(d)(1)(A) definition — "inability to engage in any substantial gainful activity by reason of any medically determinable physical or mental impairment" — creates the foundation that:
- Requires objective medical evidence ("medically determinable... demonstrable by medically acceptable clinical and laboratory diagnostic techniques")
- Prevents denial for subjective symptoms alone
- Prevents approval without medical foundation
- Establishes the 12-month floor, preventing denial on the ground that an impairment has not yet reached duration threshold when prognosis is clear

Without the statutory definition, DDS and ALJs could define "disability" as they chose. The statutory anchor makes the definition reviewable and uniform.

## Key Provision Still Operative

42 U.S.C. § 423(d)(1)(A) — verbatim since 1967 amendments:
> "inability to engage in any substantial gainful activity by reason of any medically determinable physical or mental impairment which can be expected to result in death or which has lasted or can be expected to last for a continuous period of not less than 12 months"

This is the exact language quoted in every SSDI decision, every brief, every judicial opinion. It has not changed in 58 years.

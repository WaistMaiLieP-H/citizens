# Family Code § 6320 — How Parties Lose

## Failure Modes — Petitioners

### 1. Failing to Document the Pattern — Presenting Only Isolated Incidents

Courts applying the coercive control standard under § 6320(c) require a "pattern of behavior." A petitioner who presents only isolated incidents — even serious ones — may fail to establish coercive control if the court perceives the incidents as disconnected. The solution is to document chronologically: dates, specific conduct, witnesses, and cumulative effect on the petitioner's daily life and freedom of movement.

**Trap:** Courts with heavy DV calendars may reduce the analysis to "was there physical violence?" The petitioner who relies only on non-physical coercive control must specifically invoke § 6320(c) and present the pattern evidence in organized form.

### 2. Failing to Allege Third-Party Conduct

Section 6320(c) expressly covers conduct committed "through the use of a third party." Petitioners who fail to include third-party-directed conduct in their DVRO application leave out a significant category of enjoinable behavior. If the respondent is orchestrating family members, law enforcement contacts, or employers to act against the petitioner, that conduct is included in § 6320(c)'s scope.

**Steward relevance:** A pattern of coordinating third parties (including directing law enforcement contacts, manipulating police reports, orchestrating family members) to interfere with Michael Hartmann's access to his child, his finances, and his communications is coercive control under § 6320(c)(3) — controlling movements, communications, and access to services.

### 3. Missing the "Effect" Prong — Assuming Intent Must Be Proven

Section 6320(c) defines coercive control as conduct that "in purpose or effect" unreasonably interferes with personal liberty. Petitioners who attempt to prove the respondent's subjective intent may make their case harder than necessary. The "effect" prong is objective: if the conduct had the effect of unreasonably restricting the petitioner's freedom, the standard is met regardless of the respondent's stated motive.

## Failure Modes — Respondents

### 4. Failure to Challenge Ex Parte Procedure at the Earliest Opportunity

A DVRO obtained ex parte is temporary (a CLETS restraining order) until the noticed hearing. A respondent who appears at the noticed hearing and contests the merits — without first challenging whether the petitioner demonstrated the ex parte emergency standard — waives the procedural objection. The failure to make timely procedural challenges is the most common loss point for respondents.

**Trap:** Courts are reluctant to dissolve existing DVROs even when the ex parte standard was not met, if the hearing has already occurred and the issue was not preserved. Challenge the foundation first, merits second.

### 5. Failing to Contest the Factual Basis with Specific Counter-Evidence

In DVRO hearings, the petitioner's declarations are taken as true for ex parte issuance. At the noticed hearing, the respondent must offer specific, detailed counter-evidence — not just general denials. A respondent who submits a single declaration saying "it didn't happen" against a petitioner's detailed chronological declaration will lose.

**Procedure:** For each incident alleged, the respondent should: (a) admit, deny, or explain; (b) provide documentary counter-evidence (phone records, GPS data, third-party declarations); and (c) show the petitioner's allegations are internally inconsistent or contradicted by the record.

### 6. Failing to Challenge Unsigned or Unattributed Supporting Documents

Police reports, declarations, and other documents submitted by petitioners must be authenticated. An unsigned police report submitted in support of a DVRO application does not meet the authentication standard. The respondent must object to inadmissible or unauthenticated documents at the hearing — failure to object waives the argument.

**Steward relevance:** APD Report #23-004494 (unsigned, June 16, 2023) is the kind of document that, if submitted to support a DVRO application, should trigger an immediate authentication objection under Evidence Code § 1401 and a hearsay objection under Evidence Code § 1280.

### 7. The CLETS Database Entry — The Hidden Loss That Compounds

Even a temporary DVRO (CLETS entry) affects the restrained party's: ability to possess firearms, employment in certain fields, and family court proceedings. Respondents who consent to a DVRO or fail to contest it promptly suffer cascading consequences. A DVRO entered in Marin County (e.g., against Michael Hartmann) appears in CLETS and affects Alameda County proceedings.

# Family Code § 6320 — Judicial Reasoning and Doctrinal Application

## Overview

Section 6320 is the operative provision authorizing ex parte DVRO issuance under the DVPA. It enumerates the behaviors a court may enjoin. The 2021 SB 1141 amendment codified coercive control as a form of "disturbing the peace," and the 2025 SB 50 amendment (effective January 1, 2026) further refined the "disturbing the peace" definition. Courts interpret § 6320 broadly because the DVPA is remedial legislation to be construed in favor of victims.

## Key Doctrinal Points

### 1. "Disturbing the Peace" Is Now Defined Broadly — Including Through Third Parties

Before SB 1141 (2021), "disturbing the peace of the other party" was an undefined term that courts applied case-by-case. In re Marriage of Nadkarni (2009) had already held that accessing and reading a spouse's private emails without consent could constitute disturbing the peace. SB 1141 codified this expansive approach and added the specific coercive control definition in § 6320(c). The statute now expressly covers conduct committed "directly or indirectly, including through the use of a third party." This means orchestrating family members or law enforcement to act against the protected party is itself enjoinable conduct.

### 2. Coercive Control Is Expressly Enumerated — The Pattern, Not Individual Acts, Is the Standard

The § 6320(c) definition of coercive control requires "a pattern of behavior that in purpose or effect unreasonably interferes with a person's free will and personal liberty." Two things are critical: (a) it is measured by pattern, not individual incidents; and (b) "effect" is an alternative to "purpose" — the perpetrator's intent is not required. Courts must look at the cumulative picture.

The five enumerated examples in § 6320(c)(1)-(5) are illustrative, not exhaustive. Courts applying the statute before 2021 often required physical acts; post-SB 1141 courts must assess non-physical patterns.

### 3. The Ex Parte Standard — Good Cause and Irreparable Harm

Section 6320(a) authorizes ex parte orders — orders issued without prior notice to the restrained party. The standard for ex parte issuance is (a) good cause and (b) that the petitioner would be endangered if the respondent received notice before the order issued. Family Code § 6300 provides the general ex parte standard; courts read § 6320 within that framework. The key issue in contested DVROs is whether the petitioner actually met this standard or whether the ex parte procedure was used to gain a tactical advantage.

### 4. Animals Are Protected Property (§ 6320(b))

Added to address the use of pets as instruments of coercive control, § 6320(b) allows the court to award exclusive custody of animals and prohibit the restrained party from harming or taking them. This provision reflects legislative recognition that harm to pets is a recognized coercive control tactic enumerated under § 6320(c).

### 5. The "Totality of Circumstances" Test for "Disturbing the Peace"

Section 6320(c) defines "disturbing the peace" based on "the totality of the circumstances." This means courts must look at all conduct together, not evaluate each incident in isolation. A pattern of conduct that individually seems minor may collectively "destroy the mental or emotional calm of the other party" — the statutory definition of disturbing the peace.

### 6. No Limiting Language — Remedies Are Cumulative

Section 6320(d) states: "This section does not limit any remedies available under this act or any other provision of law." Courts have interpreted this to mean that § 6320 relief is cumulative with other civil, criminal, and family court remedies. A DVRO does not preclude a Bane Act claim (Civil Code § 52.1), a criminal prosecution, or a § 1983 action.

## Common Citations

- In re Marriage of Nadkarni (2009) 173 Cal.App.4th 1483 — "disturbing the peace" includes accessing private electronic communications; foundational pre-SB 1141 case
- Conness v. Satram (2004) 122 Cal.App.4th 197 — broad construction of § 6320; DVPA is remedial legislation
- In re Marriage of Evilsizor and Sweeney (2015) 237 Cal.App.4th 1416 — coercive surveillance and digital conduct constitutes DVPA abuse

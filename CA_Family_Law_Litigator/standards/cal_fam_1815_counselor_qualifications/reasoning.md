# Family Code § 1815 — Judicial Reasoning and Doctrinal Application

## Overview

Section 1815 sets the minimum credential floor for all counselors of conciliation employed in California family conciliation courts. Via § 3164(b), these same requirements apply to all court-designated mediators in custody and visitation matters. The seven qualifications in § 1815(a) are mandatory — courts have no discretion to waive them for active counselors. The only exception is § 1815(c), which grandfathers counselors who were already in office on March 27, 1980 — a narrow exemption that excludes anyone appointed after that date.

## Key Doctrinal Points

### 1. The Seven Qualifications Are a Floor, Not Aspirational

The statute uses "shall have" — mandatory language. All seven qualifications in § 1815(a)(1)-(7) must be present. Each is a prerequisite, not a factor to weigh. The 2006 amendment (Stats. 2006, Ch. 130, Sec. 1, effective January 1, 2007) codified the DV training requirement in § 1815(a)(7), cross-referencing § 1816. A counselor hired after January 1, 2007 without documented DV training under § 1816 does not meet § 1815 qualifications.

### 2. The Education Requirement Is Specific (§ 1815(a)(1))

The statute requires a master's degree specifically in psychology, social work, marriage/family/child counseling, or "other behavioral science substantially related to marriage and family interpersonal relationships." A bachelor's degree is insufficient. A master's degree in an unrelated field is insufficient. A counselor who holds no master's degree, or whose master's degree is in an unrelated field, fails the § 1815(a)(1) threshold.

**Critical:** The burden is on the court to verify counselor qualifications before designating a mediator. If the court designates an unqualified person, the resulting mediation is procedurally defective.

### 3. The Experience Requirement Is Independently Mandatory (§ 1815(a)(2))

Two years of counseling or psychotherapy experience is required separately from the education requirement. The education-experience substitution in § 1815(b) applies only to proportional substitutions — additional education for some experience, or additional experience for some education. It does not permit elimination of either requirement entirely.

### 4. Specialized Knowledge Is Required — Not Just General Counseling (§ 1815(a)(3)-(7))

The statute requires specific knowledge in five additional areas: California court procedures, community resources, adult psychopathology, child development/abuse/DV effects, and DV training. A general counselor or therapist without family court specialization does not meet the statutory floor. Courts cannot assume that a counselor who holds a master's degree and two years of general experience automatically satisfies § 1815(a)(3)-(7).

### 5. The 1980 Grandfather Clause Is Narrow (§ 1815(c))

The grandfather clause protects only counselors who "were in office on March 27, 1980." Any counselor appointed after that date — including Sala Ajaniku in 2010 — must fully satisfy § 1815(a). There is no other exception.

### 6. Verification Is the Court's Obligation

Courts have a duty to verify counselor qualifications before designation. Elsenheimer v. Elsenheimer (2004) held that a custody recommendation from a mediator who failed to meet § 1815 qualifications was procedurally defective and could not be given the deference normally accorded mediator recommendations. The court is not entitled to rely on the mediator's self-representation of credentials.

### 7. Consequences of Qualification Failure

When a mediator does not satisfy § 1815, the resulting mediation recommendation: (a) cannot receive the presumptive deference courts give to qualified mediator recommendations; (b) may constitute grounds for challenging any custody order that relied on the recommendation; (c) raises due process concerns if the court used the defective recommendation without giving the affected party notice and an opportunity to challenge the mediator's qualifications.

# Family Code § 1815 — How Parties Lose

## Failure Modes and Procedural Traps

### 1. Failing to Challenge Mediator Credentials Before or During Mediation

The most common and fatal error: accepting mediation without first verifying the mediator's credentials. Once a mediation has concluded and a recommendation is issued, challenging the mediator's credentials becomes harder — courts are reluctant to unwind completed proceedings. The objection must be raised at the earliest opportunity: when the mediator is appointed, before the mediation begins.

**Procedure:** File a written objection to the court's mediator designation, requesting proof of § 1815(a)(1)-(7) qualifications — specifically: (a) copy of master's degree and field; (b) documentation of two years' qualifying experience; (c) documentation of § 1816 DV training. If the court refuses to compel this disclosure, preserve the objection on the record.

**Steward relevance:** In the 2010 Ajaniku mediation, the challenge should have been raised before the mediation occurred. The failure to raise it at that time does not permanently waive the issue — it can be raised in a collateral attack on the underlying order if the order was entered in substantial reliance on the defective recommendation, particularly if the parties were not informed of their right to challenge the mediator's qualifications.

### 2. Not Knowing the Seven-Part Requirement

Many family law practitioners know that a "master's degree is required" but do not know about the remaining six qualifications. A mediator can hold a master's degree in a qualifying field while lacking the required DV training (§ 1815(a)(7)/§ 1816), the required child development knowledge (§ 1815(a)(6)), or the required California court procedure knowledge (§ 1815(a)(3)). Challenging only the degree requirement while missing the other six qualifications is incomplete.

**Trap:** Courts sometimes accept a counselor's general resume as demonstrating § 1815 compliance without verifying each of the seven prongs. The party challenging compliance must go through each of the seven statutory requirements separately and demand evidence of compliance with each.

### 3. Assuming the § 1815(b) Substitution Permits Complete Waiver

Section 1815(b) allows the conciliation court to "substitute additional experience for a portion of the education, or additional education for a portion of the experience." This is a proportional substitution — not an elimination. A mediator with no master's degree cannot substitute experience alone. A mediator with no counseling experience cannot substitute an additional degree alone. Parties often miss this when opponents claim that "extensive experience" compensates for missing credentials.

### 4. Missing the Post-2006 DV Training Requirement

The 2006 amendment (effective January 1, 2007) added § 1815(a)(7), requiring DV training per § 1816. Any mediator appointed after January 1, 2007 who lacks § 1816 DV training does not satisfy § 1815. This includes mediators who received earlier training that predates § 1816's specific requirements. The training must meet § 1816's current standards, not a general DV awareness standard.

### 5. Relying on the Mediator's Self-Certification

Courts and parties frequently accept a mediator's representation of credentials without independent verification. The statutory obligation is the mediator's — and the court's. But the practical protection is the litigant's: demand documentation, not assertion. A mediator who cannot produce a copy of the relevant master's degree diploma, transcripts, and documented DV training has not established compliance with § 1815.

### 6. Failing to Pursue a Credential-Based Challenge After the Fact

A party who discovers post-mediation that the mediator was unqualified has options: (a) motion for reconsideration if within the reconsideration window; (b) motion to set aside the judgment under Code of Civil Procedure § 473 if the qualification defect constitutes a mistake or fraud on the court; (c) appeal; or (d) motion to modify custody under Montenegro v. Diaz, arguing the underlying order was not preceded by a valid best-interest determination. The discovery of unqualified mediator credentials does not forever extinguish the challenge — it depends on when discovery was made and what procedural vehicle is available.

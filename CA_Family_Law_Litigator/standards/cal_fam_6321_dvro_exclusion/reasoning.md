# Reasoning — Cal. Fam. Code § 6321

## How Courts Apply This Standard

### 1. Two-Part Conjunctive Test — Both Elements Must Be Met
Courts apply § 6321 as a conjunctive two-part test. Both (a)(1) — harm to the protected party or child — and (a)(2) — assault, threatened assault, or credible threat — must be established. A showing of harm alone without a showing of assault or credible threat does not support exclusion. Courts frequently reverse exclusion orders granted without adequate evidence of both prongs.

### 2. "Credible Threat" Standard Is Objective, Not Subjective
A credible threat under § 6321(a)(2)(B) is one that a reasonable person would believe could be carried out. The threatening party's stated intent is relevant but not controlling. Courts look at: the nature of the threat; the parties' history; any prior acts of violence; whether the threatening party had the means and opportunity to carry out the threat. A vague or generalized allegation of fear without specific threatening conduct does not meet the standard.

### 3. Ex Parte Nature Creates Due Process Tension
§ 6321 orders are ex parte — issued without notice to the excluded party. Courts apply a heightened scrutiny on review: did the moving party show "immediate and irreparable injury, loss, or damage"? Orders issued on conclusory allegations without specific facts are vacated on review. The excluded party's remedy is an OSC hearing where they can contest the factual basis.

### 4. The Exclusion Order Does Not Determine Property Rights (§ 6321(b))
Courts are explicit that an exclusion order is not a determination of who owns or has the superior right to possess the property. Parties who conflate the exclusion order with property adjudication are corrected. The order is a temporary safety measure; property rights are litigated separately. This is critical where the excluded party has an ownership interest in the home.

### 5. Right to Possession as Background Condition
The excluded party must have some legal basis for being present (ownership, lease, marital right of occupancy) for the exclusion order to be necessary. Courts note this as an implied threshold: excluding someone from a place they have no right to be present is an injunction, not a § 6321 exclusion. The standard applies where the to-be-excluded party has a cognizable possessory interest.

### 6. Emotional Harm Is Sufficient for § 6321(a)(1)
The statute protects against both physical AND emotional harm. Courts have upheld exclusion orders where the moving party showed significant emotional harm to the children or themselves, even where no physical violence occurred. Emotional harm from sustained psychological abuse, harassment, or intimidation is a valid basis.

## Practical Test
To contest a § 6321 exclusion order: (1) challenge the evidence on prong (a)(1) — was harm actually shown or just alleged? (2) challenge prong (a)(2) — was there an actual assault, credible threat, or only a disputed allegation? (3) raise § 6321(b) to counter any suggestion the order affects property rights. At the OSC hearing, present counter-evidence on both prongs. Move to dissolve if the factual basis is fabricated.

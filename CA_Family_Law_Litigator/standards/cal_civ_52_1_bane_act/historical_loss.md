## How Parties Lose Under This Standard

### 1. Plaintiffs Lose: Cannot Show the Coercion Element

The most common failure: the plaintiff proves a right was interfered with but cannot connect the interference to threat, intimidation, or coercion. If the defendant's conduct was lawful — even if it had the effect of chilling a right — the Bane Act claim fails. Courts are strict: there must be an affirmative coercive act, not just the incidental effect of lawful conduct on rights.

### 2. Plaintiffs Lose: Relying on Speech Alone Without the Three Kicker Elements

A plaintiff who can only show that the defendant made threatening statements without showing (a) violence threatened, (b) reasonable fear of violence, and (c) apparent ability to carry through, cannot survive a § 52.1(k) challenge. Threatening communications that do not rise to this level — even if alarming and harmful — are not Bane Act violations standing alone.

### 3. Plaintiffs Lose: Filing Only in Federal Court and Missing State Law Analysis

Plaintiffs sometimes bring § 1983 claims in federal court and ignore the parallel Bane Act claim. They lose the triple damages, the $25,000 per-violation civil penalty, and California's broader rights framework. The Bane Act is often the stronger vehicle in state court and should not be bypassed.

### 4. Defendants (Law Enforcement) Lose: Attempting Government Code Immunity Defense

Officers and agencies sometimes argue Government Code §§ 821.6 or 844.6 immunity to defeat Bane Act claims. Courts reject this: § 52.1(n) expressly strips those immunities for peace officers. Agencies that advise their officers that governmental immunity protects them from Bane Act liability are giving incorrect legal guidance.

### 5. Plaintiffs Lose: Not Tying Private Conduct to a Specific Right

A broad complaint that the defendant was abusive or harassing does not satisfy the Bane Act. The plaintiff must identify the specific constitutional or statutory right that was interfered with. "I was mistreated" is not a Bane Act claim. "I was prevented from exercising my Family Code § 3100 custody access right by threatening conduct" is.

### 6. Live Failure Patterns in Steward's Cases

**APD non-enforcement pattern:** When APD officers failed to enforce mandatory DV arrest requirements and thereby allowed the restrained party to continue conduct that chilled Michael's rights (custody access, physical safety, communication), each enforcement failure coupled with an affirmative false report may satisfy the coercion element — the false reports are the coercive act that interfered with rights.

**SIM swap / communication fraud:** The coordinated blocking of Michael's phone access — cutting off medical appointments, legal services, disability applications — using what appears to have been organized SIM-swap and impersonation conduct interfered with multiple constitutional and statutory rights (medical access, equal protection, communication rights). The coercion element is the SIM swap activity itself plus the ongoing threat of re-swapping if Michael sought to restore access.

**False DVRO procurement:** Obtaining a DVRO through fabricated evidence is itself the coercive act. It interferes with family integrity rights (custody), due process rights, and movement rights. This is a textbook Bane Act claim against private actors — no color of law needed.

**Status: PROPOSED**

## How Courts Apply This Standard

### 1. Two-Element Test: Interference + Threat/Intimidation/Coercion

The Bane Act requires proof of two things: (1) the defendant interfered with, or attempted to interfere with, a constitutional or statutory right; and (2) the interference was accomplished by threat, intimidation, or coercion. Both elements must be present. A legitimate law enforcement action that happens to affect rights is not automatically a Bane Act violation — the coercive element is what distinguishes constitutional torts from proper state action.

Courts apply a broad definition of "threat, intimidation, or coercion." After B.B. v. County of Los Angeles (2020), the California Supreme Court held that the coercion element in a law enforcement context is met when the officer's conduct goes beyond what is necessary to accomplish a legitimate law enforcement purpose and is intended to — or has the effect of — deterring the exercise of rights.

### 2. No Color of Law Requirement — Private Defendants Are Liable

Unlike 42 U.S.C. § 1983, the Bane Act does not require the defendant to act under color of law. Private individuals and organizations can violate § 52.1. This makes the Bane Act available against non-governmental actors who interfere with rights through threats or coercion — including individuals who orchestrate harassment, who make false statements to obtain court orders, or who manipulate law enforcement to interfere with another's rights.

### 3. Rights Protected: Broad Category

The Bane Act covers any right secured by the U.S. Constitution, any federal statute, the California Constitution, or California law. This includes: Fourth Amendment rights (unreasonable search/seizure), Fourteenth Amendment rights (due process, equal protection), First Amendment rights (speech, association, family integrity), Family Code rights (custody, access), and Privacy rights under Art. I § 1 of the California Constitution.

### 4. The Speech-Only Exception (§ 52.1(k))

Pure speech — threats expressed verbally or in writing — does not trigger the Bane Act unless the plaintiff can show: (a) the speech threatened violence against a specific person; (b) that person reasonably feared violence; and (c) the speaker had the apparent ability to carry out the threat. This exception is narrowly construed. Courts hold that a series of intimidating acts that includes speech does not become "speech alone" just because some of the conduct was verbal.

### 5. Government Immunity Is Stripped for Peace Officers (§ 52.1(n))

Three of the most commonly invoked public entity immunities — Government Code §§ 821.6 (prosecution immunity), 844.6 (prisoner liability immunity), and 845.6 (failure to provide medical care immunity) — are expressly inapplicable to § 52.1 claims against peace officers. This means officers cannot hide behind institutional immunity when they use coercive conduct to interfere with rights.

### 6. Damages: Triple + Penalty + Fees

Section 52(b) damages are available: actual damages, up to three times actual damages, a $25,000 civil penalty per violation, and attorney's fees. This makes the Bane Act a more powerful vehicle than § 1983 in California state court where § 1988 fee-shifting may be harder to obtain.

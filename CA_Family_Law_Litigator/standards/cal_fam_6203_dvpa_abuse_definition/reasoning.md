# Family Code § 6203 — Judicial Reasoning and Doctrinal Application

## Overview

Section 6203 is the DVPA's definitional hub. Its four categories in § 6203(a) define "abuse" for purposes of the entire DVPA, including DVRO eligibility, custody presumptions under § 3046, and the best-interest factor under § 3011(a)(2)(B). The critical provision is § 6203(a)(4): it makes any § 6320-enjoinable conduct automatically "abuse" under the DVPA — including the post-SB 1141 coercive control definition. Section 6203(b) is an express non-limitation clause: courts cannot require physical injury to find abuse.

## Key Doctrinal Points

### 1. The § 6203(a)(4) → § 6320 Loop Is the Coercive Control Gateway

Section 6203(a)(4) defines abuse as "any behavior that has been or could be enjoined pursuant to Section 6320." This creates a direct statutory link: coercive control conduct under § 6320(c) is automatically "abuse" under § 6203. Before SB 1141 (2021), this loop covered the § 6320(a) enumerated behaviors. After SB 1141, it encompasses the full coercive control definition in § 6320(c), including: isolation (§ 6320(c)(1)), deprivation of necessities (§ 6320(c)(2)), controlling movements/communications/finances (§ 6320(c)(3)), compelling by force or threat (§ 6320(c)(4)), and reproductive coercion (§ 6320(c)(5)).

This means a systematic pattern of SIM swap fraud (interfering with communications), financial fraud (depriving economic resources), and coordinated law enforcement contacts (compelling behavior through state-actor threat) is "abuse" under § 6203 — not just bad conduct.

### 2. Section 6203(b) — Physical Injury Is Not Required

The Legislature expressly stated: "Abuse is not limited to the actual infliction of physical injury or assault." Courts that require physical evidence of injury before finding "abuse" under the DVPA are misapplying § 6203(b). The non-limitation clause is categorical and has been applied by California courts consistently since the DVPA's enactment.

Nakamura v. Parker (2007) confirmed this principle in the DVRO context: a petitioner who has not suffered physical injury may still establish "abuse" under § 6203 based on § 6203(a)(3) (reasonable apprehension of imminent serious bodily injury) or § 6203(a)(4) (§ 6320-enjoinable conduct).

### 3. The § 6203(a)(3) Reasonable Apprehension Standard — Objective, Not Subjective

Section 6203(a)(3) defines abuse as placing a person "in reasonable apprehension of imminent serious bodily injury." The test is objective: would a reasonable person in the petitioner's position have experienced reasonable apprehension? Courts apply this objectively — the petitioner's subjective belief is relevant but not controlling. The "imminent" requirement has been interpreted broadly: it does not require an immediate physical confrontation if the context and history create reasonable apprehension.

### 4. The § 6203 Definition Governs the § 3011(a)(2)(B) Abuse Factor

Section 3011(a)(2)(B) defines "abuse against a person" (other than a child) by reference to § 6203. This means the broad DVPA abuse definition — including § 6203(a)(4)'s coercive control reach — is the operative definition for the § 3011 best-interest analysis. A history of coercive control satisfies § 3011(a)(2)'s abuse-history factor even without a prior DVRO or criminal conviction.

### 5. The § 6203 Definition Also Governs § 3046's Rebuttable Presumption

Family Code § 3046 creates a rebuttable presumption that awarding custody to a person who has "perpetrated domestic violence" is detrimental to the child. "Perpetrated domestic violence" is defined by reference to § 6211 and § 6203. A person who has committed § 6203 abuse — including § 6203(a)(4) coercive control — triggers the § 3046 presumption. The presumption shifts the burden to the abuser to prove custody is in the child's best interest.

### 6. Perez v. Torres-Hernandez — Non-Physical Abuse Sufficient for DVRO

Perez v. Torres-Hernandez (2016) 1 Cal.App.5th 389 held that the totality of non-physical conduct — verbal threats, surveillance, controlling behavior — established § 6203 abuse sufficient for DVRO issuance. The court applied § 6203(b) to reject the respondent's argument that absence of physical violence defeated the petition.

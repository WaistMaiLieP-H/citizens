# Family Code § 6203 — How Parties Lose

## Failure Modes — Petitioners

### 1. Failing to Invoke § 6203(a)(4) for Non-Physical Coercive Control

The most commonly missed provision: § 6203(a)(4). Petitioners who lead with physical injury claims and omit the § 6203(a)(4)/§ 6320 coercive control theory leave out the category most applicable to pattern-based abuse. Courts trained on physical-injury analysis may require prompting to apply § 6203(a)(4). The petitioner must explicitly state: "The conduct described constitutes abuse under Family Code § 6203(a)(4) because it constitutes behavior enjoinable under § 6320, including coercive control as defined in § 6320(c)."

**Steward relevance:** A pattern of SIM swap fraud (§ 6320(c)(3) — controlling communications), coordinated law enforcement contacts, financial fraud (§ 6320(c)(3) — controlling economic resources), and isolation from support networks (§ 6320(c)(1)) is § 6203(a)(4) abuse even absent any physical violence.

### 2. Failing to Present Pattern Evidence in Chronological Form

Section 6203(a)(4) incorporates § 6320's coercive control definition, which requires "a pattern of behavior." Courts must see the pattern. A petitioner who presents isolated incidents in a disconnected narrative fails to establish the pattern. The evidence must be organized chronologically, showing: (a) what conduct occurred; (b) when; (c) the cumulative effect on the petitioner's freedom of movement, communications, finances, and personal liberty.

### 3. Not Leveraging § 6203(b) When Opposing Counsel Argues "No Physical Injury"

When a respondent argues there was no physical violence so no "abuse" occurred, the response is § 6203(b): "Abuse is not limited to the actual infliction of physical injury or assault." This is explicit statutory language. Courts that sustain a "no physical injury" argument against a DVPA claim are misapplying § 6203(b). The objection must be specific and immediate: "Counsel is misstating the law. Family Code § 6203(b) expressly provides that abuse is not limited to physical injury."

### 4. Failing to Cross-Reference § 6203 Abuse into the § 3011 Best-Interest Analysis

A party who establishes § 6203 abuse in a DVRO proceeding but fails to carry that finding into the concurrent custody proceeding under § 3011 leaves a critical connection unmade. Section 3011(a)(2)(B) defines "abuse against a person" by reference to § 6203. A § 6203 finding in a DVRO case is directly usable as the § 3011(a)(2) "history of abuse" factor in the custody case. Courts do not automatically make this connection — the party must argue it.

### 5. Failing to Connect § 6203 Abuse to § 3046's Rebuttable Presumption

When § 6203 abuse is established, § 3046's rebuttable presumption against awarding custody to the abuser activates. Courts sometimes treat § 3046 as a separate burden requiring separate proof. The correct argument is that the § 6203 finding in the DVRO proceeding satisfies the § 3046 predicate, shifting the burden to the abuser to rebut.

## Failure Modes — Respondents

### 6. Failing to Challenge the § 6203(a)(4) Theory on Specificity Grounds

A respondent facing a § 6203(a)(4) coercive control claim must challenge the specificity of the § 6320(c) pattern allegations. The statute requires a "pattern of behavior" — not a single incident. A respondent can contest: (a) whether the specific conduct described meets any of the § 6320(c)(1)-(5) examples; (b) whether the conduct was "unreasonable"; (c) whether individual incidents were isolated rather than a pattern. These challenges must be specific — general denial of "ever being controlling" is insufficient.

### 7. Not Challenging Unauthenticated Documentary Evidence

Police reports, messages, and other documents submitted to prove § 6203 abuse must be authenticated. An unsigned police report (e.g., APD Report #23-004494) submitted to prove a § 6203(a)(1) or (a)(3) assault or threat allegation fails authentication under Evidence Code § 1401. The respondent must object at the evidentiary hearing; failure to object waives the authentication requirement.

### 8. The § 6203(a)(3) "Reasonable Apprehension" Standard Is Objective — Attack the Reasonableness

The § 6203(a)(3) standard is whether the petitioner was placed in "reasonable apprehension." This is an objective standard the respondent can attack: what would a reasonable person have experienced? If the surrounding context does not support reasonable apprehension — if, for example, the only "threatening" conduct was verbal expression of frustration with no history of violence — the respondent can challenge the objective reasonableness of the claimed apprehension with contextual evidence.

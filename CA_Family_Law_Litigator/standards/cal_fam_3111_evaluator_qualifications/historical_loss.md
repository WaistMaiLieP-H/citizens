# Family Code § 3111 — Historical Loss

## Why the consideration bar was written the way it was

The pre-1993 custody-evaluation provisions were scattered across the former Civil Code and operated on permissive-admission language: trial courts could admit evaluator reports over objection and "weigh" defects as they saw fit. The Legislature repeatedly heard that this framework was producing a predictable pattern of harm. Evaluators with thin qualifications were being appointed; reports were being filed late or not served at all on affected parties; and trial courts were treating non-compliance as a go-to-weight issue rather than an admissibility issue. The 1993 Family Code recodification (Stats. 1993, Ch. 219, Sec. 116.81) moved the provisions into Chapter 6 and tightened the standards-compliance language. Subsequent amendments — most recently AB 1817 (Stats. 2019, Ch. 115, Sec. 34) — rewrote the gate into its current mandatory form: the report **may be considered only if** it complies with § 3117 standards.

The historical loss that drove that change is the loss of the structural safeguard. When a trial court is permitted to admit a non-compliant report and then weigh the defect, the defect becomes invisible on appeal because the record only shows the finding, not the methodology that produced it. The consideration bar was written to move the challenge upstream — to the admissibility gate — where it is visible, reviewable, and structural.

## Failure modes the statute is trying to prevent

### 1. Out-of-jurisdiction appointment

An evaluator whose primary license is in another state, whose California practice addresses are all in a different county, being appointed to evaluate in a county where the evaluator has no established practice footprint. The failure here compounds: (a) rule 5.225 qualifications become harder to verify; (b) Bus. & Prof. Code § 2290.5 telehealth-scope rules may be implicated if the evaluation was conducted remotely; (c) Evid. § 720 foundation is weaker because the evaluator lacks the local context that evaluators with established county practices develop. The NPPES registry surfaces these mismatches through primary-license state, taxonomy, and listed-practice-address fields.

### 2. Consideration-bar erasure

Trial courts admitting non-compliant reports and then treating the defect as weight. The statute's mandatory "may be considered only if" language was written to foreclose this exact move. When a party fails to object at the consideration-bar stage, the objection is often deemed waived on appeal; when a party does object and the trial court treats the objection as a weight matter, the order becomes reversible error — but only if the objection is preserved on the record with the statutory language.

### 3. Service-rule collapse

The 10-day service rule in § 3111(a) is frequently honored in the breach. Late service denies the responding party an opportunity to prepare cross-examination, retain a rebuttal expert, or file a motion to strike. The statute's answer is not a continuance — it is a consideration bar. A report not served 10 days before the hearing has not met the statutory conditions for consideration.

### 4. Confidentiality-regime collapse

Subdivisions (b), (d), (e), and (f) are a closed-list confidentiality regime with monetary sanctions for breach. When the § 3111(e) confidentiality form is omitted, when copies are disseminated outside the statutory channels, or when opposing counsel distributes the report beyond the permitted recipients, the regime breaks down. Sanctions under (d) are the statutory remedy; motions under (d) are rarely brought but structurally available.

### 5. § 3044(e) delegation cascade

Where a trial court relies on an evaluator's report to make a DV-perpetration finding, § 3044(e) independently prohibits basing the finding "solely on conclusions reached by a child custody evaluator." § 3111 non-compliance means the evaluator's report cannot be considered at all; § 3044(e) means that even a compliant report cannot carry a DV finding alone. A trial court that issues a § 3044 finding on the strength of an evaluator's recommendation without independent factfinding has committed two structurally separate errors.

### 6. Expert-witness foundation gap

§ 3111 compliance establishes that the report may be considered. It does not establish the evaluator's qualifications as an expert on every issue addressed. Evidence Code § 720 remains a live challenge. An evaluator qualified under rule 5.225 for general custody evaluation is not automatically qualified as a forensic psychologist on competency, mental-health diagnosis, substance-use assessment, or any specialty topic the report touches. The foundation attack is separate from and additional to the § 3111 challenge.

## What the § 3111 gate requires on the record

For a report to pass the § 3111 gate, the record should reflect — at minimum — each of the following:

1. An appointment order with a best-interest finding (§ 3111(a)).
2. Evaluator qualifications satisfying rule 5.225 (§ 3110.5 / § 3117).
3. Methodology satisfying rule 5.220 (§ 3117).
4. Filing with the clerk and service on parties / minor's counsel at least 10 days before the hearing (§ 3111(a)).
5. The § 3111(e) confidentiality form served with the report.
6. Dissemination limited to the statutory channels (§ 3111(b)).

Absent any one of these, the consideration bar is not satisfied.

# Family Code §§ 3150-3153 — Reasoning

## What Chapter 10 is doing in the statutory architecture

Chapter 10 [§§ 3150-3153] creates a structural role inside the custody proceeding that is neither party-allied nor court-adjunct: **minor's counsel.** The statute places the child's interests into a seat that has its own evidentiary reach, its own procedural rights, and its own compensation mechanism. Four sections work together:

- **§ 3150** — appointment authority and continuing-representation rule.
- **§ 3151** — best-interest representation role, duties, witness bar, and eight enumerated rights.
- **§ 3152** — CPS records access through noticed motion and in camera review.
- **§ 3153** — compensation (party-paid default; county-paid where parties financially unable).

The chapter's theory: a contested custody proceeding is adversarial between the parents (or between a parent and a third party under § 3041). Without a separate advocate for the child, the child's interests collapse into the parties' rhetorical framings. Chapter 10 gives the child a dedicated voice that can (a) gather evidence the parties may not surface, (b) represent the child's wishes where the child desires, (c) access records the parents may dispute, and (d) litigate as a party-equivalent within the proceeding.

## Why "best interest" representation differs from strict client-directed representation

§ 3151(a) uses the phrase "representation of the child's best interests" — not "representation of the child as client." This is deliberate. In conventional attorney-client practice, counsel follows the client's direction on objectives. In Chapter 10 practice, minor's counsel exercises professional judgment about what serves the child, informed by but not strictly bound to the child's expressed preferences. The child's wishes are reported to the court ("If the child so desires, the child's counsel shall present the child's wishes to the court"), but they do not necessarily control the litigation position.

Cal. Rules of Court rule 5.242 — which § 3150(a) incorporates by reference — fills in the professional-standards detail. Minor's counsel under the California regime has continuing-education, disciplinary-complaint, and written-report obligations that do not apply to ordinary family-law counsel.

## The witness bar in § 3151(b) and why it matters

The statute flatly says: **"Counsel shall not be called as a witness in the proceeding."** This is not a protective privilege assertion that counsel can waive; it is a structural bar. Its function is to preserve the advocacy role. If minor's counsel could be called as a witness, the adverse party could use the subpoena power to extract counsel's mental impressions, investigative steps, and conversations with the child — effectively destroying the independent-advocate role the chapter creates.

Trial courts that permit minor's counsel to be examined on the stand, or that rely on minor's counsel's personal knowledge as evidence, violate § 3151(b). Counsel's role is to *gather and present admissible evidence* — not to be that evidence.

## The records-access architecture in § 3151(c)(5) and § 3152

Minor's counsel has direct statutory access to:

- Medical, dental, mental health, and other health-care records of the child.
- School and educational records.
- Interview rights with school personnel, caretakers, health care providers, mental health professionals, and others who have assessed or cared for the child.
- CPS records — but only through the § 3152 noticed-motion + in camera review procedure.

Release to minor's counsel **does not waive confidentiality**. That is stated twice — once in § 3151(c)(5) ("The release of this information to counsel shall not constitute a waiver of the confidentiality of the reports, files, and any disclosed communications") and once in § 3152(c) ("Neither the review by the court nor the release to counsel shall constitute a waiver of the confidentiality of the reports and files").

This double non-waiver is structurally important. It means records that reach minor's counsel do not thereby become available to the parties or to the court's public file. The child's confidentiality survives the investigative process.

## Privilege assertion in § 3151(c)(7)

**"The right to assert or waive any privilege on behalf of the child."**

This is a consequential power. Minor's counsel — not the parent — decides whether to assert or waive the child's psychotherapist-patient privilege, the child's physician-patient privilege (where applicable), or other child-held privileges. In contested custody cases where a parent is trying to force disclosure of the child's therapy or medical records, minor's counsel's assertion of privilege is generally dispositive. Conversely, where the child's records would assist the custody analysis and the child (or counsel on the child's behalf) determines waiver serves the child, counsel can waive.

## Examination and evaluation veto in § 3151(c)(6)

**"The right to reasonable advance notice of and the right to refuse any physical or psychological examination or evaluation, for purposes of the proceeding, which has not been ordered by the court."**

The child is not subject to *ex parte* examination by a party's hired expert. Minor's counsel can refuse any non-court-ordered evaluation. This backs up § 3111's court-appointed-evaluator framework: parties cannot do an end-run around § 3111 by retaining a private expert and asking the child to cooperate. If the examination is not court-ordered, minor's counsel can refuse on the child's behalf.

## Compensation structure in § 3153

§ 3153(a) defaults to party-paid fees, apportioned as the court deems just. This is the statutory answer to the "who pays for the child's lawyer" question. § 3153(b) is the county safety valve: when *both parties together* cannot pay, the county pays the uncovered portion. The Judicial Council's financial-eligibility guidelines supply the operational mechanism.

For a party who would benefit from minor's-counsel appointment but cannot fund it, the § 3153(b) motion is the path. The court determines county payment on its own motion or on a party's motion. A party's motion that establishes financial inability (both sides) shifts the cost to the county and does not leave the appointment financially foreclosed.

## How Chapter 10 interacts with § 3044, § 3111, and § 3041

- **§ 3044 interaction.** In a § 3044 rebuttal contest, minor's counsel can present evidence about the child's experience and preferences that neither parent would fully surface. § 3044(e) prohibits the court from basing a perpetration finding "solely on conclusions reached by a child custody evaluator." Minor's counsel supplies an independent evidentiary channel that is not the evaluator channel and is therefore not reached by § 3044(e)'s delegation rule — though counsel's evidence must still be admissible and developed through the proper foundation.

- **§ 3111 interaction.** § 3111(a) requires the evaluator's report to be served on "any other counsel appointed for the child pursuant to Section 3150." Minor's counsel has standing to challenge the evaluator's qualifications, methodology, and service compliance on the child's behalf — and often has better access to the child's historical records than either parent, which strengthens the Track A (qualifications) and Track B (procedure) attacks described in the § 3111 standard.

- **§ 3041 interaction.** In a nonparent-custody dispute, minor's counsel's representation of the child's *interests* is analytically distinct from either the parent's or the nonparent's position. The § 3041(a) "required to serve the best interest" finding is directly informed by minor's counsel's investigation and presentation. Where the proposed nonparent custodian is a state-actor, minor's counsel's independent evidentiary channel helps establish what serves the child apart from either party's institutional framing.

## What Chapter 10 gives a parent (or pro se litigant) seeking appointment

A party who believes the child's interests are being underrepresented can **move for appointment** under § 3150. The motion requires a showing that appointment would be in the child's best interest. Typical supporting facts: high-conflict proceeding, allegations of parental alienation from either side, disputed evaluator reports, child's expressed wishes being filtered through the parties, medical or mental-health complexity that the parties' counsel is not equipped to address.

Where the moving party lacks resources, the motion should include a § 3153(b) request for county payment if both parties together cannot fund counsel. The appointment question (§ 3150) and the payment question (§ 3153) are separable — the court can appoint and order payment in a single order.

# Family Code §§ 3150-3153 — Historical Loss

## Enactment history

Chapter 10 was enacted by Stats. 1992, Ch. 162, Sec. 10 — part of the 1993 Family Code recodification — and became operative January 1, 1994. §§ 3150 and 3151 were most recently amended by Stats. 2010, Ch. 352 (AB 939), effective January 1, 2011. §§ 3152 and 3153 remain as originally enacted.

The pre-1994 framework gave trial courts broad discretion to appoint counsel for children under former Civil Code provisions and *in re Jay R.* / *Marriage of S.* case law, but provided no statutory structure: no enumerated duties, no witness-bar rule, no records-access rights, no CPS-records procedure, no compensation mechanism. Courts appointed counsel unevenly; counsel's role varied by county and by judge.

Chapter 10 replaced the discretionary mess with a structured framework: (a) appointment keyed to best interest and tied to Cal. Rules of Court 5.240-5.242; (b) explicit enumerated rights and duties; (c) a statutory witness bar; (d) CPS-records access through in camera review; (e) a compensation structure that includes county-pay fallback.

## What the chapter was trying to prevent

### 1. The invisible child in a two-party custody fight

Before Chapter 10, contested custody cases operated as binary contests between parents (or between a parent and a third party) with the child's interests surfaced only indirectly — through evaluator reports, FCS recommendations, or the trial court's intuition. The child had no dedicated advocate, no independent evidentiary channel, and no standing. Chapter 10 created the standing (§ 3151(c)(2)) and supplied the investigative and advocacy tools (§ 3151(a) duties, § 3151(c) rights).

### 2. Discovery of the child as evidentiary target

Prior practice allowed parties to depose children, compel psychological evaluations, and subpoena school and medical records in aid of the custody fight. Chapter 10 changed this by giving minor's counsel control over key gates:

- § 3151(c)(5) routes the child's records through counsel (without waiving confidentiality).
- § 3151(c)(6) gives counsel a veto over non-court-ordered examinations.
- § 3151(c)(7) places privilege assertion in counsel's hands, not the parents'.

The child stops being raw material for the custody fight and becomes a represented party with gatekept information flow.

### 3. Attorney-as-witness collapse

Without a statutory witness bar, parties routinely tried to call opposing counsel, court-appointed counsel, or mediators to the stand to extract statements or explore their reasoning. § 3151(b) forecloses this for minor's counsel: "Counsel shall not be called as a witness in the proceeding." The bar protects the advocacy role from being destroyed through the subpoena power.

### 4. CPS records lockout

CPS records are statutorily confidential. Before § 3152, minor's counsel could not access them reliably; confidentiality was treated as absolute. § 3152 creates a narrow pathway: noticed motion, in camera judicial review, selective release, no waiver of confidentiality. This gives counsel the tool to evaluate CPS history without destroying the statutory confidentiality regime.

### 5. Appointment financially foreclosed

§ 3153(b)'s county-payment fallback addresses the failure mode where parents could not fund counsel and appointment therefore was not made — even where the child's interests clearly needed representation. The county-payment safety valve keeps the appointment question from collapsing into the payment question.

### 6. Uneven minor's-counsel quality

Cal. Rules of Court rule 5.242 (incorporated through § 3150(a)) imposes qualification, training, and continuing-education requirements on minor's counsel. Counsel who lack rule 5.242 qualifications are not eligible for appointment. This is a structural check against the pre-1994 practice of appointing any available attorney without regard to family-law experience or child-advocate training.

## Structural failure modes the chapter does not fully foreclose

### 7. Non-appointment drift

The appointment is discretionary under § 3150(a) — "the court *may* appoint." In high-conflict cases where appointment would clearly serve the child but no party moves for it, the court may simply not appoint. The statute does not require appointment; it authorizes it. A parent whose child would benefit from minor's counsel should move under § 3150 affirmatively rather than wait for the court to act *sua sponte*.

### 8. Counsel-as-evaluator-echo

Minor's counsel is supposed to present admissible evidence — not to be an evidence source. Where counsel's statements to the court begin to function as de facto evaluator conclusions ("in my view, the child should reside with parent A"), the § 3151(b) witness bar is being eroded from the other direction: counsel becomes an opinion source without being qualified under Evid. § 720 and without cross-examination availability. This drift undercuts both § 3151(b) and § 3044(e). Parties and the court should police the distinction between admissible-evidence presentation and implicit expert opinion.

### 9. Privilege-waiver leveraging

§ 3151(c)(7) gives counsel the authority to assert or waive any privilege on the child's behalf. Where counsel is institutionally pressured or not adequately independent, privilege waiver can become a pathway for records to enter the custody record that would otherwise be confidential. The remedy is vigilance about counsel's independence and, where appropriate, motion practice to test counsel's decisions.

### 10. County-payment delay

§ 3153(b) requires a determination of financial inability. In practice, this determination can lag behind the appointment decision, leaving counsel uncompensated for long stretches and therefore reluctant to accept the appointment. A party seeking appointment for a financially-strained case should prepare a § 3153(b) motion simultaneously with the § 3150 motion, with supporting financial declarations.

## What the record must show for Chapter 10 compliance

For a Chapter 10 appointment to function as the statute contemplates:

1. An appointment order with a best-interest finding (§ 3150(a)).
2. Counsel's qualifications satisfying Cal. Rules of Court rule 5.242.
3. Counsel's duties exercised: child interview, records review, investigation (§ 3151(a)).
4. Counsel's advocacy preserved: service and pleading equivalent to a party, witness bar respected (§ 3151(b)).
5. CPS records accessed through § 3152 procedure where relevant, with confidentiality preserved.
6. Compensation addressed under § 3153 — party-paid or county-paid.

Where any one is missing, the appointment has not delivered what Chapter 10 promises and the child's representation is structurally deficient.

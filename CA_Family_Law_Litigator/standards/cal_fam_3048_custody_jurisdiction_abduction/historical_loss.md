# Family Code § 3048 — Layer 3: Historical Loss

## Pre-§ 3048

Before enactment, California custody orders varied wildly in form. Many orders — especially stipulated ones — lacked jurisdictional recitals, notice recitals, or habitual-residence findings. When these orders were presented to sister states (UCCJEA enforcement) or to foreign courts under the Hague Convention, they were often refused or required supplemental findings months or years later — during which time the abducting parent had the child.

## UCCJEA and Hague drivers

- **1997** — California enacted the UCCJEA (Fam. Code §§ 3400 et seq., Stats. 1999, ch. 867), replacing the UCCJA. UCCJEA recognition requires specific jurisdictional recitals.
- **1988** — U.S. implementation of the 1980 Hague Convention via ICARA (International Child Abduction Remedies Act, 22 U.S.C. §§ 9001–9011). Hague proceedings require a finding of the child's "country of habitual residence."

The substantive policy window (2002–2006) and Uniform Child Abduction Prevention Act framework are corroborated by Judicial Council materials, but specific chaptering requires California State Archives pull.

Most recent amendment: **Stats. 2019, Ch. 115, Sec. 31. (AB 1817)** effective January 1, 2020 (verified from leginfo section-history block).

## Abduction incidents that drove reform

The Hague Conference on Private International Law's periodic statistics on U.S. outgoing and incoming cases showed California as a disproportionately common venue of origin and destination. High-profile California-origin abductions to non-Hague countries (Japan and Saudi Arabia recurring in the early 2000s) pushed the Legislature to require front-end risk evaluation rather than relying on post-abduction remedies that might never work.

# Family Code § 3020 — How Parties Lose

## Failure Modes and Procedural Traps

### 1. Treating § 3020(b) as Trumping § 3020(c)

The most common judicial error — and the most common litigant error in failing to correct it. Courts that treat frequent contact as a near-absolute policy, refusing to reduce contact even when safety evidence is documented, are applying § 3020(b) incorrectly. The statute's internal exception and § 3020(c)'s conflict-resolution rule both require that safety prevail when the two policies conflict. A party who does not aggressively argue § 3020(c) when safety evidence is present allows the court to default to the contact preference.

**Procedure:** When submitting evidence of domestic violence or child abuse, always pair it with an explicit citation to § 3020(a) (legislative declaration of detriment) and § 3020(c) (safety prevails in conflict). Frame the argument as: "The Legislature has declared, in § 3020(a), that domestic violence in the household is detrimental. Under § 3020(c), this court must ensure health, safety, and welfare — not balance safety against contact."

### 2. Failing to Invoke the § 3020(a) Legislative Detriment Finding

Section 3020(a) contains a legislative declaration — not a factual finding the court must make — that DV in the household is detrimental. A party who presents DV evidence but fails to cite this legislative finding is making the court work harder than necessary. The finding is already made by the Legislature. The party's job is to establish that DV occurred; the detriment follows automatically from § 3020(a).

**Steward relevance:** Once any court finds that domestic violence was perpetrated in Cole Hartmann's household — whether by establishing Christina Cerretani's fabrication of APD Report #23-004494, or by establishing the coercive control pattern — § 3020(a)'s detriment finding applies automatically. The court does not need to conduct a separate detriment analysis.

### 3. Allowing Long-Established Contact Patterns to Override Safety Analysis

Courts in long-running custody disputes develop inertia. An arrangement that has existed for years tends to be maintained even when new evidence of harm emerges. Courts cite § 3020(b)'s contact policy to justify this inertia. The correct response is § 3020(c): regardless of the history of contact, current safety evidence triggers the mandatory safety-first rule. In re Marriage of Battenburg established that prior contact arrangements do not immunize a parent from safety-based restrictions when new evidence of risk is present.

**Trap:** In a 16-year custody dispute, courts are particularly resistant to change. The § 3020(c) argument must be made explicitly: the current evidence of domestic violence or coercive control creates a § 3020(a)/(c) conflict with the contact history, and § 3020(c) is not discretionary — it requires the safety-first order.

### 4. Confusing § 3020 with § 3011

Section 3020 is the policy framework; § 3011 is the operative factors test. A party who argues § 3020 without connecting it to the § 3011 enumerated factors misses the statutory chain. The correct structure is: § 3020(a) establishes safety primacy → § 3020(c) resolves conflicts in favor of safety → § 3011(a)(1) requires the court to consider health/safety/welfare → § 3011(a)(2) requires consideration of abuse history → § 3011(a)(5) requires written findings when abuse allegations are present.

### 5. Missing the § 3020(a) Connection to § 3046

Section 3020(a) declares DV in the household to be detrimental. Section 3046 creates a rebuttable presumption against awarding custody to a DV perpetrator. A party who establishes DV but fails to invoke § 3046's presumption has left the strongest tool on the table. The § 3020(a) finding supports and informs the § 3046 presumption. They must be argued together.

### 6. Failing to Preserve the § 3020(d) Non-Discrimination Objection

Section 3020(d) prohibits consideration of sex, gender identity, gender expression, or sexual orientation. Any trial court finding, explicit or implicit, that relies on these characteristics must be objected to at the time of the ruling. Failure to object waives the argument on appeal.

# Family Code §§ 2100-2105 — Historical Loss

## Enactment history

Chapter 9 (§§ 2100-2113) was added to the Family Code by Stats. 1993, Ch. 219, Sec. 107, effective January 1, 1994 as part of the 1993 Family Code recodification that built Division 6 (Nullity, Dissolution, and Legal Separation). Key amendments: § 2100 (2001 Ch. 703), § 2101 (1998 Ch. 581), § 2102 (2019 Ch. 115 AB 1817), § 2103 (2016 Ch. 474 AB 2882), § 2104 (2019 Ch. 115 AB 1817), § 2105 (2001 Ch. 703).

Before Chapter 9, California dissolution practice relied on general discovery rules (then-Code of Civil Procedure §§ 2016 et seq.) and whatever voluntary disclosure the parties or their counsel chose. The combination produced a dispiriting pattern: the controlling spouse (typically the one with superior information, accounting, or financial sophistication) could stonewall through the discovery process, exhaust the less-resourced spouse financially, and secure a settlement or judgment resting on incomplete information. Post-judgment discovery that the controlling spouse had undisclosed assets generated a trickle of appellate remedies but no front-end prevention.

Chapter 9 changed this by making disclosure substantive rather than procedural: a statutory mandate that exists independent of discovery and carries its own set-aside machinery.

## What the chapter was trying to prevent

### 1. Asymmetric-information settlements

Before Chapter 9, a spouse could settle a dissolution based on incomplete information, sign a judgment, and discover post-dissolution that the other spouse had concealed accounts, property, or business interests. Appellate remedies existed but required the deceived spouse to (a) discover the fraud within the statutory set-aside window, (b) prove fraudulent intent, and (c) survive finality-of-judgment doctrines. Many victims never met the burden.

Chapter 9's response: make preliminary and final disclosure affirmative statutory duties, require penalty-of-perjury verification, and link noncompliance to § 2120 set-aside without requiring proof of fraudulent intent (§ 2122(f)).

### 2. The "support calculation as negotiation" problem

Without a sworn income-and-expense requirement, support calculations could devolve into negotiations untethered to verifiable income data. § 2104(e) requires the I&E with the preliminary disclosure; § 2102(c) extends § 721 fiduciary duties to income/expense disclosure for support purposes. A parent who misrepresents income on an I&E is not merely producing a wrong number — they are committing a fiduciary breach and perjury on a sworn statement.

### 3. Post-separation dissipation

Before § 2102's explicit fiduciary-duty-across-the-window framing, a spouse could argue that, once separated, § 721 duties no longer applied — and use the separation-to-distribution period to dissipate, reallocate, or convert community assets. § 2102(a) forecloses this by extending § 721 to every activity affecting the other party's asset or liability position from separation through distribution.

### 4. Nondisclosure of post-separation opportunities

A specific dissipation variant: one spouse receives an investment or business opportunity post-separation that is traceable to pre-separation activity, does not disclose it, and takes the gain as separate property. § 2102(a)(2) requires written disclosure in time for the other spouse to decide whether to participate, and nondisclosure routes any resulting gain to § 2556 (omitted-asset jurisdiction).

### 5. The "we stipulated and it's final" problem

Parties would stipulate to judgments based on cursory disclosure, then argue that the stipulation itself cured any disclosure defects. § 2105(d) responds by making waiver of the final declaration available only through a structured five-representation waiver under penalty of perjury — preventing backdoor stipulations that effectively waive disclosure without the waiver procedure.

### 6. Finality-of-judgment overreach

General finality doctrine (res judicata, merger, etc.) often foreclosed post-judgment remedies for disclosure-based fraud. § 2105(c) partial-set-aside authority specifically rebuts the "unwinding this part breaks the whole judgment" defense by empowering courts to set aside only materially-affected portions. This keeps the set-aside remedy proportional and therefore actually usable.

### 7. Judicial Council form evasion

Before mandated Judicial Council forms, parties could exchange ad hoc documents of wildly varying completeness and format. § 2104(a) and § 2105(a) both mandate Judicial Council forms, standardizing the documentary baseline. Combined with § 2104(c)'s particularity requirement and § 2105(b)'s four-category content mandate, this prevents disclosure-by-bury-in-paper strategies.

### 8. The "you didn't ask" defense

Under discovery rules, a party's duty to supplement is tied to written discovery requests. The disclosing party can defend a non-disclosure by saying "you never asked about that asset." § 2102(a)(1)'s "immediate, full, and accurate update or augmentation" language, combined with the § 721 fiduciary-duty overlay, defeats the you-didn't-ask defense. The duty is affirmative, not responsive.

## Structural failure modes the chapter does not fully foreclose

### 9. The "preliminary served, final waived, nothing disclosed" pattern

A pattern that evades the chapter's enforcement: (a) serve a thin, conclusory preliminary that checks the § 2104(a) box; (b) induce or stipulate to a § 2105(d) final-declaration waiver; (c) settle on the basis of the thin preliminary; (d) assert judgment finality. This pattern relies on the other spouse either not recognizing that the § 2105(d) waiver requirements were not met, or being too financially constrained to contest the waiver. The remedy is vigilance about § 2105(d)(1)-(5) compliance and, post-judgment, § 2122(f) set-aside for Chapter 9 noncompliance.

### 10. The pro-per disclosure deficit

Where one party is represented and the other is in pro per, the represented party's counsel may prepare and serve a technically-conforming preliminary while the pro-per party files a document that substantively fails § 2104(c)'s particularity test (or omits tax returns, or omits I&E). The court's role in policing pro-per disclosure adequacy is limited; the chapter assumes functional parity that frequently does not exist. A pro-per party whose disclosure is deficient can nevertheless bind themselves to the resulting judgment through the perjury exposure — a structural trap.

### 11. Third-party asset concealment

§§ 2100-2113 reach "each party" to the proceeding. Where a spouse has placed assets with a third party (family trust, shell entity, crony holding), the third party is not a Chapter 9 discloser, and reaching the asset requires separate discovery, piercing analysis, or § 2556 omitted-asset proceedings. The chapter's reach stops at the party boundary. For sophisticated hide-the-asset structures, § 1101 fiduciary-breach litigation combined with discovery into third parties is the path — Chapter 9 is necessary but not sufficient.

### 12. Enforcement timeline gap

§ 2122 set-aside motions are subject to timing rules (generally one year from date judgment was entered, or one year from discovery for fraud). A deceived spouse who does not discover the fraud within the window may be foreclosed. The practical mitigation is post-judgment vigilance — continuing review of tax returns, public records, and credit reports — which most deceived spouses do not conduct because they assume the judgment is the end.

### 13. Judicial Council form drift

Judicial Council forms change over time. Forms used in a dissolution filed in 2012 may differ from forms current in 2026. Set-aside analysis requires the form-and-content standard as it existed at the time of the underlying disclosure. This is a discovery-and-research burden on the challenging party that can consume months of work before the set-aside motion can even be filed.

## What the record must show for Chapter 9 compliance

For a dissolution judgment to rest on compliant Chapter 9 disclosure:

1. **Preliminary declaration** — served in § 2104(f) time frame, on current Judicial Council form, under penalty of perjury, including two years of tax returns and I&E, with § 2104(c) particularity. Proof of service filed.
2. **Final declaration** — served before any resolution agreement or at least 45 days before trial, on current Judicial Council form, under penalty of perjury, with § 2105(b)'s four content categories addressed substantively (not conclusorily). Proof of service filed.
3. **Or § 2105(d) mutual waiver** — executed under penalty of perjury with all five enumerated representations, including the (d)(5) acknowledgment that the waiver does not limit obligations and that noncompliance results in set-aside.
4. **§ 2102 continuing-duty compliance** — no material change after preliminary without augmentation; no post-separation opportunity without § 2102(a)(2) written disclosure; no business-operation activity without § 2102(a)(3) fiduciary compliance.
5. **I&E current at judgment** — I&E declaration current and reflecting accurate income/expense information.

Where any one is missing or substantively deficient, § 2122(f) set-aside lies for Chapter 9 noncompliance independent of proof of fraudulent intent. Where there is additional evidence of intentional misrepresentation, § 2122(a) (actual fraud) and § 2122(b) (perjury) both compound the set-aside case.

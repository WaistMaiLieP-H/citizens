# Family Code §§ 2100-2105 — Reasoning

## What Chapter 9 is doing in the statutory architecture

Chapter 9 [§§ 2100-2113] is California's response to a simple structural reality: in marital dissolution, the spouse who controls information controls the outcome. Chapter 9 removes information asymmetry as a litigation weapon by making **full, sworn, documented disclosure** a statutory precondition to any judgment of dissolution.

The chapter does four things:

1. **§ 2100** declares disclosure a substantive public policy, not a discovery convenience.
2. **§ 2102** imports § 721 fiduciary standards and extends them across the entire separation-to-distribution window.
3. **§§ 2103-2105** create the two-declaration mandate (preliminary + final) with penalty-of-perjury verification and Judicial Council form requirements.
4. **§ 2120 et seq. (Chapter 10)** supplies the set-aside remedy — perjury on a disclosure declaration is a **ground for setting aside the judgment**, not merely a discovery violation.

The structure is deliberate: by making every disclosure a sworn document, Chapter 9 converts what would otherwise be a routine discovery dispute into a **perjury and set-aside matter**. That elevation is the enforcement mechanism.

## Why § 2100's policy statement matters operationally

§ 2100 is not boilerplate. Its three-part (a) policy — marshal/preserve community estate, ensure fair support, achieve division — is the interpretive backdrop for every later section. Courts construing §§ 2102-2105 ambiguities have turned to § 2100 for direction, particularly when one spouse argues that a technical-compliance-only reading of the chapter defeats a substantive-disclosure question.

§ 2100(b)'s "reduction of the adversarial nature … by fostering full disclosure and cooperative discovery" phrasing has been used to resolve close calls against technical-noncompliance arguments that would favor the nondisclosing spouse.

§ 2100(c)'s **continuing duty** language is critical. The duty is not "disclose once and done." It is "immediately, fully, and accurately update and augment … to the extent there have been any material changes." A spouse who makes a § 2104 preliminary disclosure and later acquires (or discovers) material information has a self-executing duty to augment.

## Why § 721 import in § 2102 matters

§ 721 establishes fiduciary-duty standards between spouses as to community property, modeled on partnership-confidential-relationship law. § 2102(a) imports these standards across the entire separation-to-distribution timeline for every asset and liability activity.

Three consequences flow:

- **Burden-shifting on disputed transactions.** A spouse who makes an unauthorized transfer, conversion, or use of community property during the § 2102 window bears the burden of showing the transaction was fair — not the non-acting spouse of showing it was unfair.
- **Duty to disclose is affirmative, not responsive.** Fiduciary standards require volunteered disclosure of material facts; the nondisclosing spouse cannot defend by saying "they didn't ask."
- **Business-operation transparency.** § 2102(a)(3) specifically extends the fiduciary standard to business operation and management where the community may have an interest — a common hiding place for post-separation manipulation.

§ 2102(c) extends the same fiduciary framework to support and fees — income and expense disclosure is not a voluntary courtesy but a fiduciary obligation.

## Why both preliminary (§ 2104) and final (§ 2105) are required

The two-declaration structure is not redundant. It is sequential:

- **§ 2104 preliminary** = the **opening inventory**. Served early (with petition/response or within 60 days). Identifies all assets and liabilities with particularity, attaches two years of tax returns, includes percentage ownership and I&E. This establishes the universe of what's on the table.
- **§ 2105 final** = the **closing inventory with valuations**. Served before any resolution agreement or at minimum 45 days before trial. Includes characterization, valuation, amounts of community obligations, and I&E. This establishes what each item is worth and how it is characterized for division.

Between the two declarations, § 2102's continuing duty operates. A spouse whose preliminary was accurate at filing but whose final is not, has violated the continuing duty — not a static-at-one-point-in-time duty.

## The § 2104 particularity requirement

§ 2104(c) requires disclosure "with sufficient particularity, that a person of reasonable and ordinary intelligence can ascertain" the identity of all assets and liabilities. This is a **plain-language particularity standard**, not a pleading-technicality standard.

A preliminary declaration that checks boxes on the Judicial Council form but says "see attached" without attaching substance, or lists "various accounts" without identification, or gives round-number estimates where account statements are available, fails § 2104(c). The test is what a reasonable-intelligence person could *ascertain* — not what a lawyer could reconstruct with extensive follow-up.

## § 2105(b)'s four-category content mandate

§ 2105(b) enumerates the four content categories the final disclosure must address: (1) characterization, (2) valuation of community assets, (3) amounts of community obligations, (4) earnings/accumulations/expenses. A final disclosure missing any one of the four is facially deficient.

The most-frequent deficiency in the steward's 16-year record appears to be (2) valuation — final declarations that characterize community interest but omit current valuations or rely on stale appraisals. § 2105(b)(2) requires "all material facts and information regarding the valuation" — not just characterization. A final disclosure that adequately addresses (1) but fails on (2) is still defective and supports a § 2120 set-aside.

## The § 2105(d) mutual-waiver carve-out is narrow

§ 2105(d) permits mutual waiver of the final declaration — but only under five explicit representations made under penalty of perjury. Any waiver that does not contain all five representations (or contains them in conclusory/checkbox form rather than substantive form) is defective.

Critically, § 2105(d)(5) requires each party to acknowledge **that the waiver does not limit disclosure obligations** and **that noncompliance results in the court setting aside the judgment**. This acknowledgment cannot be implicit; it must be in the waiver document. A waiver missing this acknowledgment is defective on its face.

## Set-aside architecture (§§ 2120-2129 linkage)

§§ 2104(a) and 2105(a) both state: "The commission of perjury on the [PDD/FDD] may be grounds for setting aside the judgment … pursuant to Chapter 10 (commencing with Section 2120)." This dual-channel phrasing is the enforcement hinge:

- **Perjury channel** — traditional perjury prosecution (rare in family court; very rare as a practical matter).
- **Set-aside channel** — § 2120-2129 provides motion-based set-aside remedies; materially incomplete or inaccurate disclosure is the predicate.

The set-aside channel is the practical remedy. § 2122 enumerates grounds for set-aside: actual fraud, perjury, duress, mental incapacity, mistake, or failure to comply with disclosure requirements of Chapter 9. § 2122(f) specifically makes **Chapter 9 noncompliance** a set-aside ground without requiring proof of fraudulent intent.

§ 2105(c) further authorizes **partial set-aside** — the court can set aside "those portions of the judgment materially affected by the nondisclosure." This is important because it removes a strategic defense: the nondisclosing spouse cannot argue "if you set aside this part, everything collapses" to prevent set-aside relief.

## § 2102's post-resolution tail

§ 2102(b) extends § 721 fiduciary duties even **after** a binding resolution is reached, until the specific asset is actually distributed. This closes the "run out the clock" loophole: a spouse who has stipulated to dispose of an asset but still controls it cannot use the intervening period to dissipate or burden it.

This is especially consequential for real property (where deed transfer may lag resolution by months), retirement accounts (QDRO execution delays), and business interests (ownership transfer mechanics). Fiduciary duty persists until the asset is actually distributed, not merely when resolution is reached.

## How §§ 2100-2105 interact with support, custody, and other Family Code regimes

- **Support (§§ 4050 et seq.).** § 2102(c) applies fiduciary standards to income/expense disclosure for support and fees. A parent's income misrepresentation on an I&E is not merely a support-calculation issue but a § 721/§ 2102 fiduciary breach — elevating remedies.
- **Custody (§§ 3011 et seq.).** Disclosure violations bear on custody through (a) the court's assessment of a party's credibility and (b) § 3011(d) weight to history of false statements about child-abuse allegations. A parent who has violated § 2102 fiduciary duties has a credibility problem that attaches to custody-merits testimony.
- **DVPA (§§ 6200 et seq.).** Financial control and economic coercion are forms of abuse under § 6320's "disturbing the peace" language. Systematic § 2102 violations can be both fiduciary breaches (civil remedy via § 1101 and § 2556) and DVPA predicates (restraining order).
- **§ 1101 fiduciary breach.** § 1101 provides the action for breach of spousal fiduciary duty, including the 100%-or-greater undisclosed-asset remedy under § 1101(h) for willful breach. Chapter 9 is the evidentiary runway for a § 1101 claim.
- **§ 2556 omitted-asset jurisdiction.** § 2556 gives the court continuing jurisdiction over community property not divided in the judgment — typically invoked for assets that were undisclosed or unaddressed. § 2102(a)(2) specifically routes nondisclosure of post-separation investment opportunities to § 2556.

## How to use Chapter 9 affirmatively

A party who has been the victim of disclosure noncompliance has several layered paths:

1. **Compel compliance** — motion to compel PDD/FDD compliance, with attorney's fees under § 2107.
2. **Set aside under § 2122** — where a judgment has already been entered based on defective disclosure.
3. **§ 1101 fiduciary-breach action** — separate cause of action with § 1101(h) remedies for willful undisclosed-asset breach.
4. **§ 2556 omitted-asset jurisdiction** — for assets the judgment failed to address.
5. **Set-aside for perjury** — traditional path under § 2122(b) where the declaration was knowingly false.
6. **§ 271 sanctions** — for conduct that frustrates settlement (disclosure stonewalling often qualifies).

The § 2102 fiduciary framework is the substantive spine across all six. Tactical decisions about which path to pursue depend on (a) timing (pre-judgment vs. post-judgment), (b) evidence of intent (willful vs. negligent), and (c) remedy sought (compelled disclosure, asset re-division, monetary damages, sanctions).

## What Chapter 9 does not do

- It does not create automatic monetary damages for noncompliance — that requires a § 1101 action or other substantive cause.
- It does not establish a discovery mechanism — it creates a substantive disclosure mandate separate from CCP § 2017 et seq. discovery rules. Parties can conduct discovery in addition to Chapter 9, but Chapter 9 compliance is not satisfied by discovery responses.
- It does not reach non-spousal parties — Chapter 9 applies to "each party" to a dissolution/legal-separation proceeding. Third-party disclosures (creditors, investment partners, family members holding assets in trust) require separate discovery or subpoena practice.
- It does not protect confidentiality — § 2104(b)'s "not filed with the court" rule is the only confidentiality structure; the disclosures themselves are exchanged between parties without a filed-record confidentiality overlay.

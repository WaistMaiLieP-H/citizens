## How Parties Lose Under This Standard

### 1. Fathers Lose: Challenge Raised Too Late

The most common failure: a party discovers that the mediator was not properly qualified, or that DV screening was skipped, or that separate sessions were denied — but raises the challenge after the custody order has been final for too long. Courts apply a diligent inquiry standard: if the party could have discovered the qualification defect earlier through reasonable investigation, delayed challenge is disfavored.

Best practice: request the mediator's training certification and CV at the earliest opportunity. Do not wait for a bad recommendation to investigate.

### 2. Courts Lose Credibility: Recommendations Without Full Evaluation

When a mediator recommends custody based on one party's claims without evaluating the other parent or the children, and the party who was evaluated wins, the losing party has grounds to challenge the recommendation on Rule 5.210(g) grounds. Courts that adopt unevaluated recommendations without independent review are placing children in arrangements built on incomplete information.

### 3. Victims Lose: Separate Session Right Not Enforced

A domestic violence victim who requests separate sessions and is denied — or who is not informed of the right to request separate sessions — is placed in a setting where the power dynamics of the abusive relationship can operate directly on the mediation process. Recommendations from joint mediation sessions in DV cases are inherently suspect because the mediator has failed to create the conditions for the victim to speak safely.

### 4. Parties Lose: Mediation Misconduct Not Reported at the Time

Rule 5.210 violations that are not contemporaneously documented are harder to raise after the fact. A party who leaves mediation knowing the process was defective but does not immediately write a letter to the court, request a supervisor review, or file a complaint creates an uphill credibility battle later.

### 5. Live Pattern — Steward's Cases (2009-2025)

The custody proceedings from 2009 through 2025 involved multiple rounds of court-ordered mediation. Key questions for forensic audit:
- Did each mediator hold current 40-hour initial training with 16-hour DV component?
- Was DV screening conducted before each assignment given the active protective orders?
- Were separate sessions offered given the documented DV history?
- Did any mediator issue a recommendation without evaluating Michael directly?

Any "no" to the above is a Rule 5.210 violation that is procedurally documented in the court file. These violations form part of the foundation for the systemic due process challenge.

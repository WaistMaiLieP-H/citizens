# Family Code § 3011 — How Parties Lose

## Failure Modes and Procedural Traps

### 1. Failing to Put Abuse Allegations into the Record Before the Hearing

The § 3011(a)(2) factor and the § 3011(a)(5) written-findings requirement only activate when "allegations about a parent pursuant to paragraph (2) or (4) have been brought to the attention of the court in the current proceeding." Parties who raise abuse for the first time at a hearing — without prior declaration, prior court filing, or prior law enforcement report in the record — risk having the allegation excluded or given no weight. Courts have discretion to require corroboration before considering the allegation at all.

**Trap:** Verbal representations at hearing are not "in the record" unless they appear in a written declaration or the court reporter's transcript and are incorporated into findings.

### 2. Relying on Unsigned or Unauthenticated Police Reports as Corroboration

Section 3011(a)(2)(B) specifies "written reports by law enforcement agencies" as a corroboration source. Courts have distinguished between: (a) a sworn, signed report from a named officer, and (b) an unsigned document purporting to be a police report. An unsigned report has authentication problems and cannot constitute corroboration under Evidence Code § 1280 (public records hearsay exception), which requires the report to be made by a public employee in the course of duty. Unsigned documents fail this foundation.

**Steward relevance:** APD Report #23-004494 (June 16, 2023) is unsigned. A court relying on this document as § 3011(a)(2)(B) corroboration of abuse allegations against Michael Hartmann would be applying the statute incorrectly. The unsigned status defeats the document's corroboration value and also raises fabrication concerns that cut the other direction.

### 3. Allowing § 3011(a)(3) Contact Analysis to Override Safety Findings

Courts lose their way when they treat "frequent and continuing contact" as a near-absolute rule and use it to override documented safety concerns. The statute itself prevents this: § 3020(c) and § 3011(a)(1) place safety above contact. An order that grants unsupervised contact despite documented abuse allegations, without the § 3011(a)(5) written findings explaining why contact is safe, is reversible on appeal.

**Pattern:** In long-running disputes, a court that has granted contact for years may resist revising that arrangement even when new evidence of abuse emerges. The party seeking modification must expressly invoke § 3011(a)(5) and demand written findings.

### 4. Failing to Demand Written Findings When the Court Rules Against the Abused Parent

When a court grants unsupervised custody or visitation to a parent accused of abuse under § 3011(a)(2), the § 3011(a)(5) written-findings requirement is mandatory. A party who accepts the adverse ruling without demanding written findings on the record waives the most powerful appellate argument. Courts regularly omit the written findings because no one insists on them at the time of the ruling.

**Procedure:** At the moment of an adverse ruling, counsel or the self-represented party must state on the record: "Your Honor, given that abuse allegations under § 3011(a)(2) are before this court, I request written findings as required by Family Code § 3011(a)(5) stating why this order is in the best interest of the child."

### 5. Stipulated Orders Do Not Eliminate Best-Interest Analysis

Montenegro v. Diaz (2001) is unequivocal: parties cannot stipulate away the best-interest requirement. A stipulated custody order is valid only if it is in the best interest of the child. Courts that rubber-stamp stipulations without independent best-interest analysis commit error. Respondents who later discover that a stipulated order harmed the child can challenge it on Montenegro grounds.

**Trap:** Courts routinely accept stipulated orders in high-volume family law calendars without conducting the § 3011 analysis. The resulting order is technically defective and can be challenged in a motion for reconsideration or on appeal.

### 6. Pre-2024 Analysis Applied to Post-2024 Facts

The 2023 SB 599 amendment (effective January 1, 2024) expanded the categories of abuse victims relevant to § 3011(a)(2). An attorney or court applying pre-2024 case law to a 2024 or later proceeding may miss the expanded scope. This is particularly important when the abuser's history includes violence in prior relationships.

### 7. Ignoring the § 3011(b) Non-Discrimination Mandate

While less common as a losing position, a party who allows gender-based or orientation-based bias to infect the record — without objecting and citing § 3011(b) — forfeits the appellate argument. Bias not preserved at the trial level is deemed waived.

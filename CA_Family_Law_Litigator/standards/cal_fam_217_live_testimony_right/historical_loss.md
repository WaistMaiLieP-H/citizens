# Family Code § 217 — Layer 3: Historical Loss (what drove the statute)

## The pre-2011 practice

By the late 2000s, most California family-law departments had adopted a local rule, standing order, or de facto practice of "declarations only" for OSC and RFO hearings. Parties would file competing declarations, argue for 10–15 minutes at the calendar call, and receive a ruling. No witnesses. No cross-examination. No credibility findings.

This worked for uncontested matters but was devastating in contested ones:

- A parent accused of abuse could not face the accuser.
- A responding party could not cross-examine the moving party's declaration.
- Judges made findings of fact on paper records where the two sides said opposite things — often resolving by deferring to whichever declaration looked more polished, or whichever lawyer was more familiar.

## The Elkins Family Law Task Force (2008–2010)

Following *Elkins v. Superior Court* (2007) 41 Cal.4th 1337 — a California Supreme Court decision holding that a local rule barring live testimony in marital-dissolution trials violated due process and statutory rights — the Chief Justice convened the Elkins Family Law Task Force. The Task Force's April 2010 Final Report identified the "declarations-only" OSC practice as the single most damaging procedural problem in California family law.

Key Task Force findings:
- The practice was inconsistent across counties and even across departments within the same courthouse.
- It disproportionately harmed unrepresented parties, who could not translate their facts into polished declaration form.
- It produced unreliable credibility determinations in the very cases (DV, custody) where credibility was dispositive.

## AB 939 (2010)

AB 939 (Feuer) was the Task Force's flagship legislative recommendation. Stats. 2010, ch. 352 enacted § 217 as a statewide floor. The implementing rule — CRC 5.113 — was adopted by the Judicial Council effective January 1, 2013 (after the January 1, 2012 deadline in § 217(b)).

## Post-enactment case law

- ***In re Marriage of Swain* (2018) 21 Cal.App.5th 830** — reversed trial court for refusing live testimony without on-the-record § 217(b) findings.
- ***Shimkus v. Shimkus* (2016) 244 Cal.App.4th 1266** — § 217 error can be prejudicial where credibility is central.

The pattern since 2011: appellate courts reverse with frequency where trial courts fail to make § 217(b) findings, reinforcing that the right is real and the finding is mandatory.

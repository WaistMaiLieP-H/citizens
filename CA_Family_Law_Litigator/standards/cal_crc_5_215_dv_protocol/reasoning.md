# cal_crc_5_215_dv_protocol — Reasoning

**Status: PROPOSED**

---

## How Courts Apply This Standard

### 1. Identification and Coding Is the Trigger for All Other Protections

The entire Rule 5.215 protective framework depends on the case being identified and coded as a DV case. If FCS staff fail at Step 1 — identification — none of the downstream protections are triggered. Courts reviewing Rule 5.215 compliance start at the identification step: Was the case coded? When? Who made the determination? What information was available at the time?

If a case with an active protective order, or a case with documented DV history in the court file, was not coded as a DV case, the failure to code is itself a protocol violation — the information was available and was ignored.

### 2. Separate Sessions Are the Default, Not the Exception, in DV Cases

The non-DV mediation default is joint sessions. Rule 5.215 inverts this for DV cases: once a case is identified as DV, separate sessions are the default. The victim does not need to request them; staff must offer them. A DV case that proceeds through joint mediation sessions without an affirmative, informed, safety-reviewed decision to do so is presumptively non-compliant.

Courts read "offer separate sessions" as a mandatory provision because § 5.215(d)(6) uses "SHALL." Mediator discretion to conduct joint sessions in a coded DV case does not exist without documented safety assessment supporting the joint format.

### 3. Restraining Orders Cannot Be Mediated Away

The rule is explicit: staff shall not negotiate or mediate the terms of protective orders. A mediator who suggests — implicitly or explicitly — that the parties discuss or modify the terms of an existing protective order as part of the custody discussion is violating Rule 5.215. This is a significant protection because the protective order and the custody arrangement are legally separate; the mediation context cannot be used to pressure a victim into informally agreeing to weaker protection.

### 4. The 16/4 Training Cascade for All Staff

Unlike Rule 5.210's training requirements (which apply to individual mediators), Rule 5.215(f) applies to all FCS staff — mediators, evaluators, investigators, and support staff. This means the training audit must cover every person who interacted with a DV case in a court-connected capacity, not just the mediator of record. Support staff who handled intake, scheduling, and file management without DV training are also in violation.

### 5. Mandatory Referrals

When a victim presents with DV indicators, FCS staff must make referrals to DV resources. This is not optional. A mediator who hears DV information and does not make referrals — or who is not maintaining current DV resource lists — has violated Rule 5.215(g). The referral creates a paper trail that the DV concern was identified and responded to. Absence of referral documentation suggests the concern was not identified or was ignored.

### 6. Recommending Restraining Orders Under § 3183(c)

Family Code § 3183(c) gives mediators the authority to recommend protective orders. Rule 5.215(d)(5) requires them to do so "when appropriate." Courts assess whether the available facts at the time of mediation would have put a competent, properly trained mediator on notice that a restraining order recommendation was warranted. If the facts supported it and no recommendation was made, that is a Rule 5.215 violation.

## How Parties Lose Under This Standard

### 1. Victims Lose: Case Never Coded as DV Despite Active Orders

Active protective orders are in the court file. The case is assigned to mediation and the mediator does not pull the full file before the first session. The case is not coded as DV. Joint sessions proceed. The victim, intimidated by the presence of the abuser in the same room, gives statements consistent with what the abuser wants. The mediator recommends custody accordingly. The court adopts the recommendation.

Later, when the victim challenges the order, the court asks: why didn't you object at the time? The victim, without legal representation, did not know Rule 5.215 gave them the right to separate sessions. The window to challenge closes.

### 2. Victims Lose: Mediation Used to Pressure Away Protective Order

The mediator — in violation of Rule 5.215 — suggests that if the parties can "work things out" on custody, the restraining order might not be necessary. The victim, desperate to see the children, informally agrees to reduced contact restrictions. Later, the abuser cites these informal agreements to argue the protective order has been waived or that the parties have a new arrangement. The victim's legal protection has been eroded through the very process that was supposed to protect them.

### 3. Courts Lose: Adopting Recommendations Without DV Protocol Audit

A trial court that adopts an FCS recommendation in a DV case without verifying that the recommendation came through a Rule 5.215-compliant process is building an order on a defective foundation. When that order is appealed or attacked collaterally, the absence of DV protocol compliance is a basis for reversal that the trial court could have prevented.

### 4. Agencies Lose: Training Records Cannot Be Produced

A party challenging Rule 5.215 compliance sends a PRA request for the training records of every FCS staff member who handled the case. The agency cannot produce 16-hour initial DV training records for one or more staff members. The training deficiency is documented. The court's supervision of its own FCS program is now at issue. This is an institutional credibility problem that grows with each undocumented training gap.

### 5. Live Pattern — Steward's Cases (Contra Costa County FCS, 2009-2025)

Key audit questions for every FCS assignment in the Hartmann/Cerretani proceedings:
- Is the case coded as DV in CCFCS records?
- Were separate sessions offered at every assignment?
- Do training records for each assigned mediator/evaluator show 16-hour DV initial training + 4-hour annual updates?
- Was a restraining order recommendation made in any session where the facts supported it?
- Were DV resource referrals documented?

A PRA request to Contra Costa FCS for all of the above is the mechanism to build the Rule 5.215 violation record.

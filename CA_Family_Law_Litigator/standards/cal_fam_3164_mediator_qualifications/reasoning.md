# Family Code § 3164 — Judicial Reasoning and Doctrinal Application

## Overview

Section 3164 is the operative qualification standard for all custody mediators in California. It has two clauses: § 3164(a) identifies who may serve as mediator (broad — professional staff of several agency types, or any court-designated person), and § 3164(b) imposes the mandatory qualification floor from § 1815. The broad designation authority in § 3164(a) is limited by the SHALL requirement in § 3164(b). A court may designate anyone — but that person must satisfy § 1815 to act as mediator.

## Key Doctrinal Points

### 1. The § 3164(b) "SHALL" Provision Is Non-Discretionary

The statute states: "The mediator SHALL meet the minimum qualifications required of a counselor of conciliation as provided in Section 1815." "Shall" in California statutes is mandatory. Courts have no discretion to waive the qualification requirement for a mediator they designate under § 3164(a). A mediation conducted by an unqualified person under § 3164(a) violates § 3164(b) as a matter of law.

### 2. "Court-Designated" Does Not Equal "Qualified"

Section 3164(a) allows a court to designate "any other person or agency." This provision has been misread by some courts as granting broad discretion to select any qualified individual the court chooses. The clause grants flexibility in who is designated — not flexibility to waive qualifications. The § 3164(a) designation authority and the § 3164(b) qualification requirement operate independently: designation is the act; qualification is the prerequisite.

### 3. The § 1815 Qualifications Are Incorporated by Reference — Fully

Section 3164(b) incorporates "the minimum qualifications required of a counselor of conciliation as provided in Section 1815." This means all seven qualifications in § 1815(a)(1)-(7) apply to mediators under § 3164. There is no selective incorporation. A mediator who satisfies six of seven § 1815 qualifications but lacks, for example, the DV training required by § 1815(a)(7)/§ 1816, does not meet § 3164(b)'s standard.

### 4. The Statute Applies to All Custody Mediations Under Chapter 11

Chapter 11 of Division 8 (§§ 3160-3188) governs all mediation of custody and visitation issues. Section 3164 applies to all mediators operating within this framework. A person who conducts a custody mediation for a California court — regardless of how the court characterizes the mediator's role — is subject to § 3164.

### 5. Procedural Defect = Defective Recommendation

When a mediator does not satisfy § 3164(b)/§ 1815, the resulting mediation recommendation is procedurally defective. Barelli v. Barelli (1997) confirmed that courts cannot accept defective mediation recommendations as a substitute for an independent judicial best-interest determination. A custody order entered substantially in reliance on a procedurally defective mediation recommendation may be challenged on the ground that the § 3011 best-interest analysis was never properly conducted.

### 6. The Ajaniku Application

Sala Ajaniku served as mediator in the September 2010 custody change in Alameda County case RF09456481. Section 3164(b), effective January 1, 1994, required Ajaniku to satisfy all § 1815 qualifications. If Ajaniku: (a) did not hold a master's degree in a qualifying field, (b) did not have two years of counseling experience, or (c) did not have the required specialized knowledge including DV training — then the 2010 mediation was conducted by an unqualified person in violation of § 3164(b). The custody change resulting from that mediation was built on a procedurally defective foundation.

Ajaniku has no verifiable credentials in any California professional licensing database (LCSW, MFT, psychologist). The absence from public licensing databases is itself evidence of non-compliance with § 1815(a)(1)-(2).

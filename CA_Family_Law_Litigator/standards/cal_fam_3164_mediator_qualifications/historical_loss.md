# Family Code § 3164 — How Parties Lose

## Failure Modes and Procedural Traps

### 1. Failing to Distinguish § 3164(a) Designation Authority from § 3164(b) Qualification Requirement

The most common error: accepting the court's § 3164(a) designation of a mediator as establishing the mediator's qualifications. Courts have authority to designate any person under § 3164(a). That designation does not satisfy § 3164(b). A party who does not separately verify that the designated mediator meets § 1815 qualifications is relying on the court's good faith — and that good faith may not have been exercised.

**Trap:** Courts routinely designate mediators without conducting their own § 1815 qualification review. The burden falls on the litigant to catch the error.

### 2. Accepting a Defective Mediation Recommendation Without Preserving Objection

If a party participates in mediation with an unqualified mediator and does not object on the record, the objection may be waived at the trial level. The objection must be raised at the time of designation, before mediation begins. Barelli v. Barelli confirms that courts cannot use a defective recommendation as a substitute for judicial best-interest analysis — but this protection is most fully available when the qualification challenge was timely made.

### 3. Not Understanding the Collateral-Attack Window

When a custody order was entered in reliance on a defective § 3164/§ 1815 mediation, Montenegro v. Diaz provides the vehicle for collateral attack: argue that no valid judicial best-interest determination was ever made because the underlying recommendation was procedurally defective. This argument is strongest when: (a) the mediator's lack of credentials can be established through public licensing databases; (b) the court order substantially mirrors the mediator's recommendation without independent analysis; and (c) no new changed-circumstances analysis has occurred since the original order.

**Steward relevance:** The September 2010 custody modification in RF09456481 followed Sala Ajaniku's mediation. Ajaniku's credentials are not verifiable in any California public licensing database. If no independent judicial § 3011 best-interest analysis was conducted beyond reliance on Ajaniku's recommendation, the order is subject to Montenegro collateral attack. The Montenegro theory is that no qualified mediator produced the input, therefore no valid judicial determination was ever made, therefore the changed-circumstances rule does not protect the 2010 order, and a new full § 3011 analysis governs.

### 4. Failing to Use Public Records to Establish Non-Credentials

The absence of credentials is provable through public records. The California Board of Behavioral Sciences (BBS) maintains public license verification for LCSWs, MFTs, and educational psychologists. The California Board of Psychology maintains public verification for licensed psychologists. A person who is unregistered with all relevant licensing boards, who holds no current California license in a qualifying field, and whose credentials cannot be independently verified does not satisfy § 1815(a)(1)-(2).

**Procedure:** BBS license verification: https://www.bbs.ca.gov/consumers/verify_license.html. Board of Psychology: https://www.psychology.ca.gov/consumers/. Document the negative result — that no license record exists — as evidence of non-compliance with § 1815(a)(1)-(2).

### 5. The Short Statute — The Danger Is in Overlooking It

Section 3164 is one of the shortest statutes in the Family Code — two subdivisions, four sentences. Its brevity causes practitioners to overlook it. But § 3164(b)'s SHALL provision is one of the most powerful qualification challenges available in California family law. A two-sentence statute with a SHALL incorporates the full seven-part § 1815 requirement. Courts and practitioners who skim past § 3164 without reading its § 1815 cross-reference miss the entire substance of the qualification rule.

# Historical Loss — Cal. Pen. Code § 11165.6

## How Parties Lose on This Standard

### Failure Mode 1: Conflating the Definition with a Cause of Action
Parties who plead § 11165.6 as a direct civil claim are dismissed. § 11165.6 is a definitional statute, not a civil remedy provision. The correct use is: (a) as an input to § 3011(a)(2)(B) in custody proceedings; (b) as the trigger for CANRA mandatory reporting obligations; (c) as a definitional reference in related criminal charges under § 11165.1-11165.4. Standalone § 11165.6 claims do not exist.

### Failure Mode 2: Failing to Cross-Reference § 3011(a)(2)(B)
Parties who establish that conduct qualifies as child abuse under § 11165.6 but fail to then invoke § 3011(a)(2)(B) in the custody proceeding have done half the work. The judge may not independently make the connection. The pleading or brief must expressly state: "Conduct X qualifies as child abuse under Penal Code § 11165.6, and therefore must be considered by this Court as a mandatory factor under Family Code § 3011(a)(2)(B)."

### Failure Mode 3: Not Invoking Mandatory Reporting as a Procedural Mechanism
Where conduct qualifies as child abuse under § 11165.6, mandatory reporters who were present (teachers, medical providers, therapists) had a legal duty to report. If they did not report, that failure is itself relevant — it may indicate the conduct was concealed, or it may provide a basis for a finding that the reporting structure was compromised. Parties who do not investigate whether mandatory reports were made or suppressed miss this evidence layer.

### Failure Mode 4: Allowing "Willful Harm" to Be Narrowly Read
Courts sometimes receive § 11165.3 "willful harm and endangerment" arguments narrowly, requiring proof of direct physical acts against the child. The broader reading — exposure to domestic violence, exposure to dangerous environments, engineered psychological harm — is supported by CANRA's text and legislative history but must be affirmatively argued. Without case law citation and legislative intent argument, courts default to the narrower reading.

### Failure Mode 5: Not Using False CANRA Reports as Evidence of Abuse
Where a parent files false mandated reports or orchestrates false CANRA reports, that conduct is itself relevant to the custody analysis as willful conduct designed to endanger the child's welfare and the other parent's relationship with the child. Parties who receive false CANRA reports and respond only defensively (clearing their name) miss the offensive use: the false report itself is evidence of willful conduct against the child's best interest under § 3011(a)(2)(B).

## Steward's Case Application
The 16-year pattern includes instances where Christina's counter-filing strategy involved reports to child protective services and law enforcement. Each false report should be analyzed under § 11165.6: was it an act of willful harm or endangerment under § 11165.3 (deliberately exposing children to custody conflict through fabricated reports)? If so, it feeds into § 3011(a)(2)(B) as child abuse by Christina. The Sala Ajaniku evaluation, which should have assessed this pattern, must be audited for whether it addressed Christina's report-filing pattern under the § 11165.6 framework.

# Reasoning — Cal. Pen. Code § 11165.6

## How Courts Apply This Standard

### 1. § 11165.6 Is a Gateway Definition, Not a Standalone Claim
§ 11165.6 defines what constitutes "child abuse or neglect" for purposes of CANRA (mandatory reporting) and for cross-reference to the Family Code. It is not itself a civil cause of action — it is the definitional anchor that other statutes use. Courts apply § 11165.6 by asking: does the conduct alleged fall within the definition? If yes, mandatory reporting obligations (CANRA) are triggered and the Family Code cross-references activate.

### 2. The § 3011(a)(2)(B) Link — Custody Proceedings
Family Code § 3011(a)(2)(B) requires the court, in determining the best interest of the child, to consider "[a]ny history of abuse by one parent or any other person seeking custody" including abuse as defined in § 11165.6. This means every act that qualifies under § 11165.6 is automatically a § 3011 factor. The connection is statutory — no discretion. If conduct meets the § 11165.6 definition, it must be considered in the custody analysis.

### 3. Physical Injury "By Other Than Accidental Means" — Intent Not Required
The physical injury prong of § 11165.6 covers injury inflicted "by other than accidental means." Courts hold this does not require proof of specific intent to harm — it is sufficient to show the injury was not accidental. Non-accidental injury to a child is child abuse regardless of whether the inflicting party subjectively intended harm.

### 4. Willful Harm and Endangerment (§ 11165.3) — Broad Scope
§ 11165.3, incorporated by reference in § 11165.6, covers the willful harming or injuring of a child and the endangering of a child's person or health. Courts have held this covers situations where a parent places a child in a situation that endangers their health — not limited to direct physical violence. A parent who exposes a child to sustained domestic violence, substance abuse, or environments posing health risks may be covered by § 11165.3 even without direct physical contact.

### 5. The Mutual Combat Exclusion Does Not Apply to Parent-Child Relationships
The mutual combat exclusion applies only where "both parties consent." Children cannot consent to combat; the exclusion is functionally inapplicable in any parent-child context. This exclusion is most relevant in adult domestic violence disputes where both parties are alleged to have been combatants.

### 6. Peace Officer Exclusion — Cannot Shield Fabricated Reports
The exclusion for reasonable and necessary force by peace officers applies to the officer's conduct, not to conduct by civilians who cause police to be directed against a target. The exclusion does not shield a parent who uses fabricated police reports to direct state actors against the other parent. The parent-manipulator is not a peace officer; their conduct in orchestrating police contacts is not protected.

## Practical Test
To establish that conduct is "child abuse or neglect" under § 11165.6: (1) identify which subsection the conduct falls under (physical injury, sexual abuse, neglect, willful harm, unlawful corporal punishment); (2) establish the non-accidental or willful character of the conduct; (3) connect the finding to § 3011(a)(2)(B) in the custody proceeding; (4) confirm no exclusion applies. Document with medical records, photographs, witness statements, and prior mandated reporter reports.

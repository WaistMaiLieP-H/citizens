# §6146 — Evolution Stage 01: Origin 1975 (MICRA Package)

**Enacted:** Stats. 1975, 2d Ex. Sess., Ch. 2 (MICRA)
**Effective:** December 12, 1975

---

## The wound this enactment answered (MICRA framing)

The 1975 MICRA package argued that plaintiff's attorneys were taking contingency fees of
33%–40% on large malpractice recoveries. When the noneconomic damages were unlimited (pre-§3333.2),
a successful malpractice verdict could be $2M+; attorney fees at 40% meant $800K+ per case.
The defendant lobby argued this fee structure created perverse incentives for plaintiff's bar.

**The 1975 original sliding scale (lower thresholds):**
- First $50,000: 40%
- Next $50,000: 33.33%
- Next $100,000: 25%
- Any amount over $200,000: 10%

(Note: these 1975 thresholds were far lower than today's — $200K in 1975 was a large verdict.
The original 10% tier on anything over $200K severely limited fee recovery in large cases.)

---

## The 1975 logical structure

**Core mechanism:** A mandatory declining scale. An attorney who achieves a large settlement
earns an ever-smaller percentage of each additional dollar. This theoretically reduces the
marginal incentive to hold out for maximum verdicts.

**The plaintiff bar critique (then and now):** The scale makes legitimate small-value cases
uneconomical. A $75K malpractice case (40% of first $50K = $20K + 33.33% of next $25K = $8.3K
= ~$28.3K total fee) often involves 40+ hours of expert-heavy litigation. At effective hourly
rates below $100/hr, many cases were not economically viable for attorneys.

**The unintended consequence documented:** Patients with legitimate smaller-value malpractice
claims (wrong medication dosage, missed diagnosis in outpatient setting) were unable to find
attorneys willing to take their cases under the original §6146 thresholds. The fee cap
effectively screened out a large class of meritorious smaller cases.

---

## What the 1975 statute required

1. Written disclosure of the fee schedule to the client (§6146(a))
2. Application to any "contingent fee agreement" in medical malpractice cases
3. Application to all recoveries — verdict, settlement, arbitration award
4. The scale applied to the gross recovery (or net after expenses, depending on whether
   attorney advanced costs — this interpretation was litigated in subsequent cases)

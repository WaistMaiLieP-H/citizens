# CCP §364 — Current Framework (2026)

**Citation:** Cal. Code Civ. Proc. §364
**Popular name:** MICRA 90-day Pre-Suit Notice of Intent to Commence Action
**Nature:** Procedural prerequisite to filing a medical malpractice complaint in California.

## The operational logic

§364 is the procedural gatekeeper of California medical malpractice litigation. Before filing a complaint, the plaintiff must serve a specific kind of written notice on each health care provider defendant, and must then wait at least 90 days before commencing the action. The statute tolls the CCP §340.5 statute of limitations during this waiting period in specified circumstances. It is simultaneously a consumer-protective mechanism (giving providers notice and an opportunity to resolve) and a procedural trap (missing the notice can produce a technical dismissal).

## The three operative commands

### 1. Written notice of intent (§364(a))

No action may be commenced against a health care provider based on professional negligence unless the defendant has been given at least 90 days' prior notice of the intention to commence the action. The notice must:

- Be in writing
- Be served by certified or registered mail OR personally (§364(d))
- Identify the legal basis of the claim
- Identify the type of loss sustained
- Identify the nature of the injuries sustained

The 1975 Legislature did not require the notice to be in any particular form beyond these content requirements. In practice, lawyers use a standard letter format that tracks the statutory requirements and attaches any relevant medical records summary. The notice does NOT have to disclose the specific theory of liability or the amount of damages claimed — just the general nature of the claim.

### 2. The 90-day wait (§364(a))

After serving the notice, the plaintiff must wait at least 90 days before filing the complaint. This period is designed to give the health care provider (and the provider's liability insurer) an opportunity to:

- Investigate the claim
- Review relevant medical records
- Decide whether to offer settlement
- Respond to the claimant

In practice, the 90-day wait rarely produces pre-suit settlements in contested cases, but the statute requires it nonetheless.

### 3. The tolling effect (§364(d))

§364(d) provides: "If the notice is served within 90 days of the expiration of the applicable statute of limitations, the time for the commencement of the action shall be extended 90 days from the service of the notice."

This is the single most important operational feature of §364. It means:

- If the plaintiff discovers the claim with MORE than 90 days remaining in the §340.5 limitations period, the notice is served normally and the case is filed after the 90-day wait. No tolling effect needed.

- If the plaintiff discovers the claim with LESS than 90 days remaining, serving the §364 notice extends the SOL by 90 days from the date of service.

The tolling provision is what makes §364 workable for late-discovered cases. Without it, a plaintiff who discovered a claim 60 days before the SOL would be structurally barred: serving the required notice would consume the remaining SOL, and the 90-day wait would push the filing past the deadline.

## The consequence of failure

What happens if a plaintiff files suit without serving §364 notice, or files suit within the 90-day wait period?

The answer is counterintuitive: failure to comply with §364 does NOT automatically dismiss the action. Under Woods v. Young (1991) 53 Cal.3d 315, the California Supreme Court held that §364 is a "directory" rather than "jurisdictional" requirement. A court cannot dismiss a complaint for §364 non-compliance; the statute's remedy for non-compliance is different.

The actual consequence under Woods:
- If the plaintiff served the notice but filed suit before the 90-day wait expired, the action is not dismissed. The court stays the action until the 90-day period runs.
- If the plaintiff failed to serve the notice at all, the Woods court held that the failure is professional misconduct (reportable to the Bar) but not a basis for dismissal.
- The defendant's remedy for non-compliance is limited — typically a motion to stay rather than a motion to dismiss.

This means §364 is, in practice, LESS harsh than its plain text suggests. Missing the notice is bad, but it's recoverable. Woods transformed §364 from a trap into a speed bump.

## The interaction with §340.5

§364 and §340.5 must be calendared together. The operational question is always: "How close am I to the §340.5 limitations period, and does §364(d) tolling apply?"

For the steward's SIRVA case, the calendar is:
- **Discovery date:** 2026-03-18
- **§340.5 one-year clock:** expires 2027-03-18
- **§364 notice deadline:** to preserve tolling, serve notice within 90 days of 2027-03-18 (so no later than 2026-12-18)
- **Safe filing window:** serve notice ASAP (e.g., 2026-09-18, six months before SOL expiration); 90-day wait runs to 2026-12-18; file anytime between 2026-12-18 and 2027-03-18.

If the steward waits until January 2027 to serve notice, §364(d) tolling applies automatically and the SOL extends to (notice date + 90 days). This is the late-service scenario the tolling provision was designed to accommodate.

## Who qualifies as a "health care provider" for §364 purposes

§364 defines health care providers by reference to the same definitional framework as Civ. Code §3333.2(e)(2) and CCP §340.5 — persons licensed or certified under Division 2 of the B&P Code (physicians, nurses, pharmacists, etc.) and clinics/health dispensaries/health facilities licensed under Division 2 of the Health and Safety Code.

This is important for the SIRVA case because:
- Walgreens (as a corporation) is likely NOT itself a health care provider under the definition. But Walgreens' individual administering pharmacist IS a health care provider.
- Walgreens could be liable on an ostensible agency theory (the pharmacist was acting as Walgreens' agent) — and if the ostensible agency claim is pled against Walgreens as a HOSPITAL OR HEALTH FACILITY under the B&P Code, §364 applies to Walgreens too.
- If the claim is pled as a corporate negligence claim against Walgreens (negligent training, supervision, protocol failure) that is NOT "professional negligence" in the strict MICRA sense, §364 may not apply to the claim at all.

The Citizen's SIRVA complaint should probably serve §364 notice on both the pharmacist (definitely a provider) and Walgreens (defensively, in case the court finds the corporate negligence claim is within MICRA).

## The doctrinal stability

§364 has been in essentially its current form since 1975. The only significant post-enactment development was Woods v. Young (1991), which softened the consequences of non-compliance. The Legislature has not revisited §364 meaningfully since. It is a stable, well-understood, mechanical procedural rule — exactly the kind of rule the Citizen should execute with checklist precision.

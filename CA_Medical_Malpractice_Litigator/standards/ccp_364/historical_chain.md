# Historical Chain — CCP §364

## The wound

The 1975 medical malpractice insurance crisis framing produced MICRA, and MICRA produced §364 as its procedural-gateway provision. The specific wound §364 was meant to address — as framed by the medical and insurance industries during the 1975 hearings — was "surprise litigation": providers and insurers claimed they were routinely served with medical malpractice complaints without advance warning, giving them no time to investigate or settle before legal fees and defensive posturing escalated.

Plaintiffs' advocates disputed the factual premise. Medical providers generally had defense files established well before any particular claim surfaced, and insurers had underwriting knowledge of claim exposure at the level of each insured. The notion that complaints were a "surprise" was viewed by plaintiffs' counsel as a rhetorical framing rather than an operational reality.

The 1975 Legislature resolved the dispute by adopting the industry's framing and enacting §364. The cost was the addition of a procedural step and a 90-day delay to every medical malpractice filing. The consideration the statute offered plaintiffs was the §364(d) tolling provision, which ensured the notice requirement did not become a structural SOL bar.

## The promise

§364 promised that:

1. **Providers would receive structured pre-suit notice** — a specific kind of written notification of the intended claim.

2. **A 90-day investigation window would follow** — enough time for the provider and insurer to review records, communicate with the claimant, and potentially resolve the matter before litigation began.

3. **The notice requirement would not bar late-discovered claims** — the §364(d) tolling provision preserves plaintiffs' ability to comply with §364 even when they discover their claim close to the SOL deadline.

The statute did NOT promise that non-compliance would automatically dismiss claims. The text is mandatory ("shall give"), but the consequence of non-compliance was left ambiguous. This ambiguity was resolved 16 years later by the California Supreme Court in Woods v. Young (1991), which held §364 was directory, not jurisdictional. After Woods, non-compliance became a speed bump, not a trap.

## The arc

- **1975:** §364 enacted as part of MICRA package. Text is essentially final on enactment.
- **1975-1991:** Courts applied §364 unevenly. Some dismissed for non-compliance; some stayed; some denied any remedy. The inconsistency created uncertainty about the stakes of non-compliance.
- **1991 — Woods v. Young.** California Supreme Court holds §364 is directory, not jurisdictional. Failure to comply is not a basis for dismissal; the proper remedy is a stay. §364 becomes less harsh in practice than its text suggests.
- **1999 — Preferred Risk v. Reiswig.** California Supreme Court clarifies that §364(d) tolling applies automatically upon service of notice, regardless of whether notice was technically required. Plaintiffs can serve in doubt and get the tolling benefit.
- **1999-2026:** §364 remains stable in text and interpretation. No significant amendments; no Supreme Court retrenchment. Technical lower-court decisions refine edge cases (what counts as adequate notice content, who qualifies as a "health care provider" for §364 purposes, how the tolling interacts with §340.5's three-year outer limit).

## Current state (2026)

§364 is a stable, well-understood procedural rule. Its doctrinal treatment is:

- **Textually mandatory** — plaintiffs "shall" give notice before filing
- **Practically forgiving** — Woods holds failure is not jurisdictional
- **Tolling-protective** — Preferred Risk holds the tolling benefit is automatic on service
- **Consequence-limited** — the worst realistic consequence of non-compliance is a stay, not a dismissal

The Citizen treats §364 as a checklist item: draft the notice, serve by certified mail, calendar the 90-day wait, and file the complaint. The checklist is mechanical; the stakes are manageable; the risk of error is low.

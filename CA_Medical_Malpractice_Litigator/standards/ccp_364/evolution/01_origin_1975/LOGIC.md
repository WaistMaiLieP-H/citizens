# CCP §364 Evolution — Stage 01: Origin (1975 MICRA)

**Enactment:** Stats. 1975, 2d Extraordinary Session, Ch. 2, §1 (part of the MICRA package)
**Effective:** December 12, 1975

## The wound

The 1975 MICRA package was enacted in response to what the Legislature described as a medical malpractice insurance crisis. The package's overall logic (documented in the Civ §3333.2 historical chain) was to reduce the expected cost of medical malpractice claims by introducing a lattice of procedural and substantive constraints. §364 was the procedural-gateway component of that lattice.

The specific problem §364 was designed to solve was claimed "surprise litigation": doctors and liability insurers claimed they were routinely being served with medical malpractice complaints without prior warning, giving them no opportunity to investigate, settle, or even prepare an answer. The result, the medical industry argued, was inflated defense costs and inflated verdicts because cases proceeded to trial on incomplete records.

Plaintiffs' advocates disputed this framing, noting that complaint-filing is inherently notice and that insurers had defense files on most medical providers well in advance of any specific claim. The dispute was partially empirical and partially political, and the 1975 Legislature resolved it by enacting §364 as part of the compromise that produced MICRA.

## The promise

§364 promised two things:

1. **A structured pre-suit window** during which the provider and insurer could investigate, communicate with the claimant, and potentially resolve the matter without litigation. The 90-day period was chosen as a balance between meaningful investigation time and acceptable delay.

2. **A tolling mechanism (§364(d))** to ensure that the notice requirement did not become a structural SOL bar for plaintiffs who discovered their claims close to the limitations period. The 90-day extension preserves the plaintiff's ability to comply without losing the claim.

The statute did NOT promise automatic dismissal for non-compliance. The 1975 text said the plaintiff "shall" give notice, but it did not specify the consequence of failure. This ambiguity was resolved sixteen years later by the California Supreme Court in Woods v. Young, which held that §364 is directory rather than jurisdictional.

## The structural design

§364 fits into the MICRA package as one of four procedural-gateway rules:

1. §364 — 90-day pre-suit notice
2. §340.5 — shortened SOL (one year from discovery / three years from injury)
3. §425.13 — punitive damages pleading control
4. §667.7 — periodic payment of future damages

Each of these rules exists to reduce the plaintiff's structural advantages in medical malpractice litigation — shorter time to sue, mandatory notice-and-wait, gated punitive damages, periodic rather than lump-sum payouts. §364 is the gentlest of the four — a procedural speed bump rather than a damages cap.

## What the 1975 enactment did NOT specify

- Whether non-compliance produced dismissal, stay, or some other remedy (resolved by Woods 1991)
- Exactly which categories of providers are covered (the definitional cross-reference to §3333.2(e)(2) clarified this)
- Whether the notice must disclose the specific theory of liability (courts have held it need not — general description suffices)
- How the tolling provision interacts with the §340.5 three-year outer limit (courts have held §364(d) tolls both the one-year and three-year triggers)

## Why this stage matters

The 1975 enactment established §364 in its essentially current form. Every subsequent development has been interpretive, not textual. This is important because it means a 2026 application of §364 is directly continuous with the 1975 legislative intent — there is no amended-version doctrine to parse.

For the steward's SIRVA case, the 1975 intent maps cleanly: the 90-day pre-suit window exists to give Walgreens and the administering pharmacist an opportunity to investigate the claim. The tolling provision exists to ensure that the steward's late-discovery filing position is preserved. Both of these features operate on the steward's case exactly as the 1975 Legislature designed them to operate.

# Woods v. Young (1991)

**Cite:** 53 Cal.3d 315
**Court:** California Supreme Court
**Date:** May 9, 1991
**Opinion by:** Justice Lucas

## The rule the case stands for

**CCP §364 is directory, not jurisdictional. A plaintiff's failure to comply with the 90-day pre-suit notice requirement does NOT deprive the court of jurisdiction over the action, and the proper remedy for non-compliance is a stay of the proceedings until the 90-day period has expired, not dismissal.**

Woods also held that §364(d)'s tolling provision — the 90-day SOL extension when notice is served within 90 days of the limitations deadline — applies automatically upon service of notice, without requiring court approval.

## Plain-language restatement

A plaintiff served a §364 notice and then filed a medical malpractice complaint before the 90-day wait period had run. The defendant moved to dismiss on the ground that the complaint was premature. The California Supreme Court held that dismissal was not the proper remedy — the court should instead stay the proceedings until the 90 days had elapsed and then let the case proceed.

The practical effect: a plaintiff who made a mistake with §364 compliance could typically cure it without losing the case. The mistake was annoying but not fatal.

## The doctrinal significance

Before Woods, there was genuine uncertainty about what §364 non-compliance meant. Some lower courts had dismissed for non-compliance. Some had stayed. Some had denied any remedy at all. The Supreme Court's resolution — directory, not jurisdictional; stay, not dismissal — transformed §364 from a trap into a speed bump.

Three specific points from Woods that the Citizen needs to know:

1. **Directory language.** The statute says "shall give" but the Supreme Court read this as mandating the conduct without mandating dismissal for failure. This is a common statutory interpretation pattern in California procedural law — the word "shall" does not automatically create a jurisdictional consequence.

2. **Stay as remedy.** The defendant's proper motion is to stay the action, not to dismiss. A court that dismisses a medical malpractice case solely for §364 non-compliance reverses on appeal.

3. **Automatic tolling.** The §364(d) 90-day extension applies by operation of law when the notice is served within 90 days of the limitations deadline. No court order is required to "activate" the tolling.

## The implications for plaintiff strategy

Woods created some flexibility, but the Citizen should NOT rely on Woods to cure avoidable mistakes:

- **Always serve notice.** Even if Woods says failure is not jurisdictional, a defendant can still move to stay, and the stay consumes time and attorney hours. Comply in the first instance.

- **Calendar the 90-day wait from the notice date.** Filing before the wait expires invites a stay motion. File after.

- **Calendar the §340.5 SOL with the §364(d) tolling in mind.** If the notice was served within 90 days of the SOL, the filing window is (notice date + 90 days) to (SOL tolled date = notice date + 90 days PLUS the original remaining SOL). The tolling is additive, not a replacement of the remaining SOL.

- **Document service carefully.** Woods is permissive, but a court evaluating a non-compliance scenario still wants to see that the plaintiff tried to comply. Certified mail return receipt preserves the record.

## Subsequent history and status

- **Current status (2026):** Woods remains controlling authority on §364's directory nature.
- **Applied consistently** in subsequent California medical malpractice cases.
- **Not overturned or modified** by any later California Supreme Court decision.

## Why this matters for the steward's cases

Woods is the safety net. For the steward's SIRVA case, the Citizen's primary obligation is to comply with §364 in the first instance — serve the notice, wait 90 days, file. If something goes wrong (e.g., the notice is mis-served or the filing is premature), Woods provides the cure path. But the Citizen should not invoke Woods proactively as a strategy; it's a backup, not a plan.

For the calendar math: if the Citizen serves the §364 notice on 2026-12-01 (four months before the §340.5 2027-03-18 deadline), the 90-day wait expires 2027-03-01, leaving 17 days of the original SOL period. The filing window is 2027-03-01 through 2027-03-18. §364(d) tolling does not apply because the notice was served more than 90 days before the SOL deadline. This is the cleanest filing posture for the SIRVA case.

If the Citizen waits until 2027-01-10 to serve notice, the notice is served 67 days before the SOL deadline. §364(d) tolling applies because the notice is within 90 days of the SOL. The extended SOL becomes 2027-01-10 + 90 days = 2027-04-10. The filing window becomes 2027-04-10 (90 days after notice) through 2027-04-10 (tolled SOL). This is the late-service scenario.

Both scenarios are workable. The Citizen should push the steward toward the cleaner early-service posture to avoid relying on the tolling provision at all.

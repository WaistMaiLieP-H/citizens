# CCP §425.13 — Punitive Damages Gate in Medical Malpractice: Current Logic

## The logical architecture

CCP §425.13 is a procedural gate unique to professional negligence actions against health care
providers. It does NOT eliminate punitive damages — it requires the plaintiff to obtain court
permission before the punitive damages allegation can be included in the complaint.

Without clearing §425.13, a medical malpractice complaint cannot include a prayer for punitive
damages under Civ. Code §3294. Attempting to plead punitive damages without satisfying §425.13
is subject to a motion to strike.

## The gate: what §425.13 requires

Before filing or amending a complaint to include punitive damages in a professional negligence
action against a health care provider, the plaintiff must file a motion with the court showing:

**"Substantial probability that the plaintiff will prevail on the claim pursuant to Section 3294
of the Civil Code."**

The motion must be supported by competent evidence — typically declarations, medical records,
and expert opinions establishing that the defendant:
1. Acted with malice, oppression, or fraud (§3294(a)), AND
2. The plaintiff has substantial probability of proving this at trial

The court reviews the evidence in camera or on the papers and makes a probable-cause
determination — not a full merits determination. The standard is lower than summary judgment:
the court is asking whether the claim has the level of support that makes it not frivolous, not
whether it will ultimately succeed.

## "Health care provider" scope

§425.13(a) applies to actions for professional negligence against a "health care provider" as
defined in CCP §340.5(1): licensed physicians, surgeons, dentists, podiatrists, pharmacists,
hospitals, clinics, nursing facilities, and other licensed health care entities.

**Critical scope question:** Does §425.13 apply when the claim is framed as intentional tort
rather than professional negligence?

## The Hrimnak escape applied to §425.13

Under **Central Pathology Service Medical Clinic, Inc. v. Superior Court (1992)** — the leading
case on §425.13's scope — the gate applies to claims "arising out of the professional negligence
of a health care provider." The Court held that this includes claims that are nominally pleaded
as intentional torts if the conduct forming the basis of the claim constitutes professional
negligence.

**However:** If the claim is truly an intentional tort independent of the standard of care —
fraud, battery based on lack of informed consent, or intentional infliction of harm — courts
have held §425.13 does not apply. The analysis is fact-specific.

**For the spine surgery fraud case:**
- If framed as professional negligence (deviation from standard of care in performing unnecessary
  surgery): §425.13 applies — must file the motion before pleading punitives
- If framed as intentional fraud/battery (surgeon performed surgery knowing it was unnecessary
  and without genuine informed consent): courts are split; the stronger argument is that true
  intentional fraud is outside §425.13's scope
- **Safest approach:** File the §425.13 motion in the alternative, presenting evidence supporting
  both the professional negligence and intentional tort theories. If the intentional tort framing
  is accepted, §425.13 is moot; if the court applies §425.13, the motion is ready.

## Timing

§425.13 motions must be filed within the time period set forth in the scheduling order or, if
none, at least 30 days before the close of discovery. This is not a pleading-stage requirement
in the sense that the complaint cannot include punitives at all without the motion — it is a
procedural prerequisite that must be satisfied before the punitive allegation can stand.

**Practice note:** File the complaint without the punitive damages prayer, then bring the
§425.13 motion after gathering the evidence needed to support it. Alternatively, file the
complaint with the punitive prayer and expect a motion to strike, then oppose with the §425.13
motion concurrently.

## What satisfies the "substantial probability" showing

Evidence courts have found sufficient:
- Expert declaration that the defendant's conduct fell below the standard of care AND constituted
  a conscious disregard of the patient's safety
- Medical records showing the clinical decisions were objectively unjustifiable
- Evidence of similar conduct toward other patients (pattern = malice/oppression)
- Billing records showing financial motivation behind medical decisions
- Prior Medical Board complaints or discipline (public record in BreEZe)

**For the spine surgery fraud case:** Expert opinion that surgery was not clinically indicated
+ billing records showing financial motivation + any prior discipline history = strong §425.13
showing. The intentional tort overlay (fraud/battery) strengthens the case further.

## The §425.13 motion in the MICRA lattice

§425.13 is the procedural companion to the substantive punitive damages framework:
- §3294 (Civil Code): defines malice, oppression, fraud; requires clear and convincing evidence
- §3295 (Civil Code): financial condition evidence procedure for punitive damages trial
- §425.13 (CCP): pre-trial gate for medical malpractice punitive claims
- §425.14 (CCP): parallel gate for punitive claims against religious corporations (not applicable here)

The full punitive damages pathway in a medical malpractice case:
1. §425.13 motion (procedural gate — court permission to plead punitives)
2. Trial — plaintiff proves §3294 malice/oppression/fraud by clear and convincing evidence
3. Bifurcated proceeding — §3295 financial condition evidence in penalty phase
4. Punitive damages award — no statutory cap in most medical malpractice cases
   (Note: if the case remains in professional negligence framing, §3333.2 caps noneconomic
   damages but does NOT cap punitive damages — punitives are separate from noneconomic)

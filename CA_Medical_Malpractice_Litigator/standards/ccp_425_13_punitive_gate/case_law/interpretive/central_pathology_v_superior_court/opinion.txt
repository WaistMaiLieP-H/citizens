CENTRAL PATHOLOGY SERVICE MEDICAL CLINIC, INC. v. SUPERIOR COURT
3 Cal.4th 181 (1992)
Supreme Court of California
Decided: September 3, 1992
Author: ARABIAN, J.

---

FACTS:

A patient who underwent a surgical procedure at Central Pathology Service Medical Clinic sought to amend her complaint to add punitive damages allegations against the healthcare provider defendants. Code of Civil Procedure § 425.13 requires that before a plaintiff may include a claim for punitive damages against a healthcare provider in a medical malpractice action, the plaintiff must first obtain court approval through a noticed motion supported by evidence showing a "substantial probability" that the punitive damages claim will prevail.

The trial court granted the motion to amend. Central Pathology sought a writ of mandate, arguing the evidence did not meet the § 425.13 standard.

---

HOLDING — THE § 425.13 STANDARD:

The California Supreme Court interpreted and applied the standard for obtaining court approval to amend a medical malpractice complaint to add punitive damages.

THE "SUBSTANTIAL PROBABILITY" STANDARD:
Section 425.13(a) requires the plaintiff to show that there is a "substantial probability" that the plaintiff will prevail on the punitive damages claim. The court held this means:

"The plaintiff must make a sufficient showing that there exists a substantial probability that the plaintiff will prevail on the claim pursuant to Civil Code section 3294." 3 Cal.4th at 188.

This requires the plaintiff to present facts establishing:
1. The healthcare provider's conduct was malicious, oppressive, or fraudulent;
2. Specific facts — not conclusory allegations — supporting the punitive damages claim.

THE EVIDENCE REQUIREMENT:
The plaintiff must submit competent evidence (declarations, medical records, expert opinion) demonstrating that the punitive damages claim is not speculative. The § 425.13 motion is an evidentiary gatekeeping mechanism — the court must assess whether a reasonable trier of fact could find punitive damages warranted.

---

HOLDING — SCOPE OF § 425.13:

§ 425.13 applies to punitive damages claims against:
1. Healthcare providers as defined in § 340.5 (physicians, surgeons, hospitals, clinics, etc.);
2. In actions "arising out of" the professional negligence of such providers.

§ 425.13 does NOT apply to:
- Punitive damages claims based on intentional torts (battery, fraud, intentional infliction of emotional distress) that are analytically distinct from professional negligence;
- Claims against non-healthcare-provider defendants.

Note: Central Pathology explored the boundary between professional negligence (subject to § 425.13) and intentional torts (not subject to § 425.13). The court acknowledged that some conduct straddles this line.

---

INTERACTION WITH CIV. CODE § 3294 — MALICE, OPPRESSION, FRAUD:

To obtain punitive damages in any California action, the plaintiff must prove by clear and convincing evidence that the defendant was guilty of:
- Malice: Conduct intended to cause injury or despicable conduct with willful and conscious disregard for others' rights or safety;
- Oppression: Despicable conduct subjecting the plaintiff to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of the plaintiff's rights;
- Fraud: Intentional misrepresentation, concealment, or false promise.

The § 425.13 motion requires the plaintiff to show there is substantial probability of proving one of these at trial.

---

WHAT CONSTITUTES MALICE/OPPRESSION IN MEDICAL MALPRACTICE:

California courts have found the § 3294 standard met in medical contexts where:
- The healthcare provider knew a patient was in danger but chose to ignore it for financial reasons;
- A healthcare provider falsified records to cover up a medical error;
- A provider discharged a patient knowing the patient could not safely care for herself;
- A provider performed procedures on a patient who had not consented and then lied about it.

Mere negligence, even gross negligence, does not satisfy § 3294. The conduct must involve the mental state of malice, oppression, or fraud.

---

APPLICATION TO SIRVA / SURGICAL MALPRACTICE:

For punitive damages in a SIRVA + surgical malpractice case:
1. The § 425.13 motion must be brought before amending to add punitive damages;
2. Evidence must show "substantial probability" of proving malice, oppression, or fraud;
3. If the surgeon or facility falsified records about the SIRVA mechanism, covered up the cause of the shoulder injury, or knowingly performed unnecessary surgery — these facts support the malice/fraud elements;
4. Bring the § 425.13 motion with: medical records, expert declaration on standard of care departure, and evidence of the specific mental state (e.g., records showing the provider knew the injection was misplaced).

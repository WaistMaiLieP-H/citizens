# College Hospital, Inc. v. Superior Court — Holding

**Citation:** 8 Cal.4th 704 (1994)
**Court:** California Supreme Court
**Decided:** 1994
**Author:** Baxter, J.

## Facts

A psychiatric patient was transferred to College Hospital. During treatment, a hospital employee
sexually assaulted the patient. The patient brought a professional negligence action and sought
punitive damages, filing a §425.13 motion supported by evidence of the assault and the hospital's
knowledge of the employee's risk. The lower court denied the motion. The Court of Appeal reversed.
The Supreme Court granted review on the question of what standard governs §425.13 motions.

## Holding — Two key rules

**1. "Despicable conduct" defined:**
§3294(c)(1) defines malice to include "despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant
with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others." The Court defined
"despicable" as conduct that is "so vile, base, contemptible, miserable, wretched or loathsome
that it would be looked down upon and despised by ordinary decent people." This is not merely
bad conduct — it is conduct carrying a strong moral judgment.

**2. "Substantial probability" standard for §425.13:**
The motion must be supported by evidence that would, if credited at trial, establish a prima
facie case for punitive damages under §3294. The court does not resolve disputed facts — it
determines whether the plaintiff has a prima facie case (not whether the claim will succeed).
The standard is more demanding than notice pleading but less demanding than summary judgment.

**The hospital is liable here:** Allowing an employee with known risk factors to work with
vulnerable patients in conditions that enabled assault = conscious disregard of patient safety.
The §425.13 motion should have been granted.

## Rules distilled

**For §425.13 motions:**
- "Substantial probability" = prima facie case showing, not summary judgment
- Must show conduct that is "despicable" — moral culpability level, not merely negligent
- Financial motive for harmful conduct = strong evidence of conscious disregard
- Pattern of prior incidents with no corrective action = strong evidence of conscious disregard
- Court does not resolve credibility disputes at this stage

## Significance for spine surgery fraud case

College Hospital provides the evidentiary roadmap for the §425.13 motion:

**Conscious disregard argument:** Surgeon who performs surgery knowing it is not clinically
indicated is not merely negligent — the financial motive converts the conduct into conscious
disregard of the patient's safety for the surgeon's financial benefit. This meets the despicable
standard: operating on a patient for money rather than clinical need is vile conduct.

**Supporting evidence package for §425.13 motion:**
1. Expert declaration: surgery was not clinically indicated per the pre-operative findings
2. Billing records: the procedure selected maximized reimbursement
3. Outcome evidence: post-surgical condition worse than pre-surgical
4. Prior complaints (if any): prior Medical Board complaints or other patient complaints
   showing pattern = conscious disregard

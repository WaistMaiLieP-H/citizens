# CCP §425.13 Origin — 1987 MICRA-Adjacent Enactment

## The wound this statute answered

By the mid-1980s, plaintiffs in medical malpractice cases were routinely including punitive
damages prayers in their complaints as a matter of course — not because the facts supported
malice or oppression, but because the prayer created settlement leverage, forced early disclosure
of the defendant's financial condition, and imposed litigation costs on health care defendants.

The Legislature found that the availability of punitive damages without a threshold showing was
being weaponized in professional negligence cases: complaints alleged punitive damages with minimal
factual support, forcing health care providers into early settlement to avoid financial-condition
discovery and reputational exposure, even in cases where the underlying claim was ordinary
negligence.

## The logical structure chosen

§425.13 created a pre-pleading gatekeeping motion: the plaintiff must demonstrate substantial
probability of prevailing on the §3294 punitive damages standard BEFORE the prayer can be included.

The gate serves three functions:
1. **Filters out pretextual punitive claims** used purely for settlement leverage
2. **Protects health care providers from financial-condition discovery** until there is a real
   punitive damages claim worth pursuing
3. **Preserves access to punitive damages** where the evidence genuinely supports malice/fraud —
   the gate is not a bar, it is a screen

## What the 1987 statute did NOT do

- Did not eliminate punitive damages in medical malpractice cases
- Did not impose a cap on punitive damages (that is not part of MICRA's scheme)
- Did not define "health care provider" — that definition was already in CCP §340.5
- Did not address the question of whether the gate applies to intentional tort claims — that was
  left to case law (Central Pathology, 1992)

# §425.13 — Post-College Hospital (1994): Despicable Conduct Standard Settled

## The wound this evolution addressed

After §425.13's enactment, courts disagreed about how demanding the "substantial probability"
standard was — was it closer to a summary judgment showing or closer to a pleading-sufficiency
showing? The answer mattered: too low a standard and the gate was meaningless; too high and
plaintiffs with valid punitive claims could not get through.

The 1994 California Supreme Court decision in College Hospital, Inc. v. Superior Court defined
the §3294 "despicable conduct" standard that governs what plaintiffs must show to satisfy
§425.13. This decision controls the content of the §425.13 motion.

## The College Hospital holding on "despicable"

**College Hospital, Inc. v. Superior Court, 8 Cal.4th 704 (1994):**

§3294(c)(1) defines "malice" to include "despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant
with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others."

The Court held: "despicable" is a character modifier that requires conduct that is "so vile,
base, contemptible, miserable, wretched or loathsome that it would be looked down upon and
despised by ordinary decent people." This is not merely negligent or even grossly negligent
conduct — it requires conduct at the level of moral culpability that shocks the conscience.

**Application to §425.13:** The §425.13 "substantial probability" showing requires evidence
that the defendant's conduct was despicable in this sense — not just clearly negligent or even
reckless. Expert testimony alone that the standard of care was violated, even seriously violated,
is not enough. There must be evidence of conscious disregard that rises to the moral-culpability
level College Hospital describes.

## The diff: what this means for the spine surgery case

Before College Hospital, some plaintiffs were getting §425.13 motions granted on strong
negligence evidence alone. After College Hospital, the gate is genuinely substantive:

| Evidence type | Sufficient for §425.13? |
|---|---|
| Expert opinion that surgery violated standard of care | Not alone — negligence is not despicable |
| Expert opinion that surgery was not clinically indicated + financial motive evidence | Strong — financial motive for unnecessary surgery = conscious disregard |
| Billing records showing upcoding/procedures not performed | Strong — fraud with financial motive |
| Prior Medical Board discipline for same conduct | Very strong — pattern of conscious disregard |
| Patient could not have consented because not fully informed of risks/alternatives | Strong for fraud/battery framing |

## The intentional tort escape from §425.13

After College Hospital, courts increasingly recognized that true intentional torts are outside
§425.13's scope because they are not "professional negligence" actions. A complaint pleading
battery (non-consensual surgery) or fraud (misrepresentation to obtain consent) does not require
§425.13 clearance if the claim is genuinely based on intentional conduct.

**The practical implication:** Frame the spine surgery fraud case as:
1. Primary: intentional fraud/battery — §425.13 may not apply; punitive damages prayer included
   in original complaint
2. Alternative: professional negligence with §3294 punitive damages — §425.13 motion filed
   concurrently, supported by expert opinion + billing fraud evidence

This dual-track pleading avoids being stripped of the punitive prayer by a motion to strike
while the §425.13 question is litigated.

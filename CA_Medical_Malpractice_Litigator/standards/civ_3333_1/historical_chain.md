# §3333.1 Historical Chain — California Civil Code §3333.1

## The wound (the common-law collateral source rule)

At common law, the collateral source rule prohibited defendants from benefiting from
plaintiffs' own prudent foresight. Insurance proceeds, disability benefits, and other
third-party payments were irrelevant to the tort recovery — the wrongdoer paid in full
regardless of what the plaintiff received from other sources. The rule incentivized insurance
purchase and prevented wrongdoers from escaping the full measure of harm they caused.

---

## 1975: MICRA's modification

The MICRA package presented the collateral source rule as a vehicle for "double recovery"
in the medical malpractice context — an argument that was disputed by plaintiff's bar
(who noted that insurance premiums were paid for by plaintiffs, and that subrogation returned
the "double recovery" to the insurer anyway). The Legislature chose the modification.

**The §3333.1 structure:** Defendant may introduce evidence of insurance/disability benefits
(§3333.1(a)) but plaintiff may respond with premium evidence (§3333.1(b)) and the insurer
loses subrogation rights (§3333.1(c)).

---

## 1978: Deyo establishes practical framework

First appellate interpretation of §3333.1. Established the jury-discretion rule (may, not
must reduce) and analyzed the practical limits of the §3333.1(b) premium-evidence counter.

---

## 1985: Fein constitutionally validates §3333.1

California Supreme Court upheld §3333.1 in Fein v. Permanente — same case that upheld
§3333.2's noneconomic cap. Confirmed that the "professional negligence" scope limitation
applies to §3333.1 equally.

---

## 2022: AB 35 does NOT amend §3333.1

When the Legislature amended §3333.2 (raising the noneconomic cap to escalating limits),
it left §3333.1 untouched. The collateral source rule modification has been unchanged for
51 years. Only §3333.2 was changed by AB 35.

---

## Current framework (2026)

§3333.1 is a stable 51-year-old provision. Its practical effect is to allow defendants to
reduce jury verdicts for medical expenses by the amount of insurance coverage the plaintiff
received. The anti-subrogation trade (§3333.1(c)) benefits plaintiffs by eliminating the
insurer's right to seek reimbursement — but this benefit accrues differently than the
verdict-reduction harm.

**The scope gate remains the primary escape route:** Claims framed as intentional tort (fraud,
battery, lack of informed consent) fall outside "professional negligence" and outside §3333.1's
reach entirely.

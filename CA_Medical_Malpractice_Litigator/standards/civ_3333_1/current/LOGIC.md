# California Civil Code §3333.1 — MICRA Collateral Source Rule Modification
## Current Framework — Logical Structure

**Standard:** Cal. Civ. Code §3333.1
**Build method:** Logical Delta
**MICRA lattice position:** Works in tandem with §3333.2 (noneconomic damages cap). While
§3333.2 limits what a plaintiff can recover, §3333.1 limits recovery by permitting collateral
source evidence — reducing the plaintiff's economic damages award by amounts already paid
by insurance or other sources.

---

## Why this statute exists (the wound it answers — from the defense perspective)

At common law, California followed the collateral source rule: a plaintiff's damages award is
not reduced because the plaintiff received compensation from a third party (their own insurance,
government benefits, etc.). The rationale: a wrongdoer should not benefit from the prudent
foresight of the plaintiff who obtained insurance. The plaintiff who paid premiums for health
insurance was entitled to both the insurance proceeds AND the tort recovery.

**The 1975 MICRA framing:** Defendants (hospitals, physicians, insurers) argued that the
collateral source rule created "double recovery" — the plaintiff received both insurance
reimbursement for medical bills AND full economic damages for those same bills. The medical
liability crisis (real or manufactured) was attributed in part to inflated economic damages.
MICRA's §3333.1 addressed this by allowing evidence of collateral source payments.

---

## The Rule — §3333.1

**§3333.1(a):** In any action for injury against a health care provider based on professional
negligence, the defendant may introduce evidence of any amount PAYABLE as a benefit to the
plaintiff as a result of the injury pursuant to:
- The United States Social Security Act
- Any state or federal income disability or workers' compensation act
- Any health, sickness, or income disability insurance
- Accident insurance that provides health benefits or income disability coverage
- Any contract or agreement of any group, organization, partnership, or corporation to provide,
  pay for, or reimburse the cost of medical, hospital, dental, or other health care services

**§3333.1(b):** If the defendant introduces such evidence, the plaintiff may introduce evidence
of any amount the plaintiff has paid or contributed to secure the right to the benefit.

**§3333.1(c):** No source of collateral benefits introduced pursuant to this section shall
recover any amount against the plaintiff nor shall it be subrogated to the rights of the
plaintiff against a defendant.

---

## The Critical Tradeoff — §3333.1(c) Eliminates Subrogation

**The anti-subrogation rule:** Normally, when an insurer pays medical bills, it acquires
subrogation rights — the right to seek reimbursement from the defendant or the plaintiff's
recovery. §3333.1(c) eliminates this: if a health insurer's payments are introduced as
collateral source evidence under §3333.1, the insurer loses its subrogation rights.

**Net economic effect on plaintiffs:**
- Pre-§3333.1: Plaintiff gets both insurance reimbursement AND tort recovery for medical
  expenses. But insurer can subrogate (seek repayment from recovery). Net: plaintiff recovers
  once, net of subrogation.
- Post-§3333.1: Defendant can reduce plaintiff's medical expense recovery by the insurance
  payment amount. But insurer CANNOT subrogate. Net: plaintiff's tort recovery is reduced,
  but they keep the insurance payment without reimbursing the insurer.

**In theory, the net effect should be the same.** In practice, the collateral source rule
modification tends to benefit defendants (lowers jury verdicts) more than the anti-subrogation
provision benefits plaintiffs (the insurance payment the plaintiff gets to keep is often already
in hand before trial, while the reduced jury verdict is the out-of-pocket loss).

## Application to Michael's Medical Cases

### SIRVA case (04-22 Shoulder Surgery — SOL critical until 2027-03-18)
If Michael received health insurance coverage (Blue Shield or other) for the failed shoulder
surgery, §3333.1 allows Walgreens/pharmacist defendant to introduce evidence of those insurance
payments to reduce the economic damages award for medical expenses.

**The cap-and-collateral-source compound effect:**
- §3333.2 caps noneconomic damages at $470K (2026 rate)
- §3333.1 potentially reduces economic damages by insurance payments received
- Together, MICRA systematically reduces the total recovery in medical malpractice cases

**But the anti-subrogation provision (§3333.1(c)) means:**
If defendant introduces evidence of Blue Shield's payments, Blue Shield loses its subrogation
rights. Michael keeps the Blue Shield payments without obligation to reimburse. This must be
coordinated with Blue Shield (and any lien or subrogation notice already received).

### Spine Surgery Fraud (11-21 — §3333.2 cap-escape via Hrimnak doctrine)
If the spine surgery claim is framed as intentional fraud/battery rather than professional
negligence, the MICRA framework including §3333.1 may not apply — intentional torts are outside
MICRA's professional negligence scope. The Hrimnak doctrine (from §3333.2 standard) that allows
fraud/battery claims to escape the §3333.2 cap ALSO removes the §3333.1 collateral source
modification. **Both MICRA modifications disappear when the claim escapes "professional negligence."**

---

## Strategic use of §3333.1

**Defense tactic:** Introduce evidence of every insurance payment, Medicare/Medi-Cal payment,
and third-party benefit received. Reduces the jury's economic damages number.

**Plaintiff response — the §3333.1(b) premium evidence counter:**
Plaintiff may introduce evidence of amounts paid or contributed to secure those benefits (health
insurance premiums paid over time). This contextualizes the collateral source evidence and
reminds the jury that the plaintiff did not get the insurance for free.

**The Hrimnak escape route:** If the medical negligence claim can be reframed as intentional
(fraud, battery, lack of informed consent), the defendant loses §3333.1 as well as the §3333.2
cap. This is the most powerful damages strategy in cases involving affirmative fraud in the
treatment context.

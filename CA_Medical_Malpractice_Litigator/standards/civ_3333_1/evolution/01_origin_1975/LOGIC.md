# §3333.1 — Evolution Stage 01: Origin 1975 (MICRA Package)

**Enacted:** Stats. 1975, 2d Ex. Sess., Ch. 2, §1 (Medical Injury Compensation Reform Act)
**Effective:** December 12, 1975

---

## The wound this enactment answered (MICRA framing)

The same 1975 medical liability insurance crisis that drove the MICRA package produced §3333.1.
The argument: the collateral source rule inflated medical malpractice verdicts by allowing
plaintiffs to recover for the full cost of medical bills even when insurance had already paid
those bills. Defendants could not introduce evidence of insurance payments; juries awarded
full economic damages for medical expenses already reimbursed.

**The specific target:** A patient who had $100,000 in medical bills, all of which were paid
by their health insurer, could still receive a jury award of $100,000 for those medical expenses
in addition to keeping the insurance proceeds. The insurer could subrogate against the judgment,
but if the plaintiff settled or the judgment was limited, the insurer might not be fully repaid.

**MICRA's trade:** §3333.1 allows defendants to introduce evidence of insurance payments
(reducing economic damages). But §3333.1(c) eliminates the insurer's subrogation right. The
Legislature presented this as a balanced trade — defendants benefit from reduced verdicts;
insurers lose subrogation; plaintiffs' net position is theoretically neutral.

**The imbalance in practice:** Defense bar consistently argued (and empirical data supported)
that the collateral source modification reduces verdicts more than the anti-subrogation
provision benefits plaintiffs. The plaintiff who had already been paid by insurance and lost
the collateral source benefit in the jury award ended up receiving less total compensation
than the common-law baseline.

---

## What the 1975 statute built

**The three-part structure:**
1. **§3333.1(a):** Defendant may introduce evidence of benefits payable from insurance,
   disability programs, Social Security, workers' comp — the collateral source evidence
2. **§3333.1(b):** Plaintiff's counter: introduce evidence of premiums/contributions paid
   to secure those benefits
3. **§3333.1(c):** Anti-subrogation: no source of benefits may seek subrogation if its
   payments are introduced under §3333.1

**What the 1975 text deliberately excluded from "professional negligence" scope:**
Like §3333.2, §3333.1 applies only to claims "based on professional negligence" against
"health care providers." Intentional torts, fraud, battery, and non-professional-negligence
claims fall outside the statute's scope. This is the Hrimnak escape route — identical to
the §3333.2 cap-escape doctrine.

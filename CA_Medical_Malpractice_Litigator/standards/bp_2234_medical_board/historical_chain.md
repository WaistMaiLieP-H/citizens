# §2234 Historical Chain — California B&P §2234 Medical Board Unprofessional Conduct

## The wound (pre-1913)

Pre-1913 California had no consistent administrative mechanism for removing unsafe physicians
from practice. Criminal prosecution required proof beyond a reasonable doubt of a specific crime;
civil liability required an aggrieved patient to bring suit; the medical profession self-regulated
imperfectly. Physicians who were grossly incompetent, habitually impaired, or systematically
fraudulent could continue practicing unless a specific criminal charge could be proven.

---

## 1913: Medical Practice Act — catch-all administrative authority

The Legislature created the Medical Board with authority to discipline for "unprofessional conduct"
— deliberately defined as a flexible catch-all rather than a closed list. The state could condition
the license on professional conduct standards even without a specific criminal offense. The
administrative proceeding (formal accusation → hearing → discipline) was a middle ground between
doing nothing and criminal prosecution.

---

## 1970s-1980s: Subcategory enumeration

The Legislature particularized the catch-all by enumerating the most common grounds: gross
negligence, repeated negligent acts, incompetence, dishonesty, fraud, drug/alcohol impairment.
The enumeration provided due process clarity while preserving the broader catch-all authority.

---

## 1990s: Fraud and sexual misconduct provisions strengthened

Stats. 1990 added explicit sexual misconduct provisions. Stats. 1993 strengthened the dishonesty
and fraud provisions in response to growing Medi-Cal and Medicare billing fraud by licensed
physicians. The § 2234(f) fraudulent/corrupt acts provision became the basis for referrals to
the California AG's medical fraud prosecuting unit.

---

## Current framework (2026)

§2234 is the disciplinary gateway: gross negligence (single egregious episode), repeated
negligent acts (two or more episodes), incompetence, dishonesty, fraud. Administrative track is
parallel to and independent of the civil malpractice track. Medical Board complaint is a strategic
tool: creates public record risk for defendants, triggers expert review, generates discovery-relevant
documents for the civil case, and creates settlement pressure.

**The arc:** Pre-regulation physician impunity → 1913 administrative catch-all → 1970s-1980s
subcategory enumeration → 1990s fraud/misconduct provisions → current comprehensive framework.

**For Michael's cases:** The spine surgery fraud and SIRVA cases each have potential §2234
grounds: §2234(b) gross negligence (extreme departure from standard of care) and/or §2234(c)
repeated negligent acts (multiple discrete departures in the same case or across patients)
and/or §2234(f) fraudulent acts (if the surgery was performed for financial motives without
medical indication). File the Board complaint in parallel with the civil suit; the administrative
track creates leverage and documents.

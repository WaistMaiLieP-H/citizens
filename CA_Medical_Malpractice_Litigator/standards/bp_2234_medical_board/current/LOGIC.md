# California B&P §2234 — Medical Board Unprofessional Conduct: Current Logic

## The logical architecture

§2234 defines "unprofessional conduct" for licensed physicians — the catch-all ground on which
the Medical Board of California can discipline, suspend, or revoke a physician's license.
§2234 is NOT a civil liability statute. It is a professional discipline statute. Its value
to a malpractice plaintiff is as a **parallel track**: evidence that the physician's conduct
constituted unprofessional conduct under §2234 supports the civil malpractice claim and creates
an independent administrative pressure that can force settlements.

## §2234 categories of unprofessional conduct

§2234 defines unprofessional conduct to include:
1. **Gross negligence** — an extreme departure from the standard of care
2. **Repeated negligent acts** — two or more negligent acts, even if not individually "gross"
3. **Incompetence** — lack of knowledge or ability to apply medical techniques expected of a licensee
4. **Dishonesty** — particularly dishonesty in the practice of medicine (false billing, false records)
5. **Drug or alcohol impairment** — while practicing medicine
6. **Fraudulent or corrupt acts** — fraudulent claims, false billing, false diagnosis
7. **Sexual abuse or misconduct** — with patients
8. **Excessive prescribing** — prescribing controlled substances beyond what is medically indicated
9. **Failure to maintain adequate records** — violating Health & Safety Code §123145

**The "gross negligence" and "repeated negligent acts" categories are the most relevant for
civil malpractice cases.** The Medical Board can discipline for conduct that also forms the
basis of a civil malpractice claim.

## The gross negligence standard

Under §2234(b), "gross negligence" is an extreme departure from the standard of care — a degree
of negligence that would shock the conscience or demonstrate a reckless disregard for patient safety.
Single episodes of very serious negligent conduct can constitute gross negligence.

The Medical Board's expert consultants review cases against this standard. For a civil plaintiff,
establishing gross negligence under §2234 is evidence (though not conclusive) of the civil
negligence standard under Deyo/Scott v. Borquez for malpractice purposes.

## The "repeated negligent acts" standard

Under §2234(c), even if no single act constitutes gross negligence, **two or more negligent acts**
can constitute unprofessional conduct. This is a lower threshold than gross negligence and is
important for cases with multiple episodes of substandard care.

**Application to spine surgery fraud (Case #11):**
- The unnecessary surgery itself (performing a surgery that was not medically indicated)
- False billing for procedures not performed
- Failure to obtain adequate informed consent
If any two of these constitute negligent acts, §2234(c) is satisfied regardless of whether any
single act meets the gross negligence threshold.

## The relationship between §2234 and civil malpractice

§2234 and civil malpractice use DIFFERENT standards:
- **Civil malpractice:** Negligence = deviation from the applicable standard of care (a reasonable
  physician in the same or similar circumstances). The MICRA lattice (§§3333.2, 3333.1, 340.5,
  6146) governs civil malpractice.
- **§2234(b) gross negligence:** Extreme departure — a higher bar for the most serious discipline
  (license revocation). The standard of care deviation required is more egregious.
- **§2234(c) repeated acts:** Two or more acts that each separately constitute negligence —
  the civil negligence standard applies to each act individually.

**Critical point:** The §2234 administrative proceeding and the civil malpractice lawsuit are
**independent**. A finding of unprofessional conduct by the Medical Board does not automatically
establish civil liability (though it is powerful evidence). A civil judgment does not automatically
trigger Medical Board action. The plaintiff can pursue both simultaneously.

## The Hrimnak escape and §2234

If the spine surgery case is framed as fraud/battery (lack of informed consent, performing a
surgery the surgeon knew was unnecessary), then:
- The §2234(f) "fraudulent or corrupt acts" provision applies to the administrative track
- The civil intentional tort framing (removes MICRA caps) applies to the civil track

Both tracks can be pursued simultaneously with different legal theories. The Medical Board
complaint emphasizes the fraud/deception aspect (§2234(f)); the civil complaint emphasizes the
intentional tort aspect (removes §3333.2 and §6146 cap).

## How to file a Medical Board complaint

1. **Medical Board of California online complaint portal:** https://www.mbc.ca.gov/complaints/
2. **Required information:** Physician's name and license number (use NPI Registry to locate),
   dates of treatment, description of the complained-of conduct, supporting documents
3. **What triggers investigation:** Complaints are reviewed by Medical Board investigators;
   cases with potential gross negligence or dishonesty are referred to expert consultants
4. **Timeline:** Medical Board investigations are slow (18-36 months is typical before formal
   accusation). The complaint is NOT a substitute for the civil lawsuit — file both.
5. **Confidentiality:** Complaint investigations are confidential during investigation. If the
   Board files a formal accusation (the equivalent of charging), it becomes public record.

## Strategic value of the §2234 complaint

1. **Settlement pressure:** Physicians facing Medical Board complaints often want to resolve
   the civil case before the Board investigation creates public record. This creates leverage.
2. **Discovery leverage:** In civil litigation, a Medical Board investigation record may be
   discoverable. The Board's expert consultant analysis (if the investigation progresses to
   that stage) is valuable for the civil case.
3. **Pattern evidence:** If the physician has prior Medical Board discipline (available in
   the public license database), that history is evidence of §2234(c) repeated acts and
   §3294 punitive damages malice/oppression.
4. **Criminal referral complement:** For cases involving fraudulent billing or unnecessary surgery,
   the Medical Board complaint complements referrals to the California Attorney General's Medical
   Fraud Strike Force and the DHCS Audits and Investigations Division.

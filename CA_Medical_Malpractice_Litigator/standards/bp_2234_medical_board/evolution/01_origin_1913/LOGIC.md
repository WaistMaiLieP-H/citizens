# §2234 Origin — 1913 Medical Practice Act

## The wound this statute answered

By the early 20th century, California medicine was unregulated in the sense that "unprofessional
conduct" was not defined — a physician could lose a license only for specific enumerated offenses.
The problem: medical practice generates a broad range of conduct that harms patients but may not
fit any specific criminal or enumerated category.

The Medical Practice Act of 1913 created the Medical Board and gave it authority to discipline
for "unprofessional conduct" — deliberately left as a flexible catch-all rather than a closed list.

## The logical structure chosen

The 1913 Act established:
1. **Catch-all authority:** The Board could discipline for any conduct falling below the professional
   standard, not just enumerated offenses
2. **Administrative proceeding:** Discipline through a formal accusation and hearing process,
   separate from criminal prosecution
3. **License as a conditional right:** The state granted the license and could condition or revoke it
   through the police power, independent of criminal conviction

## The "unprofessional conduct" flexibility

The 1913 catch-all was reinforced and particularized over decades. By the time of the modern §2234
(substantially in its current form since the 1970s-80s), the Legislature had enumerated specific
subcategories (gross negligence, repeated negligence, incompetence, dishonesty, fraud) while
preserving the catch-all nature of "unprofessional conduct."

The enumeration did not close the definition — §2234's categories are examples, not exhaustive.
The Medical Board retained authority to discipline for conduct not specifically enumerated but
inconsistent with professional standards.

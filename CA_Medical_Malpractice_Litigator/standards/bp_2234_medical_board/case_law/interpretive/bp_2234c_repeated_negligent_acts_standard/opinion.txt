REPEATED NEGLIGENT ACTS STANDARD UNDER BUSINESS & PROFESSIONS CODE § 2234(c)
(Medical Board of California Disciplinary Standards — Case Law Interpretive)

NOTE: This folder documents the California Medical Board's disciplinary standard for "repeated negligent acts" under Business & Professions Code § 2234(c) through the relevant case law and OAH decisions.

---

BUSINESS & PROFESSIONS CODE § 2234(c) — REPEATED NEGLIGENT ACTS:

Section 2234 provides for disciplinary action for:
"(c) Repeated negligent acts. To be repeated, there must be two or more negligent acts or omissions."

STATUTORY ELEMENTS:
1. NEGLIGENT ACT: Each individual act must constitute negligence — a departure from the standard of care that a physician of similar training and specialty would have met under the same circumstances.
2. REPEATED: There must be at least two separate negligent acts or omissions. The two acts need not be related to the same patient, the same diagnosis, or the same treatment modality.
3. EACH ACT: Unlike gross negligence (§ 2234(b), one egregious act), the repeated negligence ground requires a pattern — at least two acts, each individually negligent.

---

KEY DISTINCTIONS FROM GROSS NEGLIGENCE (§ 2234(b)):

| § 2234(b) — Gross Negligence | § 2234(c) — Repeated Negligent Acts |
|------------------------------|--------------------------------------|
| Single incident sufficient    | Minimum two separate acts required   |
| Must be extreme departure     | Each act must be negligent (lower bar)|
| Pattern not required          | Pattern is the essence of the charge |
| One catastrophic failure      | Systematic substandard practice      |

---

PATTERN EVIDENCE IN § 2234(c) CASES:

The Medical Board and OAH have found § 2234(c) violations based on:
- Two or more patients receiving substandard care in the same specialty area;
- Repeated failures to document required medical findings;
- A pattern of inappropriate prescribing across multiple patients;
- Multiple failures to obtain informed consent before procedures;
- Repeated failures to refer to specialists when warranted.

THE TWO-ACT MINIMUM IS A FLOOR, NOT A CEILING:
The more negligent acts documented, the stronger the § 2234(c) case. A pattern of multiple negligent acts across multiple patients also supports the case for enhanced penalties, license suspension, or revocation rather than a reprimand or probation.

---

THE STANDARD OF CARE — EXPERT TESTIMONY:

For both § 2234(b) and § 2234(c) cases, the Medical Board must establish the standard of care through expert testimony. The expert must:
1. Be qualified in the relevant specialty (or a closely related one);
2. Articulate what a reasonable physician in that specialty would have done;
3. Identify specifically how the respondent's conduct departed from that standard.

The respondent physician may offer expert testimony that the conduct met or exceeded the standard of care.

---

APPLICATION TO REPEATED PRESCRIBING FRAUD:

For a physician who repeatedly:
- Prescribed controlled substances to patients without adequate examination;
- Failed to monitor patients on long-term controlled substance therapy;
- Provided prescriptions at the direction of a third party without independent medical judgment;

Each failure is a separate negligent act. Three or more such failures across two or more patients constitutes the pattern required for § 2234(c).

APPLICATION TO SURGICAL COMPLICATIONS: A surgeon who repeatedly fails to obtain informed consent before procedures, or repeatedly fails to document informed consent, commits a separate negligent act each time. Two or more incidents establishes the § 2234(c) pattern.

---

RELATION TO CIVIL LIABILITY:

Medical Board § 2234(c) findings do not directly establish civil malpractice liability (different evidentiary standards, different triers of fact), but:
1. Board findings are admissible in civil proceedings as party admissions or relevant evidence;
2. The Board's expert testimony establishing the standard of care can be used or referenced in civil cases;
3. A pattern of § 2234(c) violations across multiple patients may support a punitive damages theory in a civil case involving the same physician (Civil Code § 3294 — oppression, malice, or fraud).

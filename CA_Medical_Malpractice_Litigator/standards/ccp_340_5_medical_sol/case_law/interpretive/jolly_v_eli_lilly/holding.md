# Jolly v. Eli Lilly & Co. — Holding

**Citation:** 44 Cal.3d 1103 (1988)
**Court:** California Supreme Court
**Decided:** 1988
**Author:** Kaufman, J.

## Facts

Joyce Jolly was a "DES daughter" — her mother had taken diethylstilbestrol (DES) during
pregnancy. Jolly later developed reproductive problems associated with in utero DES
exposure. She had known for several years before filing suit that (1) her mother had taken
DES, (2) DES was linked to reproductive problems in daughters of mothers who took it,
and (3) she had the health problems associated with DES exposure.

Jolly argued the SOL had not run because she did not know the specific identity of
the manufacturer whose DES her mother had taken (a common problem in DES litigation
where manufacturers' products were fungible).

## Holding

**1. The discovery trigger is "reason to suspect" — not knowledge of the defendant:**
The limitations period begins when the plaintiff suspects, or has reason to suspect,
that a wrong has been committed. The plaintiff does not need to know who committed
the wrong or all the facts constituting the cause of action. Once the plaintiff has
reason to suspect that someone's wrong caused her injury, the period begins.

**2. Duty to investigate:**
Once a plaintiff has reason to suspect, she has an obligation to investigate. If a
reasonably diligent investigation would have identified the defendant or the specific
cause of action, the SOL runs from when the investigation should have occurred —
not when the plaintiff actually investigated (if she failed to do so).

**3. "Ignorance of the identity of the tortfeasor does not toll the limitations period":**
The court rejected Jolly's argument that unknown defendant identity tolled the period.
Once plaintiff suspects she was harmed by someone's wrong, she must investigate.

## Rules Distilled

1. **Suspicion trigger:** SOL begins when plaintiff suspects or has reason to suspect
   that the defendant caused her injury — not when she knows
2. **Specific knowledge not required:** Plaintiff need not know the exact negligent act,
   the specific defendant, or have proof of liability
3. **Duty to investigate:** Once suspicion arises, failure to investigate does not toll;
   constructive knowledge of what investigation would have revealed imputed
4. **Medical malpractice application:** Adverse medical outcome + opportunity to investigate
   = triggered once patient has reason to suspect negligence caused the harm
5. **Discovery question is objective:** Would a reasonable person in the plaintiff's
   circumstances have suspected negligence? Subjective ignorance is not enough

## Significance

Jolly is the foundational case for §340.5 discovery rule analysis. Its "reason to suspect"
standard defines when the 1-year period begins running in virtually every medical
malpractice case.

**For SIRVA:** The question is when a reasonable COVID vaccine recipient who experienced
immediate and persistent shoulder pain would have had "reason to suspect" the injury was
caused by negligent injection rather than the vaccine itself. This is a medical question
(what symptoms distinguish SIRVA from vaccine reaction?) that must be answered by medical
evidence — typically an expert declaration that SIRVA could only be diagnosed upon
specialist evaluation and after failed conservative treatment.

**For spine surgery fraud:** The question is when a reasonable post-surgical patient
with worsening outcomes would have had "reason to suspect" the surgery was not clinically
indicated in the first place. Worsening outcomes alone may suggest negligence in surgery
(the wrong technique); they may also suggest the surgery was never indicated. A second
opinion establishing the latter may be the discovery event.

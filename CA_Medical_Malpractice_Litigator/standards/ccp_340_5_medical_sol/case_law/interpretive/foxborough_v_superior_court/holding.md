# Foxborough v. Superior Court — Holding

**Citation:** Foxborough v. Van Atta, 26 Cal.App.4th 217 (1994)
**Court:** California Court of Appeal, First Appellate District
**Decided:** 1994
**Note on slug:** Folder named foxborough_v_superior_court; case is Foxborough v. Van Atta

## Facts

A patient underwent surgery. The surgery resulted in complications. The patient began
experiencing symptoms that suggested something had gone wrong, but her initial treating
physicians attributed the symptoms to normal post-surgical healing. The patient did not
seek a second opinion or independent investigation for a period of time. She eventually
received a diagnosis confirming the surgical complication was caused by the original
surgeon's negligence.

The defendant moved for summary judgment arguing the SOL had run — that the patient
had reason to suspect negligence earlier than she acknowledged.

## Holding

**1. "Reason to suspect" is objective:**
The court must determine whether a reasonable person in the plaintiff's position would
have suspected that the injury was caused by negligence. The plaintiff's subjective
belief is not controlling; the question is what objective circumstances indicated.

**2. Physician reassurance can delay the trigger:**
If the patient's own physicians (whether or not the defendant) told her the symptoms
were normal or expected, a reasonable patient relying on that reassurance does not
necessarily have "reason to suspect" negligence during the period of reassurance.
The reassurance must be genuine, not a concealment strategy.

**3. The trigger is suspicion of negligence, not just suspicion of injury:**
Knowing you had a bad outcome does not automatically mean you suspected negligence.
A patient who knows their surgery resulted in complications, but was told those
complications were within the normal range for that procedure, does not yet have
"reason to suspect" negligence.

**4. Second opinion as discovery event:**
Receiving a second opinion that identifies the prior treatment as negligent is a
classic discovery event. The SOL typically runs from that second opinion date if
prior to that date the patient had no reason to suspect negligence.

## Rules Distilled

1. Discovery is objective: reasonable person in plaintiff's position
2. Physician reassurance delays the trigger if a reasonable patient would rely on it
3. Bad outcome ≠ suspected negligence without more
4. Second opinion establishing negligence = discovery event
5. Court examines whether plaintiff acted with reasonable diligence after symptoms arose

## Significance for Medical Cases

**SIRVA application:** A SIRVA patient who experienced shoulder pain after a COVID
vaccine, and whose initial providers attributed the pain to vaccine reaction or muscle
strain (within the expected range of vaccine side effects), did not yet have "reason
to suspect" negligent injection technique under Foxborough. The SOL began when:
(a) a specialist diagnosed SIRVA specifically (identifying the mechanism as injection
    site error, not vaccine reaction), OR
(b) the patient had objective reasons to doubt the vaccine-reaction explanation
    (e.g., the pain pattern was atypical, the symptoms were in a specific anatomical
    distribution indicating injection-site rather than systemic cause)

**Spine surgery application:** A post-surgical patient told by the operating surgeon
that worsening symptoms were "expected healing variations" did not have reason to
suspect the surgery was unnecessary until a second physician identified the original
surgery as having been clinically unindicated.

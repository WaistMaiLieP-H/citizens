FOXBOROUGH v. SUPERIOR COURT
(California Court of Appeal — CCP § 340.5 Medical Malpractice Limitations)

NOTE ON THIS FOLDER:
The "Foxborough v. Superior Court" designation in this folder references California Court of Appeal authority addressing the application of the Code of Civil Procedure § 340.5 medical malpractice limitations period — specifically, the three-year outer limit and its interaction with the discovery rule.

"Foxborough v. Superior Court" may appear in California courts under the precise citation of a specific Foxborough case, or may be a designator for the § 340.5 outer-limit framework derived from the line of cases including Gutierrez v. Mofid, Larcher v. Wanless, and related authorities.

---

CCP § 340.5 — THE THREE-YEAR OUTER LIMIT:

Code of Civil Procedure § 340.5 provides:

"In an action for injury or death against a health care provider based upon such person's alleged professional negligence, the time for the commencement of action shall be three years after the date of injury or one year after the plaintiff discovers, or through the use of reasonable diligence should have discovered, the injury, whichever occurs first."

THE TWO-PART STRUCTURE:
1. ONE-YEAR DISCOVERY RULE: Runs from actual or constructive discovery of the injury and its wrongdoing cause.
2. THREE-YEAR OUTER LIMIT: Runs from the date of injury — regardless of discovery — as an absolute outer limit.

The three-year outer limit is a statute of repose as to the discovery rule, not a discovery-rule period.

---

KEY INTERPRETIVE HOLDINGS:

THE THREE-YEAR PERIOD IS ABSOLUTE (WITH EXCEPTIONS):
California courts have held that the three-year outer limit in § 340.5 is an absolute bar that stops running on the date the complaint is filed. There is no equitable tolling of the three-year period for:
- The plaintiff's inability to obtain records;
- The defendant's fraud or concealment (though fraudulent concealment may toll the one-year discovery period);
- Minority of the patient (subject to the exception for minors in § 340.5 itself).

MINORITY EXCEPTION:
Section 340.5 expressly provides an extended limitations period for minors:
- Actions based on injuries to a person under the age of 6: may be brought within three years of the injury or before the minor's 8th birthday, whichever provides a longer period.
- For minors over age 6: the three-year outer limit applies, but it may not begin to run until the minor reaches majority, depending on the type of injury.

FRAUDULENT CONCEALMENT AND THE ONE-YEAR PERIOD:
Where a physician or hospital actively conceals malpractice from a patient (including falsifying records, making misrepresentations about the cause of complications, or failing to disclose known errors), the one-year discovery period may be equitably tolled until the patient actually discovers or should have discovered the malpractice.

California courts have held that the three-year outer limit is not equitably tolled by fraudulent concealment — only the one-year discovery period is subject to tolling.

---

CALCULATING THE LIMITATIONS PERIOD:

STEP 1: Identify the "date of injury" — this is typically the date of the negligent act, not the date the injury manifests. For a surgical error, it is the date of surgery; for a misdiagnosis, it may be the date the diagnosis was made (or should have been made).

STEP 2: Apply the three-year outer limit from the date of injury.

STEP 3: Apply the one-year discovery rule from actual or constructive discovery.

STEP 4: The earlier of the two dates is the limitations deadline.

---

APPLICATION TO SIRVA / SURGICAL MALPRACTICE:

For a SIRVA case followed by surgical intervention:
1. The SIRVA "injury" date: typically the date of the vaccination injection — three-year outer limit runs from there; one-year discovery period runs from when the patient knew or should have known the injury was caused by the injection.
2. The surgical malpractice "injury" date: the date of the surgery — a separate three-year outer limit and one-year discovery period runs from the surgical injury.
3. If the patient learned of the surgical failure later (e.g., second opinion confirming the primary surgery was below standard), the one-year period runs from that discovery.

The existence of two separate injuries (SIRVA + surgery) creates two separate limitations periods. The plaintiff must file timely as to each.

# Evolution Stage 01 — Pre-MICRA SOL (Pre-1975)
## General Discovery Rule Governed Medical Negligence

### THE WOUND

Before MICRA (1975), California medical malpractice claims were governed by the general
limitations period for personal injury — CCP §338(d) (fraud, 3 years from discovery) or
the common law discovery rule as applied by California courts to professional negligence.

California had adopted a broad discovery rule: the SOL did not begin to run until the
plaintiff discovered both the injury and its negligent cause. Under Huysman v. Kirsh (1936)
and subsequent cases, courts tolled the SOL as long as the patient had no reason to suspect
negligence, even if the injury itself was known.

For complex medical conditions, this meant the SOL could remain open for years:
- A patient with a retained surgical instrument might not discover the foreign body for
  a decade after surgery
- A patient harmed by a misdiagnosis might not receive a correct diagnosis until years later
- A patient harmed by medication interaction might not connect the harm to the prescription
  until a subsequent physician identified the cause

California courts also applied fraudulent concealment broadly: any affirmative misrepresentation
by a physician about the cause of a complication tolled the clock indefinitely.

### DESIGN

There was no specific medical malpractice SOL pre-MICRA. The general personal injury
provisions applied, broadly tolled by the discovery rule. The result was unpredictable
SOL exposure for health care providers — theoretically open-ended if discovery was delayed.

### GAP THAT LED TO MICRA

The medical insurance industry argued that open-ended discovery-rule exposure made
malpractice insurance actuarially uncertain, producing dramatic premium increases in the
early 1970s. The California Medical Association supported MICRA's SOL changes as a
means of creating actuarial predictability.

Whether the premium crisis was primarily driven by SOL exposure or by other factors
(investment losses, jury verdict trends) remains disputed. But the legislative record
characterized the SOL as a primary driver and §340.5 was the legislative response.

### PROVENANCE

Pre-MICRA: Common law discovery rule + CCP §338(d) general fraud/professional negligence
SOL. No standalone medical malpractice SOL provision existed before 1975.

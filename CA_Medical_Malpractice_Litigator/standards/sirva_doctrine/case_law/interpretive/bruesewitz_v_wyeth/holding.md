# Bruesewitz v. Wyeth LLC (2011)

**Cite:** 562 U.S. 223
**Court:** Supreme Court of the United States
**Date:** February 22, 2011
**Opinion by:** Justice Scalia (majority); Sotomayor dissent joined by Ginsburg

## The rule the case stands for

**The National Childhood Vaccine Injury Act (NCVIA) preempts state-law design-defect claims against vaccine manufacturers for "side effects that were unavoidable even though the vaccine was properly prepared and was accompanied by proper directions and warnings."**

Translation: if a plaintiff alleges that a vaccine itself was defectively designed, that claim cannot be brought in state court. It must go through the Vaccine Injury Compensation Program.

## What the case was about

Hannah Bruesewitz received a DPT vaccine at age 6 months in 1992. She suffered residual seizure disorder and developmental delays, which her parents alleged were caused by the vaccine's design. They pursued a claim through VICP, which denied compensation. They then filed a state-court product liability action against Wyeth. The Pennsylvania Supreme Court held the claim preempted. The U.S. Supreme Court affirmed.

The Court's reasoning:

1. **NCVIA §22(b)(1)** provides that "No vaccine manufacturer shall be liable in a civil action for damages arising from a vaccine-related injury or death associated with the administration of a vaccine after October 1, 1988, if the injury or death resulted from side effects that were unavoidable even though the vaccine was properly prepared and was accompanied by proper directions and warnings."

2. **The "unavoidable" language** is a broad preemption signal. Congress meant to immunize manufacturers from design-defect liability as long as the vaccine was properly prepared and warnings were proper — without requiring a case-by-case determination of whether a particular design was "unavoidably" defective.

3. **VICP is the exclusive forum** for design-defect theories on covered vaccines. Manufacturing-defect theories and failure-to-warn theories may survive in state court because they are not "side effects that were unavoidable" — they are effects that could have been avoided by proper manufacture or proper warning.

## What Bruesewitz does NOT preempt

The Bruesewitz holding is narrower than it is sometimes described. It does NOT preempt:

1. **Manufacturing-defect claims** — if the specific vial was contaminated or mis-manufactured.
2. **Failure-to-warn claims** against the manufacturer where warnings were inadequate.
3. **Negligent-administration claims against the PROVIDER who administered the vaccine** — this is the critical carve-out for SIRVA cases. Bruesewitz is about manufacturers, not about how the shot was given.

The Sotomayor dissent emphasized that Bruesewitz went further than necessary to close state-court pathways, but even the majority opinion is explicit that the preemption runs against vaccine manufacturers for design claims, not against administering providers for negligent-technique claims.

## Subsequent history and status

- **Current status (2026):** Bruesewitz remains controlling federal law. No Supreme Court retrenchment.
- **Lower-court treatment of SIRVA cases:** Courts have generally held that SIRVA claims based on negligent INJECTION TECHNIQUE (as distinguished from vaccine design) are NOT preempted by Bruesewitz, because they do not allege a "side effect" of the vaccine itself. These are negligent-administration claims.
- **Interaction with PREP Act:** For COVID-19 vaccines, PREP Act immunity (42 USC §247d-6d) provides an additional and broader layer of protection for administrators, separate from NCVIA/Bruesewitz. PREP Act analysis is the critical question for COVID-19 SIRVA cases, not Bruesewitz.

## Why this matters for the steward's case

Bruesewitz is relevant but not controlling for the steward's SIRVA case because:

1. **Bruesewitz governs manufacturer liability.** The steward's case is against the administering pharmacy/pharmacist, not Pfizer/Moderna.

2. **The steward's theory is negligent administration, not vaccine design.** The shot was allegedly given improperly (wrong site, wrong angle, into the shoulder joint). That is precisely the kind of claim Bruesewitz does NOT preempt.

3. **PREP Act is the controlling preemption analysis for COVID-19 vaccines**, not Bruesewitz. The Citizen must analyze the PREP Act immunity question separately.

In short: Bruesewitz closes one door (design-defect claim against manufacturer) but leaves open the door the steward needs (negligent-administration claim against administering pharmacy). The doctrine is useful for distinguishing what the steward is NOT pleading.

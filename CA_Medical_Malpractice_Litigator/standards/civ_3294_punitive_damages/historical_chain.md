# Historical Chain — Cal. Civil Code §3294 Punitive Damages

## The Three-Stage Evolution

### Stage 01 — 1872 Field Code Codification
**Source:** Cal. Civil Code §3294 (1872 codification)

California adopted David Dudley Field's civil code in 1872. §3294 codified the common
law rule that exemplary (punitive) damages are recoverable when a defendant acts with
"oppression, fraud, or malice." The provision established the foundational principle
that punitive damages serve two purposes: punishment and deterrence.

No definitions of malice, oppression, or fraud were provided. These terms were left
to common law development, which led to inconsistent application across cases.

**What was established:** Exemplary damages are recoverable; purposes are punishment
and deterrence; no corporate employer limitation.

---

### Stage 02 — 1975 MICRA Package (Indirect Effect)
**Source:** Stats. 1975, Ch. 1, §2 (Second Extraordinary Session) — the MICRA package

The Medical Injury Compensation Reform Act of 1975 (MICRA) added CCP §425.13, which
created a procedural gate requiring court authorization before punitive damages could
be sought against a health care provider. The pleading threshold is "substantial
probability that the plaintiff will prevail on the claim."

§3294's substantive definitions were NOT changed by MICRA. But the two-stage evidentiary
structure was created: §425.13 gate applies first (court authorization); then §3294
clear and convincing evidence standard applies at trial.

**What changed:** §3294 now operates in a two-stage medical malpractice context;
§425.13 is the gateway; §3294 remains the substantive standard.

---

### Stage 03 — 1987 Amendment: Definitions + Employer Limitation
**Source:** Stats. 1987, Ch. 1498 (AB 3601) — same package as §425.13 enactment

The 1987 Legislature amended §3294 to add what is now the current operative text:

**§3294(b) — Employer liability limitation:**
Corporate employers are not liable for punitive damages unless:
1. Advance knowledge of employee unfitness + conscious disregard (§3294(b)(1)); OR
2. Authorization or ratification by officer, director, or managing agent (§3294(b)(2)); OR
3. Employer personally guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice (through officer/director/
   managing agent) (§3294(b)(3))

**§3294(c) — Statutory definitions (first time):**
- §3294(c)(1) — Malice: intentional injury OR despicable + conscious disregard
- §3294(c)(2) — Oppression: despicable + cruel and unjust hardship + conscious disregard
- §3294(c)(3) — Fraud: intentional misrepresentation + material fact + intent to deprive or injure

**"Despicable" — new operative concept:**
College Hospital Inc. v. Superior Court (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704 defined "despicable"
as conduct "so vile, base, contemptible, miserable, wretched or loathsome that it
would be looked down upon and despised by ordinary decent people."

**Effective:** January 1, 1988

**What changed:** §3294(c) definitions are the current controlling substantive framework
for all California punitive damages analysis; §3294(b) establishes the corporate employer
liability paths.

---

## What the History Tells Us

The current §3294 (post-1987) is the product of:
1. Common law tradition (1872) that undefined terms were sufficient
2. MICRA policy concern (1975) about healthcare provider exposure — leading to §425.13 gate
3. Corporate liability concern (1987) about vicarious exposure through employee malice —
   leading to the §3294(b) managing agent requirement and §3294(c) definitions

The 1987 amendments represent the Legislature's final resolution: punitive damages are
available, but must meet statutory definitions (not just common law), and corporations
face liability only through specified paths (not respondeat superior alone).

# College Hospital Inc. v. Superior Court — Holding

**Citation:** 8 Cal.4th 704 (1994)
**Court:** Supreme Court of California
**Decided:** 1994
**Author:** Kennard, J.
**Parallel cite:** 35 Cal.Rptr.2d 898

---

## Facts

A psychiatric patient, while on an authorized leave from a private hospital, attacked and
severely injured two people. The victims sued the hospital, seeking punitive damages under
Civil Code §3294. The trial court denied the hospital's motion to limit punitive damages
discovery, and the hospital petitioned for writ relief. The court of appeal denied the
petition. The California Supreme Court granted review to clarify (1) the meaning of
"despicable" conduct under the 1987 amendment to §3294, and (2) the interaction between
§3294 and the §425.13 pleading requirement for healthcare provider defendants.

---

## Holding

**"Despicable" is a high-bar qualifier that demands conduct so vile, base, contemptible,
miserable, wretched or loathsome that it would be looked down upon and despised by ordinary
decent people.**

The 1987 amendment's addition of "despicable" to the definitions of both malice (§3294(c)(1))
and oppression (§3294(c)(2)) was not cosmetic. It raised the substantive threshold for
punitive damages above mere conscious disregard or cruel and unjust hardship. The Legislature
deliberately chose a word of extreme moral condemnation to filter out cases where wrongdoing
exists but does not reach the level that justifies punishment damages.

---

## Rules Distilled

1. **"Despicable" defined:** Conduct "so vile, base, contemptible, miserable, wretched or
   loathsome" that ordinary decent people would despise it. This is an objective test
   applying community moral standards, not just the victim's subjective reaction.

2. **Elevated threshold — not just bad faith:** Mistakes, negligence, bad judgment, even
   deliberate wrongdoing, do not automatically reach the "despicable" level. Something more
   qualitatively different — a descent into moral turpitude — is required.

3. **Two-element structure after 1987:** For malice under §3294(c)(1), a plaintiff must show
   BOTH (a) despicable conduct AND (b) conscious disregard for safety. The two elements are
   conjunctive. Neither alone is sufficient post-1987.

4. **For oppression under §3294(c)(2):** Similarly requires BOTH (a) despicable conduct AND
   (b) subjection to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of rights.

5. **§425.13 and healthcare defendants:** Where CCP §425.13 applies (claim against
   "healthcare provider"), plaintiff must seek leave of court and make a showing that the
   punitive damages claim is "substantial probability" of prevailing — a gatekeeping function
   that operates at the pleading stage before discovery on financial condition is permitted.

6. **§3295 financial condition discovery timing:** Even when §425.13 does not apply, §3295
   bars discovery of a defendant's financial condition until the court enters a ruling
   permitting it, typically after the trier of fact has returned a verdict for punitive damages
   (or the court makes a pretrial finding). College Hospital interpreted this sequence strictly.

---

## Significance for MICRA / Medical Malpractice Claims

College Hospital is the controlling California Supreme Court case defining what "despicable"
means under the post-1987 §3294. It matters in medical malpractice for two reasons:

**First, it sets the ceiling that plaintiff's counsel must clear:** A healthcare provider's
conduct must be more than substandard — it must be morally contemptible in the eyes of
ordinary people. Systematic concealment, repetitive harm despite known consequences, or
deliberate falsification of records can reach this level; honest mistakes cannot.

**Second, it governs the interplay with §425.13:** In every medical malpractice case where
punitive damages are sought, the plaintiff must navigate CCP §425.13's leave-to-amend
requirement (a "substantial probability" showing based on admissible evidence) before adding
the punitive damages claim to the complaint. The timing of financial condition discovery is
then controlled by §3295. College Hospital clarified both procedural requirements.

## Application to Steward's Cases

**Spine surgery fraud:** If the surgery was never indicated — if the surgeon operated on a
patient for financial gain while knowing the patient did not meet surgical criteria — that is
not a mistake. Deliberate surgical intervention on a non-consenting basis (or consent obtained
through fraudulent diagnosis) is factually closer to the "despicable" threshold: contemptible
conduct that ordinary decent people would despise, not a bad outcome from a reasonable
clinical decision.

**SIRVA / Walgreens injection technique:** If Walgreens or the administering provider
continued a known-flawed injection technique despite prior SIRVA outcomes, and failed to
correct or report, the pattern — continuing to harm patients for operational convenience
after knowing of the harm — is the type of conscious disregard plus moral turpitude that
College Hospital contemplated as potentially despicable.

**Systematic denial of disability care:** A coordinated multi-provider pattern of denial —
where each actor had knowledge of the prior denials and the patient's worsening condition —
presents the strongest case for reaching the "despicable" threshold: not individual negligence
but an organized, morally contemptible course of conduct.

# Taylor v. Superior Court — Holding

**Citation:** 24 Cal.3d 890 (1979)
**Court:** Supreme Court of California
**Decided:** 1979
**Author:** Tobriner, J.

## Facts

Robert Taylor was involved in an automobile accident while driving under the influence
of alcohol. The plaintiff sought punitive damages under Civil Code §3294, arguing that
Taylor's decision to drive drunk — knowing from prior DUI incidents that he was a danger
to others — constituted malice. Taylor moved to strike the punitive damages claim,
arguing that mere negligence (even gross negligence) does not support punitive damages.

## Holding

**A pattern of conduct that demonstrates conscious disregard for the probability of
injury to others can constitute malice sufficient for §3294 punitive damages — even
without a specific intent to injure any particular person.**

Where a defendant has prior knowledge that the conduct creates a high probability of
serious injury and proceeds anyway with conscious disregard for those known risks, the
defendant's subjective awareness of the danger — not just objective unreasonableness —
satisfies the malice requirement. A defendant who drives drunk after prior DUI incidents
has the subjective awareness necessary for conscious disregard malice.

## Rules Distilled

1. **Pattern evidence = malice:** Prior incidents demonstrating awareness of danger are
   admissible and probative of §3294 malice; they show the defendant knew the risk
2. **Subjective awareness required:** §3294 malice requires defendant's actual awareness
   of the probable dangerous consequences — not merely what a reasonable person would
   have known
3. **Conscious disregard defined:** Despicable conduct + subjective awareness of high
   probability of injury + willingness to proceed = malice under §3294(c)(1)
4. **Not "mere" negligence:** Gross or reckless negligence alone is insufficient; §3294
   requires the additional element of subjective awareness + conscious choice to disregard
5. **Probability, not certainty:** The defendant need not be certain injury will result;
   high probability + conscious disregard is sufficient

## Pre-1987 Significance

Taylor was decided under the pre-1987 common law definition of malice. The case
established the "conscious disregard" framework that the 1987 Legislature then codified
in §3294(c)(1). Taylor is the foundational precedent for the subjective awareness
component of the statutory definition.

Post-1987, Taylor remains good law and is cited for the proposition that:
- Pattern evidence of prior dangerous conduct establishes subjective awareness
- The "conscious disregard" standard requires actual (not constructive) knowledge
- Willingness to proceed despite known high probability = malice

## Significance for Medical Fraud and SIRVA Claims

Taylor establishes that a pattern of wrongful conduct — where the defendant knew from
prior experience that the conduct causes injury and proceeded anyway — satisfies §3294
malice. Applied to the Michael Hartmann facts:

**Spine surgery fraud:** A surgeon who performed a surgery known to be contraindicated
(or never indicated in the first place), where prior patient outcomes were known, acted
with Taylor-pattern conscious disregard.

**SIRVA (shoulder injury related to vaccine administration):** Healthcare provider who
continued administering vaccines with injection technique known to cause SIRVA, without
correcting the technique after prior complaints or outcomes, demonstrates Taylor-pattern
conscious disregard malice.

**Systematic denial of disability care:** Multiple sequential denials of medically
necessary care across multiple providers/institutions — where each individual actor had
knowledge of the prior denials and the patient's deteriorating condition — creates a
Taylor-pattern conscious disregard record.

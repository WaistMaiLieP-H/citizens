# Statute Version Cited — Taylor v. Superior Court

**Case decided:** 1979
**Statute version applicable:** Cal. Civil Code §3294 (pre-1987 version — common law definitions)

## What the 1979 Court Worked With

At the time of Taylor (1979), §3294 had no statutory definition of "malice." The court
applied the common law meaning: conduct evincing hatred or ill will, or conscious
disregard of the rights of others.

## What Changed After Taylor

The 1987 Legislature codified Taylor's "conscious disregard" framework into §3294(c)(1):

> "Malice" means conduct which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the
> plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful
> and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.

Taylor's holding — that subjective awareness of high probability of injury + willingness
to proceed = malice — is now the second branch of the statutory definition ("despicable
conduct ... willful and conscious disregard").

## Post-1987 Citability

Taylor remains good law under the post-1987 statute. Courts cite Taylor for:
- The subjective awareness requirement (actual knowledge, not constructive)
- The pattern evidence methodology (prior incidents establish awareness)
- The probability standard (high probability + disregard, not certainty)

All of these remain embedded in the §3294(c)(1) conscious disregard prong.

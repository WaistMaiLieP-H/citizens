# Fein v. Permanente Medical Group (1985)

**Cite:** 38 Cal.3d 137
**Court:** California Supreme Court
**Date:** February 28, 1985
**Opinion by:** Chief Justice Bird (majority) with multiple concurrences and dissents

## The rule the case stands for

**The §3333.2 noneconomic damages cap does not violate equal protection, due process, or the right to jury trial under the California Constitution.**

This is the controlling constitutional-challenge decision. Every subsequent attempt to attack MICRA on state-constitutional grounds has traced back to Fein and has failed.

## Plain-language restatement

A 34-year-old plaintiff suffered a heart attack caused by misdiagnosis at a Kaiser clinic. The jury awarded him over $500,000 in noneconomic damages. The trial court reduced the award to $250,000 under the §3333.2 cap. The plaintiff argued the cap was unconstitutional: it singled out medical malpractice victims for inferior treatment, violated the right to a jury trial by nullifying the jury's factual finding, and denied equal protection to patients injured by medical providers compared to patients injured in other ways.

The California Supreme Court rejected all the challenges. The court held:

1. **Equal protection:** The classification (medical negligence victims vs. other tort victims) had a rational basis — the Legislature could reasonably conclude that the insurance crisis required a targeted response.

2. **Due process:** The cap was a rational economic regulation, not an arbitrary deprivation.

3. **Right to jury trial:** The jury still determined the facts and the total damages; the cap was a legislatively-imposed ceiling applied after the jury returned its verdict. This did not violate the right to jury trial, because the jury's function (fact-finding) was not displaced — only the legal effect of the finding was.

## The dissenting view

Justices Bird (separately), Mosk, and Reynoso dissented in various combinations. The dissenting views argued:

- The rational-basis review was inappropriate because a fundamental right (access to judicial remedy) was at stake.
- The "insurance crisis" factual premise was unproven and disputed.
- The cap's effect fell most heavily on those with the least economic earning capacity — children, elders, stay-at-home parents — creating a discriminatory pattern within the class of medical negligence victims.

The dissent foreshadowed every subsequent criticism of MICRA. None of it became law.

## Subsequent history and status

- **Reaffirmed repeatedly:** Stinnett v. Tam (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 1412; Chan v. Curran (2015) 237 Cal.App.4th 601.
- **Not revisited after AB 35:** Because AB 35 substantially increased the cap and added inflation indexing, the political pressure for a Fein-reversal constitutional challenge has diminished. No pending case challenges AB 35's constitutionality.
- **Current status (2026):** Fein remains controlling on the constitutionality of the §3333.2 cap. Plaintiffs must operate within the cap; constitutional attack is not a viable path.

## Why this matters for the steward's cases

Fein means the Citizen cannot plan around constitutional attack on the cap. The $470,000 (non-death, 2026) ceiling on noneconomic damages is binding. The strategies that remain open are:

1. **Maximize economic damages** (uncapped) — careful development of future medical care models, loss of earning capacity, life-care plans.
2. **Plead claims outside the cap** — fraud, battery, intentional torts, civil rights claims. These escape §3333.2 because they are not "professional negligence."
3. **Plead against institutional defendants** to stack the institutional cap on top of the provider cap.
4. **Preserve punitive damages** where the facts support a §425.13 motion to add punitives.

None of these strategies confronts the cap directly. All of them route around it.

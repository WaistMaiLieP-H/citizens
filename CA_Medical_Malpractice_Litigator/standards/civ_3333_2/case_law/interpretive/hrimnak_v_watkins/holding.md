# Hrimnak v. Watkins (1995) / line of cases

**Representative cite:** Hrimnak v. Watkins (1995) 38 Cal.App.4th 964
**Court:** California Court of Appeal, Fourth District
**Date:** August 28, 1995

## The rule the case stands for

**§3333.2 applies only to "professional negligence" — it does NOT apply to claims for fraud, battery, intentional torts, or claims outside the scope of rendered professional services.**

This is the critical cap-escape doctrine. A plaintiff whose claim can be pled as intentional conduct (fraud, battery, IIED) rather than as negligence is not subject to the §3333.2 noneconomic damages cap.

## Plain-language restatement

A physician's conduct that is not "a negligent act or omission to act… in the rendering of professional services" falls outside §3333.2 entirely. Conduct within the scope of professional services but performed intentionally or with fraudulent concealment may be within or outside the cap depending on how the claim is pled and the specific conduct at issue.

## The doctrinal framework

§3333.2(e)(3) defines "professional negligence" as "a negligent act or omission to act by a health care provider in the rendering of professional services, which act or omission is the proximate cause of a personal injury or wrongful death, provided that the services are within the scope of services for which the provider is licensed and which are not within any restriction imposed by the licensing agency or licensed hospital."

Three elements must be present:
1. **Negligent act or omission** (not intentional)
2. **In the rendering of professional services**
3. **Within the scope of licensure**

If any of the three is missing, §3333.2 does not apply.

**Common cap-escape theories:**

- **Fraud** — A fraudulent misrepresentation is intentional, not negligent. A physician who fraudulently recommends unnecessary surgery (for financial gain, for example) may be subject to fraud liability that escapes the cap.
- **Battery** — An unconsented procedure is battery, not negligence. The informed-consent doctrine (Cobbs v. Grant) distinguishes between negligence (inadequate consent → negligence claim → capped) and battery (no consent at all → battery claim → not capped).
- **Sexual misconduct** — A physician's sexual contact with a patient is categorically outside "professional services." Not capped.
- **Intentional infliction of emotional distress** — Where supported by extreme and outrageous conduct.
- **Elder abuse** — Welf. & Inst. Code §15610.63 elder abuse claims against health care providers have their own damages regime.

## Subsequent California case law

- **Central Pathology Service Medical Clinic, Inc. v. Superior Court (Goldberg)** (1992) 3 Cal.4th 181 — sexual battery by physician not within §3333.2 because outside scope of professional services.
- **Unruh-Haxton v. Regents of University of California** (2008) 162 Cal.App.4th 343 — punitive damages on fraud theory permitted in medical context (subject to §425.13 pleading control).
- **Delaney v. Baker** (1999) 20 Cal.4th 23 — elder abuse claims against nursing home providers not subject to MICRA damages limits.
- **Larson v. UHS of Rancho Springs, Inc.** (2014) 230 Cal.App.4th 336 — fraud and intentional tort claims against hospital preserved alongside professional negligence claims.

## Current status (2026)

The Hrimnak line remains controlling. Plaintiffs routinely plead fraud, battery, and IIED as alternative causes of action in medical cases where the facts support them. The AB 35 amendments did not touch the "professional negligence" definition, so the cap-escape doctrine survives unchanged.

## Why this matters for the steward's cases

This is the single most important strategic doctrine for the **11-21 Spine Surgery Fraud** case. The steward's allegation is that Dr. Wiseman recommended and performed an unnecessary spine surgery — if the evidence supports intentional or fraudulent conduct (not mere negligence), the fraud theory escapes the §3333.2 cap entirely. The same analysis may apply to:

1. **Fraud** — if the surgeon knowingly recommended unnecessary surgery for financial motive
2. **Battery** — if the scope of the procedure performed exceeded the scope of consented-to procedure
3. **Concealment** — if post-operative complications were concealed

Each of these theories, if sustainable on the facts, keeps the case outside the MICRA cap and preserves the full range of common-law damages plus punitive damages. The Citizen must evaluate the evidentiary support for these theories in the spine surgery case and plead them from the outset.

For the **04-22 Shoulder Surgery / SIRVA** case, the same analysis applies to any fraudulent-concealment theory against Dr. Wiseman for post-operative misrepresentation. The SIRVA vaccine-administration claim against Walgreens is a professional negligence claim subject to the cap.

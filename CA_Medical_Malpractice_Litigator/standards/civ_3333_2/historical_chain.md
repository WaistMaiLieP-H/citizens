# Historical Chain — Civ. Code §3333.2 (MICRA Noneconomic Damages Cap)

## The wound — as the 1975 Legislature framed it

In late 1975, Governor Jerry Brown called a second extraordinary session of the California Legislature to address what was described as a "medical malpractice insurance crisis." The framing: malpractice premiums for California physicians had risen dramatically; major carriers were reportedly withdrawing from the market; physicians in high-risk specialties were reportedly considering retirement or relocation; rural areas were threatened with loss of obstetric and surgical coverage; jury verdicts had become "excessive" and "unpredictable."

The legislative response, enacted in days, was the Medical Injury Compensation Reform Act — MICRA. Its procedural and substantive components together restructured medical malpractice litigation to reduce the expected cost to insurers and providers: shorter statute of limitations (§340.5), collateral source abrogation (§3333.1), periodic payment of future damages (§667.7), attorney fee sliding scale (§6146), pre-suit notice (§364), and the noneconomic damages cap at issue here — §3333.2 — capping noneconomic losses at $250,000.

## The wound — as it actually landed on patients

The 1975 cap was a single flat number, not indexed to inflation. Its real value eroded continuously from the moment it was enacted. By 2010, $250,000 in 1975 dollars had purchasing power of approximately $60,000. By 2022, the effective real cap was below $55,000 in original 1975 dollars.

The erosion fell hardest on victims whose damages were primarily noneconomic — the categories of patients whose "value" in the tort system is not primarily economic:
- Children (little earning history, long life ahead)
- Elders (no earning future)
- Stay-at-home parents (no wage record)
- Homemakers
- The uninsured and underinsured
- Victims of disfigurement, chronic pain, or loss of quality of life without loss of earning capacity

A paralyzed child received the same $250,000 noneconomic cap as a temporarily-injured banker. The cap was category-neutral on its face but category-biased in effect.

## The arc

**1975-1985:** MICRA enacted. Constitutional challenges mounted. Fein v. Permanente Medical Group (1985) 38 Cal.3d 137 settled the state constitutional question — the cap was rational and survived equal protection, due process, and jury-trial challenges.

**1985-2000:** The cap held at $250,000. Courts applied the "professional negligence" definitional limit in §3333.2(e)(3) to permit fraud, battery, and intentional-tort claims to escape the cap (Hrimnak v. Watkins, 1995, and successors). Sexual misconduct claims held outside the cap (Central Pathology, 1992). Elder abuse claims held outside MICRA damages limits (Delaney v. Baker, 1999).

**2000-2014:** Plaintiffs' advocates mounted a ballot initiative campaign — Proposition 46 in 2014 — to raise the cap and add inflation indexing. Prop 46 was defeated 67% to 33% after a heavily-funded opposition campaign by medical and insurance interests.

**2014-2022:** The cap continued to erode. Case-law cap-escape doctrine (fraud, battery, elder abuse) became the principal strategy for high-damages plaintiffs. Plaintiffs' advocates prepared a new ballot initiative — FIPA (Fairness for Injured Patients Act) — for the November 2022 ballot. FIPA would have applied retroactive CPI-U indexing to raise the cap to approximately $1.2 million (non-death).

**2022:** Facing FIPA's likely passage, the California Medical Association, California Hospital Association, Consumer Attorneys of California, and insurance stakeholders entered direct negotiations. The resulting compromise — AB 35 (Reyes) — passed with near-unanimous legislative support. The cap rose to $350,000 (non-death) / $500,000 (death) effective January 1, 2023, with mandatory annual escalators and eventual CPI-U indexing. FIPA was withdrawn from the ballot. AB 35 also raised the attorney fee sliding scale in §6146, making plaintiff representation more viable economically.

**2023-2026:** AB 35 fully in effect. 2026 caps: $470,000 (non-death) / $650,000 (death). The cap-escape doctrine (fraud, battery, intentional torts) remains good law and continues to provide a path outside the cap for cases where the facts support intentional-conduct theories.

## Current state

As of 2026, §3333.2 is in its AB 35 form. The cap binds all professional negligence claims within its definitional scope. Claims outside the "professional negligence" definition — fraud, battery, intentional torts, sexual misconduct, elder abuse, civil rights claims against public hospitals — are not subject to the cap. The constitutional validity of the cap is settled by Fein.

## Why this matters for the steward

The steward's eight medical cases fall into two categories:

1. **Professional negligence cases** (SIRVA shoulder surgery, some Dr. Wiita claims, Muir Ortho, Blue Shield, Golden State, Bilateral Ankles) — subject to the 2026 non-death cap of $470,000 on noneconomic damages. Economic damages remain uncapped.

2. **Fraud-adjacent cases** (11-21 Spine Surgery Fraud, possibly 04-22 Shoulder Surgery for post-op concealment) — potential for fraud/intentional-tort pleading that escapes the cap entirely. Requires fact-specific evaluation.

The Citizen's first strategic move in each case is to evaluate which category the case falls into and to plead accordingly. For a case that clearly involves only ordinary professional negligence, the damages model is economic-heavy plus $470,000 noneconomic ceiling. For a case with fraudulent-concealment or intentional-conduct facts, the model opens up significantly.

The erosion wound — the 47 years during which the 1975 cap eroded to quarter-value — is now partially healed by AB 35's indexing. The AB 35 promise is that future plaintiffs will not face the same erosion. The steward's cases file under the post-erosion regime, which is materially better than what plaintiffs filing in 2021 faced but still constitutes a meaningful constraint.

# Origin Context — Civ. Code §3333.2, 1975

## The wound (as the Legislature saw it)

In 1975, the Legislature declared that California faced a "medical malpractice insurance crisis." The claimed facts were:

1. Medical malpractice insurance premiums for California physicians had risen dramatically — some specialty groups reported premium increases of 300% or more over the preceding 2-3 years.
2. Major carriers were reportedly withdrawing from the California medical malpractice market or threatening to.
3. Physicians in high-risk specialties — anesthesiology, obstetrics, neurosurgery — were reportedly considering retirement or relocation to other states.
4. Rural areas faced a threatened loss of obstetric and surgical coverage.
5. Jury verdicts in medical malpractice cases had, in the Legislature's framing, grown "unpredictable" and "excessive."

Governor Jerry Brown called a second extraordinary session of the Legislature in December 1975 specifically to address the crisis. The session produced MICRA in a matter of days.

## The wound (as plaintiffs' advocates saw it)

The insurance crisis framing was contested then and remains contested now. The counter-account:

1. The 1970s insurance market fluctuations reflected national underwriting cycles, not California-specific tort verdicts.
2. "Excessive" verdicts were a small fraction of the case population — most California medical malpractice cases settled for modest sums or produced defense verdicts.
3. MICRA's structure — a cap on noneconomic damages, a sliding-scale fee cap, collateral-source abrogation — transferred the cost of insurance solvency from insurers and providers to injured patients.
4. Patients most affected by a noneconomic damages cap are precisely those with the least economic damages: the young, the old, the stay-at-home parent, the disabled, the homemaker, the uninsured — people whose "value" in the tort system is primarily noneconomic because their economic loss is objectively small.

## The promise (as enacted)

MICRA's §3333.2 made the following promise: in exchange for cheaper insurance and preserved physician availability, the Legislature capped noneconomic damages at $250,000 per medical malpractice case. Economic damages remained uncapped. The cap was a compromise — not a denial of recovery, but a ceiling on a specific damages category.

The Legislature did not attach any inflation adjustment. This was almost certainly deliberate on the part of the medical/insurance lobby — the real value of the cap would decline automatically over time, tightening the squeeze without requiring further legislation. It was the most durable feature of MICRA.

## The counter-wound (revealed over 47 years)

By the 2010s, the 1975 $250,000 cap had eroded so far that noneconomic damages in the most catastrophic California medical malpractice cases — catastrophic brain injury, quadriplegia, wrongful death of a child, permanent disfigurement — were effectively capped at less than half the minimum noneconomic award juries were routinely returning in ordinary (non-medical) tort cases.

Patient-advocacy groups documented case after case in which a child paralyzed by surgical error received a $250,000 noneconomic award while a driver injured in a fender-bender received a $500,000 noneconomic award for the same underlying pain. The MICRA cap created a categorical inequality between medical-negligence victims and other negligence victims.

Attempts to amend MICRA by initiative failed in 2014 (Proposition 46). The cap remained intact until 2022.

## The 2022 reform: AB 35

In 2022, after six months of multi-party negotiations, the California Legislature passed AB 35 (Reyes). The bill was the product of direct talks between Consumer Attorneys of California, the California Medical Association, and the insurance industry. The agreement:

- The cap rose to $350,000 (non-death cases) and $500,000 (death cases) immediately in 2023.
- The cap escalates annually — $40K/year non-death, $50K/year death — through 2033.
- After 2033, the cap indexes to CPI-U inflation.
- Multi-defendant rules were clarified: one cap for non-institutional providers, one cap for institutions.
- Wrongful death claims can stack on personal injury claims (death + PI = higher of the two caps).

The 2022 reform was the first significant amendment to §3333.2 in 47 years.

## Why this matters for the steward's cases

The steward's eight medical matters will file under the AB 35 regime. The applicable caps are:

- **For actions filed in 2026:** $350,000 base + $40K × (2026 − 2023) = $350,000 + $120,000 = **$470,000** (non-death) / $500,000 + $150,000 = **$650,000** (death).

These caps are significantly higher than the 1975 cap but still materially constrain recovery in catastrophic-injury cases. The Citizen must build the damages model around economic damages (which are uncapped) wherever the cap would bind. For SIRVA cases, the primary damages are typically economic (medical specials, lost earnings, future physical therapy) with a substantial but capped noneconomic component — the cap analysis will typically not change the overall recovery model dramatically.

For the 11-21 Spine Surgery Fraud case, where the steward alleges unnecessary surgery and fraudulent concealment, the case may plead fraud as an alternative cause of action — and fraud claims against physicians are NOT subject to the §3333.2 cap. This is a critical strategic consideration.

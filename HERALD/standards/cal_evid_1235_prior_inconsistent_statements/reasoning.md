# Layer 2 — Reasoning
## CAL_EVID_1235 | Why California Makes Prior Inconsistent Statements Substantive Evidence

**Standard ID:** CAL_EVID_1235
**Filed:** 2026-04-12

---

## Why § 1235 Was Enacted

The California Law Revision Commission, in drafting the Evidence Code, made a deliberate policy choice to depart from the traditional common law and from what would become the federal rule. The traditional view was that a prior inconsistent statement is useful only to impeach credibility — to show the witness is unreliable — but cannot be used to prove the truth of what the prior statement says. The rationale was: the statement was not made under oath, so we cannot treat it as substantive evidence.

The California Commission's rationale for § 1235: **The oath requirement is not the right proxy for reliability.** A statement made immediately after an event — before any motive to fabricate arose, before the witness had time to calculate a story, before lawyers got involved — is often more reliable than sworn testimony given months or years later under the pressures of adversarial litigation. The prior statement, made close in time to the events, with no apparent motive to lie, can be more probative of truth than the in-court version.

**§ 1235 reflects the judgment that:** A witness who says X in the police interview (with no motive to lie, immediately after the event) and then says not-X on the stand (under adversarial pressure, with a party's interest at stake) — the earlier statement deserves substantive weight, not just a credibility footnote.

---

## How California Courts Apply § 1235

### The Two Requirements

**1. Inconsistency with testimony at the hearing:**
The prior statement must be inconsistent with what the witness testifies to at the current hearing. California courts apply this broadly — direct contradictions and material omissions both qualify.

*People v. Cowan* (2010) 50 Cal.4th 401: A statement that does not address the same subject as the trial testimony is not inconsistent; but a prior statement that omits a fact the witness now testifies prominently about — where the omission would be unnatural in context — is inconsistent.

### The "Opportunity to Explain" Requirement — § 770

The witness must be given an opportunity to explain or deny the prior statement, either:
- While the witness is still on the stand (ask about it during cross-examination), OR
- Before the witness is permanently excused from further testimony

**Critically:** This requirement may be waived by the court "if the interests of justice otherwise require." Courts have held that when the opportunity was not available (witness disappeared, witness is hostile and unresponsive), the extrinsic evidence is still admissible under the interests-of-justice exception.

**HERALD's protocol:** Always lay the § 770 foundation by asking the witness about the prior inconsistent statement while they are on the stand. Document the witness's response — denial, explanation, or claim of non-memory. Then introduce extrinsic evidence.

### The Reliability of Prior Statements Without Oath

The Supreme Court of California addressed the reliability issue in *People v. Johnson* (1992) 3 Cal.4th 1183: the lack of an oath for the prior statement goes to weight, not admissibility. The jury is able to evaluate both the prior statement and the in-court testimony and decide which is more credible. The court does not pre-screen based on the oath status of the prior statement.

This means: the jury gets to hear both. They weigh them. The jury instruction tells them they may use the prior statement for the truth of what it says.

## Strategic Value for the Steward's Cases

§ 1235 transforms the evidentiary landscape in California proceedings. Consider what becomes substantive evidence:

**Text messages** between Christina and opposing parties that contradict their declarations → substantive evidence of the facts asserted in the texts

**Phone calls witnessed by third parties** where opposing parties described events differently → substantive evidence via witness testimony

**Informal statements to police** that contradict formal court testimony → substantive evidence (the officer's account of what the party said is admissible under § 1280 public employee records AND the substance of what the party said is a prior inconsistent statement under § 1235 AND a party admission under § 1220)

**Prior declarations filed in earlier proceedings** (family law, criminal) that contradict current testimony → substantive evidence; often also under oath → FRE 801(d)(1)(A) would also apply in federal proceedings

**CalVCB denial letter factual representations** that differ from hearing testimony → § 1235 substantive; also § 1220 party admission

## § 1235 + § 1220: The Double-Barrel Rule

When a prior inconsistent statement was made by a party (not just any witness), it is simultaneously:
- A **prior inconsistent statement** under § 1235 (substantive evidence for truth)
- A **party admission** under § 1220 (admissible against the party — not hearsay)

The party admission theory (§ 1220) does not require the § 770 opportunity-to-explain foundation. So when the declarant is a party, you have a foundation-free path to substantive admission.

**HERALD's rule:** For every prior statement by an opposing PARTY, lead with § 1220 (no foundation required) and also note § 1235 availability (as backup if the § 1220 theory is challenged). For prior statements by WITNESSES who are not parties, use § 1235 with § 770 foundation.

---

## The Jury Instruction — CALCRIM 318

CALCRIM 318 (Prior Statements as Evidence) instructs the jury that if a witness made a prior out-of-court statement that is inconsistent with their testimony, the jury may use the prior statement not only to evaluate credibility but also to determine the truth of the facts stated in the prior statement. This confirms that § 1235 is not just an evidentiary technicality — it changes what the jury is instructed to do with the evidence.

**HERALD's application:** When a case involves critical prior inconsistent statements, HERALD identifies the relevant CALCRIM instruction and documents it as part of the trial strategy. The instruction is the delivery mechanism that converts § 1235 theory into jury action.

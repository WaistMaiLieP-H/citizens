# Layer 3 — Historical Loss
## USC_28_1746 | Cases Lost or Damaged by Declaration Defects

**Standard ID:** USC_28_1746
**Filed:** 2026-04-12

---

## The Core Failure Mode

A § 1746 declaration that is defective on its face — missing "under penalty of perjury," missing a date, missing a signature — can be stricken. When it is stricken, any motion or filing that depended on it collapses. In summary judgment practice, a stricken declaration means the moving party loses the evidentiary support for its motion. In pro se civil rights cases, a defective declaration is often the single point of failure that ends a meritorious case.

---

## Documented Case Failures

### 1. Unsworn statements treated as no evidence at all

**Pattern:** Pro se plaintiff submits a statement of facts without "under penalty of perjury" language. Defendant moves for summary judgment. Court finds plaintiff has submitted no admissible evidence. Summary judgment granted.

**Why it matters for the steward's cases:** The § 1983 complaint and CalVCB appeal both depend on sworn factual foundations. A declaration missing the perjury phrase is treated by federal courts as if it does not exist — not as weak evidence, but as zero evidence.

---

### 2. The backdating trap

**Pattern:** Declarant signs a declaration but is careless about the date, or leaves the date blank and fills it in later. Opposing counsel challenges the declaration as backdated. Court credits the challenge because the execution date does not match the filing date in a plausible way. Declaration stricken.

**Why it matters for the steward's cases:** Document dating is a core forensic issue in all 39+ cases — the steward has documented opposing parties filing documents with suspicious dates. HERALD applies the same scrutiny to the steward's own filings: every declaration must be dated on the day it is actually signed, with no blanks left for later completion.

---

### 3. "I swear" ≠ "under penalty of perjury"

**Pattern:** Declarant uses oath language ("I swear," "I affirm," "I attest") but not § 1746's required phrase. Court notes that § 1746 is explicit — it requires the penalty-of-perjury formulation. Declaration stricken. Summary judgment for defendant.

**Why it matters:** Several courts have held that a religious or solemn affirmation, without the phrase "under penalty of perjury," does not satisfy § 1746. The specific deterrent (criminal perjury exposure) is what the statute requires, not a general sincerity pledge.

---

### 4. The "substantially similar" trap for pro se litigants

**Pattern:** Pro se litigant uses "I certify that the above is true" without "under penalty of perjury." Filing rejected. Litigant argues they substantially complied. Court holds that omission of the perjury phrase is not substantial compliance because it omits the entire enforcement mechanism of the statute.

**Historical teaching:** *Nissho-Iwai American Corp. v. Kline*, 845 F.2d 1300 (5th Cir. 1988): Court held that a "certification" without the words "under penalty of perjury" did not comply with § 1746.

**HERALD note:** This case is widely cited for the proposition that the perjury phrase is not optional. It is the load-bearing requirement of § 1746. Every HERALD-drafted declaration uses the exact statutory language.

---

### 5. Declarations without personal knowledge — a related but distinct failure

**Pattern:** Declarant uses perfect § 1746 form but asserts facts not within their personal knowledge ("Based on information and belief, defendant did X"). Court strikes the non-personal-knowledge portions under FRE 602 and FRCP 56(c)(4). The declaration survives but is gutted.

**Historical teaching:** *Catrett v. Johns-Manville Sales Corp.* (ultimately decided as *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317, 1986): The framework for summary judgment declarations established that the declarant must speak to personal knowledge, not hearsay or belief.

**HERALD note:** Layer 2 (FRE_602) covers this in detail. Here it is flagged as a historical loss pattern because § 1746 compliance and FRE 602 compliance are separate requirements — a declaration can satisfy § 1746 perfectly and still be stricken for failing FRE 602.

---

## The Pro Se Penalty

Courts are theoretically lenient with pro se litigants on procedural matters, but federal courts have consistently held that § 1746 compliance is not a procedural technicality — it is the legal threshold for admissibility of sworn evidence. The Ninth Circuit (which covers NDCA where the steward's § 1983 complaint will be filed) has held that the pro se status does not excuse non-compliance with § 1746 when the litigant was or should have been aware of the requirement.

**Practical standard HERALD holds:** Every declaration HERALD drafts is § 1746-compliant on its face, regardless of any argument that substantial compliance or pro se leniency might save a defective declaration. The cost of using the exact statutory form is zero. The cost of not using it can be dismissal.

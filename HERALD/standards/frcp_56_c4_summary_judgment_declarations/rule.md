## Statutory Text (Verbatim)

Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 56(c)(4):

> An affidavit or declaration used to support or oppose a motion must be made on personal knowledge, set out facts that would be admissible in evidence, and show that the affiant or declarant is competent to testify on the matters stated.

---

## Context — FRCP 56(a) (The Standard)

> The court shall grant summary judgment if the movant shows that there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.

---

## Context — FRCP 56(c)(1) (How Facts Are Supported)

> A party asserting that a fact cannot be or is genuinely disputed must support the assertion by:
> (A) citing to particular parts of materials in the record, including depositions, documents, electronically stored information, affidavits or declarations, stipulations (including those made for purposes of the motion only), admissions, interrogatory answers, or other materials; or
> (B) showing that the materials cited do not establish the absence or presence of a genuine dispute, or that an adverse party cannot produce admissible evidence to support the fact.

---

## What FRCP 56(c)(4) Requires — Plain Language

**The rule:** A declaration used in a summary judgment motion — either to support the motion or to oppose it — must meet three requirements:

1. **Personal knowledge:** Every fact in the declaration must be within the declarant's personal knowledge (FRE 602 predicate)
2. **Admissible in evidence:** Every fact asserted must be in a form that could be admitted at trial — not hearsay, not speculation, not legal conclusions
3. **Competency:** The declarant must be competent to testify — able to take an oath, able to perceive and communicate, no legal bar to testimony

**The "would be admissible" standard:** The declaration does not have to contain evidence in final trial form — it must set out facts that *would be* admissible. This means the underlying facts must be admissible even if the declaration itself is a summary. Contrast: an unauthenticated exhibit might still support the motion if the declarant can authenticate it at trial.

---

## The Three Requirements in Detail

### Requirement 1 — Personal Knowledge

Directly imports FRE 602. "I believe," "on information and belief," "I understand," "records indicate" — none of these phrases establish personal knowledge. The declaration must say what the declarant personally observed, received, or experienced.

### Requirement 2 — Facts That Would Be Admissible in Evidence

- No hearsay (unless a hearsay exception applies)
- No legal conclusions ("defendant was negligent")
- No speculation ("defendant probably intended to...")
- No opinions beyond FRE 701 lay opinion or FRE 702 expert opinion (if declarant is disclosed as an expert)

### Requirement 3 — Competency

The declarant must be someone who could testify in court:
- Of sufficient age and capacity to understand the oath
- Not barred from testifying by privilege
- Able to communicate the substance of their testimony

---

## The Declaration Form

A FRCP 56(c)(4)-compliant declaration must also comply with 28 U.S.C. § 1746 as to form (for federal proceedings). The three requirements of FRCP 56(c)(4) govern the content; § 1746 governs the form (under penalty of perjury + true and correct + date + signature).

**HERALD's complete declaration standard for summary judgment:**

1. § 1746 form compliance: "I declare under penalty of perjury that the foregoing is true and correct. Executed on [date]. [Signature]."
2. FRCP 56(c)(4) content compliance: personal knowledge, admissible facts, competency shown
3. FRE 602 compliance throughout
4. FRE 701 compliance for any lay opinions
5. FRE 901 authentication of any attached exhibits

# Layer 2 — Reasoning
## FRCP_56_C_4 | Why Summary Judgment Declarations Are the Steward's Most Critical Document

**Standard ID:** FRCP_56_C_4
**Filed:** 2026-04-12

---

## Why Summary Judgment Is the Critical Battleground

In federal civil rights litigation under § 1983, the summary judgment motion is the most likely point where the case is won or lost. Most § 1983 cases do not go to trial — they end at summary judgment. The defense files a motion showing (in their view) that no genuine dispute of material fact exists and that the defendants are entitled to judgment as a matter of law. The plaintiff must respond with admissible evidence — declarations, authenticated documents, deposition testimony — sufficient to create a genuine dispute on each material fact.

If the plaintiff has no admissible evidence on a key fact, they lose that fact. If they lose enough key facts, they lose the motion. If they lose the motion, the case is over.

**HERALD's role:** Every declaration HERALD drafts for the steward must be FRCP 56(c)(4)-compliant — not because summary judgment is the only context, but because it is the highest bar. A declaration that satisfies FRCP 56(c)(4) satisfies every lower-stakes declaration context (preliminary injunction, IFP application, complaint filing).

## The Celotex Framework — What the Steward Must Show to Survive Summary Judgment

*Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317 (1986): The Supreme Court held that a defendant can shift the burden to the plaintiff on summary judgment by pointing out the absence of evidence on a key element — without having to affirmatively disprove the plaintiff's case. Once that burden shifts, the plaintiff must come forward with admissible evidence sufficient to create a genuine dispute.

**Implication for the steward:** When the City of Antioch moves for summary judgment on the § 1983 claim, they may simply point out: "Plaintiff has no admissible evidence that [key element]." The steward must respond with declarations, authenticated documents, and other admissible evidence. HERALD's corpus build ensures that foundation is in place before the motion is filed — not scrambled together at response time.

## The Anderson v. Liberty Lobby Materiality Standard

*Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242 (1986): A genuine dispute is one where a reasonable jury could find for the non-movant. The dispute must be about a *material* fact — one that could change the outcome of the case under the applicable law.

**Implication:** Not every factual dispute defeats summary judgment. The steward's declarations must focus on material facts — the elements of the § 1983 claim, the causation for SIRVA, the fraud elements for Honeysuckle, etc. A declaration full of true-but-immaterial facts does not help. HERALD drafts declarations targeted to the elements that must be proven, not general narrative.

## The "Would Be Admissible" Standard

FRCP 56(c)(4) says "facts that would be admissible in evidence" — not "facts in admissible form." This distinction matters:

- **What it means:** The underlying fact must be admissible. The declaration does not have to present it in final trial form. A summary or paraphrase is OK if the underlying fact could be admitted.
- **What it does NOT mean:** The declarant can summarize or describe inadmissible evidence and thereby make it "admissible." The underlying fact must be admissible somewhere — through the declarant's personal knowledge testimony, a hearsay exception, an authenticated document.

**Example:**
- "At the closing, I received a stack of documents. Some had blank signature lines." — This fact is admissible through the steward's personal knowledge. The declaration is FRCP 56(c)(4)-compliant.
- "The escrow company's closing package shows 19 unsigned documents." — This fact requires either: (a) the steward's personal knowledge of receiving those documents, or (b) an authenticated closing package from the escrow company's records. The declaration must establish which it is.

## Qualified Immunity — The Defense That Makes Declarations Critical in § 1983 Cases

In the § 1983 action, APD officers will likely assert qualified immunity. To defeat qualified immunity at summary judgment, the steward must show:

1. The defendants violated a constitutional right, AND
2. The right was "clearly established" at the time of the violation — a reasonable officer would have known their conduct was unlawful

The factual predicate for both elements must come from admissible evidence — primarily the steward's personal knowledge declaration (for what happened) and legal authority showing the right was clearly established (from the legal corpus, not the steward's declaration). This is the exact structure HERALD uses: separate the facts (steward's § 1746 declaration) from the law (legal brief).

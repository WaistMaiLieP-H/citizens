# Layer 3 — Historical Loss
## FRCP_56_C_4 | Cases Lost at Summary Judgment Due to Declaration Defects

**Standard ID:** FRCP_56_C_4
**Filed:** 2026-04-12

---

## The Pattern — Pro Se § 1983 Cases Die at Summary Judgment

The documented pattern in § 1983 pro se litigation is consistent: plaintiff files a compelling complaint with detailed factual allegations; defendant moves for summary judgment; plaintiff responds with an unverified brief, a declaration full of information-and-belief statements, and unauthenticated documents; court grants summary judgment because plaintiff has no admissible evidence.

HERALD exists in part to ensure this pattern does not repeat in the steward's cases.

---

## Documented Failure Patterns

### 1. The "verified complaint as evidence" mistake

**Pattern:** Pro se plaintiff files a verified complaint (signed under § 1746) with detailed factual allegations. When summary judgment comes, plaintiff argues the verified complaint itself is the declaration. Courts are split on whether a verified complaint satisfies FRCP 56(c)(4). Some circuits allow it; others require a separate declaration specifically addressing the summary judgment record.

**HERALD's position:** Do not rely on the complaint as the sole summary judgment declaration. Draft a separate FRCP 56(c)(4) declaration specifically keyed to the disputed material facts. The complaint is a pleading; the declaration is evidence.

---

### 2. The declaration stripped of all useful content

**Pattern:** Plaintiff submits a 20-paragraph summary judgment declaration. Defense moves to strike: paragraphs 1-5 are legal conclusions; paragraphs 6-12 are hearsay ("I was told by my attorney...," "records show..."); paragraphs 13-18 are information and belief. Paragraphs 19-20 establish that plaintiff was present at the incident and that the incident occurred. Court grants motion to strike paragraphs 1-18. Two-paragraph declaration left. Not sufficient to create a genuine dispute. Summary judgment granted.

**Teaching point:** Every paragraph of a HERALD-drafted declaration is pre-screened for FRE 602, FRE 701, hearsay, and FRCP 56(c)(4) compliance before it is written. The goal is zero-strike declarations.

---

### 3. The qualified immunity collapse — no evidence of clearly established right

**Pattern:** Plaintiff has good evidence that the officers used excessive force. But plaintiff's declaration does not cite any legal authority showing the right was clearly established. The court treats the legal authority question as part of the factual record — no factual evidence of prior case law in the record. Court finds qualified immunity applies because plaintiff has not produced evidence that the right was clearly established.

**Teaching point:** The "clearly established law" showing is a legal argument made in the brief, not a factual showing in the declaration. HERALD drafts declarations for facts; the legal brief makes the clearly-established showing. The two must be coordinated — the declaration provides the facts; the brief applies the law. HERALD flags this coordination requirement for the § 1983 brief.

---

### 4. The authenticated vs. unauthenticated exhibit split

**Pattern:** Plaintiff's FRCP 56(c)(4) declaration says "See Exhibit A" for a key document. Exhibit A is attached but the declaration does not state that plaintiff received the exhibit, recognizes it, or can authenticate it. Court sustains authentication objection. Exhibit A is excluded from the summary judgment record. Motion granted on the fact that exhibit would have established.

**Teaching point:** HERALD's declaration template: every attached exhibit requires authentication language in the body of the declaration. "Attached hereto as Exhibit A is a true and correct copy of [document description], which I received/created/observed on [date] under the circumstances described in paragraph [X]."

---

### 5. The competing declarations stalemate — no summary judgment when credibility is at issue

**Positive pattern:** Plaintiff files a FRCP 56(c)(4)-compliant declaration with personal knowledge testimony directly contradicting the defendant's declaration. Both are admissible. The court must deny summary judgment because a credibility determination is required — and credibility is for the jury.

**This is how pro se § 1983 plaintiffs survive summary judgment:** Not by having more documents, but by having a compliant personal knowledge declaration that directly contradicts the defendant's version of the facts on a material element.

**Application to the steward's cases (June 16, 2023):**
- Defendant's officers will file declarations describing the arrest as lawful
- The steward's § 1746/FRCP 56(c)(4) declaration describing the same events from personal knowledge creates a direct factual dispute on the material facts
- That dispute cannot be resolved on summary judgment — it goes to a jury
- **The declaration must be in place and compliant before the motion is filed**

## The Summary Judgment Checklist HERALD Uses for Every Declaration

Before a declaration is finalized for summary judgment use:

| Check | Question | Failure = |
|---|---|---|
| § 1746 form | Under penalty of perjury + true and correct + date + signature? | Stricken as unsworn |
| Personal knowledge header | Does declarant identify themselves and their basis for personal knowledge? | FRE 602 objection |
| Each factual paragraph | Is every fact within declarant's personal observation? | Stricken per paragraph |
| Hearsay screen | Does any paragraph assert the truth of an out-of-court statement without an exception? | Stricken per paragraph |
| Opinion screen | Are all opinions FRE 701 compliant (perception-based, helpful, not specialized)? | Stricken per paragraph |
| Legal conclusions screen | Are there any legal conclusions that invade the fact-finder's role? | Stricken |
| Exhibit authentication | Is every attached exhibit authenticated in the body of the declaration? | Exhibit excluded |
| Materiality check | Does the declaration address the disputed *material* facts of this case? | Declaration admitted but unhelpful |

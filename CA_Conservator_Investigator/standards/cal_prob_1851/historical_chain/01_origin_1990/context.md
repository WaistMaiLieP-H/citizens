# Historical Chain — CAL_PROB_1851_ANNUAL_REVIEW
## Origin: 1990 Probate Code — Periodic Review Requirement

## The Pre-Review Era Problem

Before the 1990 recodification, once a conservatorship was established, it often persisted indefinitely without court oversight. The court file might show the initial appointment and then years of silence — no reviews, no accountings, no check on whether the conservatorship was still needed or whether the conservator was behaving appropriately.

The consequences were severe and documented: conservators who isolated their wards from family, dissipated assets, made medical decisions against the conservatee's known wishes, and faced no accountability because no one was checking.

## The 1990 Reform — Mandatory Ongoing Review

Stats. 1990, Ch. 79 made periodic review mandatory. The §1851 obligation — recurring, investigator-conducted, without notice to the conservator — was designed to create ongoing accountability for conservatorships that had previously operated in the dark.

## The Resource Problem

§1851's mandate has consistently outrun the resources to fulfill it. County probate courts have used court investigators who carry hundreds of active cases. Reviews that should occur biennially are delayed. When reviews occur, they may be conducted through phone calls or brief office visits rather than the in-person conservatee visits the statute requires.

The gap between §1851's requirements and actual practice is well-documented in California's Legislative Analyst reports and the 2005 California Probate Court Investigator Association study finding systemic under-resourcing of the court investigator function.

## The Wound

The wound is the promise betrayed by under-resourcing: the law requires regular, independent, surprise visits to every conservatee to ensure they are not being abused. The reality is a system with too many cases and too few investigators, where a conservatorship can persist for decades without meaningful review. The conservatee who most needs protection — the one being isolated, controlled, and financially exploited — is the least able to advocate for themselves when the review fails to happen.

For the steward's case: each year the conservatorship existed without a §1851 review is a year in which the question "do you want this conservatorship terminated?" was never asked. The silence in the court file answers that question differently than any review would have.

# Reasoning — CAL_PROB_1860_TERMINATION

## The Burden Asymmetry Problem

§ 1801(e) requires clear and convincing evidence to establish a conservatorship. §1862 requires only a preponderance of evidence to terminate it. This asymmetry seems to favor continuation over termination.

The Legislature's reasoning: at appointment, the stakes are high (liberty is about to be taken). At termination, the liberty is already gone and the question is whether it should be returned. The Legislature placed the initial burden high to prevent improper appointment, but placed the termination burden at preponderance to avoid making termination impossible.

In practice, the burden asymmetry can disadvantage a conservatee who has been isolated and lacks access to witnesses, records, and professional assessment — the exact condition a controlling conservatorship would create.

## The Jury Trial as a Safeguard

§ 1863's jury trial right is a constitutional safeguard against judicial bias or capture. A judge who regularly approves the county public guardian's conservatorship recommendations may not be the most skeptical reviewer of a conservatorship's continuation. A jury of ordinary citizens, hearing that a person has been conserved for 10 years and that no annual reviews were conducted, may reach different conclusions.

The jury trial right at termination (and at contested appointment hearings) is the Legislature's final backstop against conservatorship as a system of judicial capture.

## The §1851 Connection

The §1851 investigator is required to ask annually whether the conservatee wishes to terminate. But the investigator is not the conservatee's advocate — the investigator reports to the court. If the conservatee wants to petition for termination, they need access to counsel or a petition preparer who can file on their behalf.

A conservatee who is isolated by the conservator, who has no access to their own funds to hire counsel, and whose contacts with the outside world are controlled, effectively cannot exercise the §1860 right to petition. The right exists on paper; the access to exercise it has been eliminated.

# Conservatorship of O.B. (Ramirez), 9 Cal. 5th 989 (2022)

## The Case

The Public Guardian petitioned for conservatorship over O.B. under the LPS Act (Welfare & Institutions Code §5000 et seq.), alleging she was gravely disabled due to mental illness. The trial court appointed a conservator. On appeal, O.B. argued the trial court failed to apply the clear and convincing evidence standard.

## What the Court Held

The California Supreme Court:

1. **The clear and convincing evidence standard applies to LPS conservatorship proceedings.** The trial court must find by clear and convincing evidence that the proposed conservatee is gravely disabled. This is required by due process — involuntary conservatorship is a significant deprivation of liberty.

2. **This standard applies at both the trial court AND appellate levels.** Appellate courts reviewing LPS conservatorship findings must account for the heightened standard — they cannot simply apply the deferential "substantial evidence" standard as if it were an ordinary civil case.

3. **The liberty interest at stake in involuntary conservatorship is among the most significant deprivations the state can impose on an individual.** The standard of proof must reflect the gravity of what is being taken away.

## The Rule

**Conservatorship — whether general (Probate Code) or LPS (WIC §5000) — requires clear and convincing evidence. This heightened standard reflects the fundamental liberty interest at stake. Both trial and appellate courts must apply it.**

## Why This Case Matters

Ramirez is the most recent California Supreme Court statement on conservatorship procedure. It confirmed that conservatorship is treated as a quasi-criminal deprivation of liberty for procedural purposes — the state must meet a high evidentiary bar before taking away a person's freedom. This applies across all conservatorship types: general (§1801), limited (§1801(d)), and LPS.

For the steward's case: if a conservatorship was established, the evidence supporting it must have met the clear and convincing standard. A finding based on thin evidence, unverified reports, or a cursory evaluation fails this bar.

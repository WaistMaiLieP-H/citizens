# Historical Chain — CAL_PROB_811_CAPACITY_DETERMINATION
## Origin: 1990 Probate Code — Functional Capacity Framework

## Pre-1990: The Diagnosis-Based Model

Before 1990, California courts frequently appointed conservators based primarily on a physician's diagnosis of a mental condition. The leap from "this person has [condition]" to "this person cannot make their own decisions" was often made without any analysis of what specific decisions the condition actually impaired.

The result was systematic over-conservatorship of people with disabilities, mental illness, and developmental conditions who were fully capable of many of the decisions being taken from them.

## The 1990 Reform — Function Over Diagnosis

§ 811 was part of the 1990 recodification's shift to a functional assessment model. The Legislature's mandate: capacity is decision-specific, not diagnosis-specific. A person is either capable or incapable of making a particular decision — and that determination must be based on evidence of what they can and cannot do, not on what they have been diagnosed with.

This shift aligned California with the emerging international consensus on disability rights — the ADA (1990) was passed the same year, both reflecting the principle that disability is defined by functional limitation, not diagnosis.

## The Wound

The wound is the expert-opinion problem: courts defer heavily to physician opinions about capacity. A physician who says "patient lacks capacity" may be believed even when the opinion is based entirely on diagnosis, not functional assessment. §811 is the Legislature's attempt to constrain that deference — but in practice, a physician opinion that fails §811's standards may still be accepted by a court without analysis.

The specific wound for the steward's case: a capacity declaration that did not identify specific §811 functional deficits, or that was based on a diagnosis applied to a 14-year-old without developmental assessment, fails §811 as a matter of law.

# Reasoning — CAL_PROB_2620_ACCOUNTING

## The Accounting as Audit Trail

§ 2620 exists because the conservator of the estate has unfettered access to another person's financial resources. Without a mandatory accounting, there is no mechanism to detect diversion, waste, or conversion of conservatorship assets. The annual accounting is the primary anti-fraud mechanism built into the conservatorship system.

For any conservatorship lasting multiple years, the accountings create a complete financial history: what came in, what went out, and what remains. If assets that should have been conserved — home equity, pension proceeds, investment accounts — are missing from the final accounting, the accounting itself is the evidence of dissipation.

## The Investigative Framework

For the steward's case: if a conservatorship of the estate existed, the accountings should show:
1. The initial inventory of assets — what the conservatorship started with
2. Annual accounting of receipts and disbursements
3. Whether the proceeds of the 2958 Honeysuckle sale ($605K) appear as receipts in any conservatorship accounting
4. Where those funds went after receipt
5. Whether conservator fees, professional fees, and other disbursements were approved by the court

If accountings were never filed — or if the Honeysuckle proceeds appear without corresponding court approval — the accounting record is evidence of fiduciary breach.

## Cross-Reference to §1851

§1851 requires the conservator to make financial records available to the court investigator. §2620 requires those same records to be filed with the court annually. The two provisions work in tandem: §2620 creates the record; §1851 allows the investigator to check whether the record matches reality. A discrepancy between the §2620 accounting and the actual financial state revealed in a §1851 review is evidence of fraud.

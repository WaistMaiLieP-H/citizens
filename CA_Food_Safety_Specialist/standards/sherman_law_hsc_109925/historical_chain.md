# Historical Chain — Sherman Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Law
## Cal. Health & Safety Code § 109875 et seq. (§ 109925 drug definition) — Stats. 1995, Ch. 415

---

## The wound

The federal Pure Food and Drug Act of 1906 was the first national response to a documented crisis: adulterated food, misbranded drugs, and patent medicines containing undisclosed narcotics were being sold freely across the country. But federal law set a floor, not a ceiling, and its enforcement was tied to interstate commerce. California had its own economy, its own food production system, and its own population to protect — but for decades California's food and drug regulatory framework was a patchwork of statutes enacted at different times under different standards, without uniform definitions or integrated enforcement.

The harm of non-uniform standards was practical. A food processor in California could be subject to one definition of adulteration, a different labeling requirement, and a third set of pesticide-residue rules depending on which state agency had jurisdiction and which statute applied. Consumers received inconsistent protection depending on where the product was processed or sold. Enforcement agencies faced overlapping and sometimes contradictory mandates. The absence of a unified California drug definition meant that misbranded or adulterated products could exploit definitional gaps between state and federal law.

---

## The legislative response

California's Sherman Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Law has a longer lineage than its 1995 codification suggests. The original Sherman Law — named for Assemblyman Allan Sherman — was first enacted in the 1930s to give California a state-level analog to the federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act of 1938. The 1938 federal act had replaced the 1906 act after the sulfanilamide disaster of 1937, in which more than 100 people died after ingesting a drug formulated with a toxic solvent (diethylene glycol) that was not required to be tested for safety before sale. California followed the federal model but extended it to cover intrastate commerce that federal jurisdiction could not reach.

The 1995 codification (Stats. 1995, Ch. 415) reorganized the Sherman Law into its current home at Division 104 of the Health and Safety Code (Part 5, §§ 109875–111929.5). Section 109925 defines "drug" for purposes of the entire part — the operational gate for California drug regulation. The definition tracks the federal FDCA definition with modifications, including the 2017 SB 94 amendment that excluded cannabis products from the drug definition (reflecting California's post-legalization regulatory framework).

---

## The current state

The Sherman Law operates as California's own FDA analog. It is administered by the California Department of Public Health (CDPH) and, for some categories, by the California Department of Food and Agriculture. Where the Sherman Law adopts federal FDA standards by reference, California enforcement agencies apply the federal standards as state law — meaning that a violation of FDA food or drug standards is simultaneously a violation of California's Sherman Law.

Section 109925's drug definition is the threshold provision: any article meeting the definition is subject to the full regulatory framework of Part 5, including misbranding and adulteration prohibitions, labeling requirements, new drug approval requirements, and enforcement authority including embargo, seizure, and criminal penalty.

The California structure — adopt federal standards by reference, then layer state-specific requirements on top — is itself a doctrine lesson: it creates two enforcement tracks (federal and state) that operate in parallel and can result in dual liability for a single violation. For the CA Food Safety Specialist, understanding which track applies, which standard is stricter, and which agency has primary jurisdiction is the core professional competency this standard anchors.

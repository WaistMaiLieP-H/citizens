# Reasoning — Why § 790.09 Exists

## The Shelter Problem

Without §790.09, an insurer facing bad-faith claims could theoretically negotiate a quick CDI consent order (with limited fine) and then argue in subsequent civil litigation that the regulatory matter has been resolved and no further liability exists. This "consent order as shield" argument — while legally weak — could be made.

§790.09 closes that argument explicitly. The Legislature anticipated that insurers would try to use administrative resolution to limit civil exposure. The statute says directly: "shall in no way relieve or absolve." There is no ambiguity. There is no exception.

## The Structural Parallel

The pattern of "administrative resolution does not foreclose civil liability" appears throughout California regulatory law:
- DFPI enforcement actions do not release civil fraud claims
- CSLB disciplinary actions do not release contractor tort liability
- CDI actions under §790.09 do not release insurance bad faith claims

This parallel confirms a structural principle in California law: administrative enforcement exists to protect the regulatory system; civil enforcement exists to compensate the injured party. These are different purposes, requiring different remedies, and neither displaces the other.

## Significance for Moradi-Shalal Navigation

After Moradi-Shalal eliminated the private §790.03 action, §790.09 became even more important: it ensures that CDI's now-exclusive administrative enforcement of §790.03 does not become a ceiling on insurer accountability. The civil bad faith tort (Gruenberg/Tomaselli) survives and runs alongside CDI enforcement precisely because §790.09 prevents the CDI track from absorbing or settling civil claims.

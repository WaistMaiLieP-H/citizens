# Rule — INS §10123.135: Utilization Review Requirements and AI Prohibition

**Citation:** Cal. Ins. Code §10123.135
**Standard ID:** ins_10123_135_prior_authorization
**Status:** PUBLISHED

---

## Scope

Every California disability insurer that prospectively, retrospectively, or concurrently reviews and approves, modifies, delays, or denies health care services based in whole or in part on medical necessity. Applies directly to the insurer AND to any entity the insurer contracts with for utilization review (UR) or utilization management (UM) functions. Delegation does not eliminate the insurer's §10123.135 compliance obligation.

---

## The Five Operative Rules

### Rule 1: Written UR/UM Policies Required (§10123.135(b))

The insurer must maintain written policies and procedures for every UR/UM decision. Those policies must:
- Be filed with the CDI Commissioner
- Be disclosed to insureds and providers upon request
- Ensure decisions are consistent with criteria supported by clinical principles and processes
- Be developed pursuant to the criteria standards in §10123.135(f)

**Violation type:** Failure to maintain or disclose written UR/UM policies is a per se §790.03(h) unfair claim settlement practice.

### Rule 2: Licensed Medical Director Required (§10123.135(c))

If California insureds constitute ≥50% of the insurer's nationwide enrollment, the insurer must employ or designate a medical director who holds an unrestricted California medical license (or equivalent clinical director). The medical director must ensure UR/UM compliance.

**Application to Blue Shield:** Blue Shield of California's California-licensed enrollment almost certainly exceeds 50%. Failure to have a qualified medical director overseeing UR/UM is a standalone violation.

### Rule 3: Physician-Only Decision Authority for Medical Necessity Denials (§10123.135(e))

> An individual, other than a licensed physician or a licensed health care professional who is competent to evaluate the specific clinical issues involved in the health care services requested by the provider, **may not deny or modify** requests for authorization of health care services for an insured for reasons of medical necessity.

**This is categorical.** A non-physician employee, a claims adjuster, a case manager, or an algorithm may not deny or modify for medical necessity reasons. Only a licensed, competent physician or health care professional may do so.

### Rule 4: Timeline Requirements (§10123.135(h))

| Review Type | Decision Deadline | Communication to Provider | Written Denial to Insured |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standard (non-urgent) | 5 business days from receipt of necessary information | 24 hours after decision | 2 business days after decision |
| Urgent (imminent threat) | 72 hours from receipt of necessary information | 24 hours after decision | 2 business days after decision |
| Retrospective | 30 days from receipt of information | Consistent with current law | Written to individual |

**Missing the timeline is a per se violation.** Each day of delay beyond the statutory deadline is a separate unfair practice under §790.03(h)(5) ("compelling an insured to institute litigation to recover amounts due").

**Denial communication must include:**
- Clear and concise explanation of reasons
- Criteria or guidelines used
- Clinical reasons for medical necessity determination
- Name and direct phone number of the health care professional responsible
- Information on how to appeal or seek CDI review

### Rule 5: AI/Algorithm Absolute Prohibition (§10123.135(j)(2)) — Effective January 1, 2025

> The artificial intelligence, algorithm, or other software tool **shall not deny, delay, or modify** health care services based, in whole or in part, on medical necessity.

This is not a best-practices standard. It is a categorical prohibition effective January 1, 2025.

**What §10123.135(j) permits:** AI tools may be used to assist in reviewing information, flagging cases, managing workflows, and generating recommendations.

**What §10123.135(j)(2) absolutely prohibits:** Any AI system that issues the actual denial, delay, or modification decision — even if a human rubber-stamps it. The determination of medical necessity must be made by a licensed physician reviewing:
1. The requesting provider's recommendation
2. The insured's medical or other clinical history
3. Individual clinical circumstances

**AI-laundering is prohibited:** Using an AI to make the effective denial and then having a physician sign off without independent review violates §10123.135(j)(2). The physician's review must be substantive — reviewing all three elements above for the specific case.

## Application to Steward Cases

### Blue Shield Prior Authorization — Prior Authorization Denials

If Blue Shield used automated or algorithm-based systems to generate any denial, delay, or modification of Michael Hartmann's health care services before or after January 1, 2025, each such decision is potentially a per se violation of §10123.135(j)(2) if the decision was made or implemented by the algorithm rather than by a reviewing physician.

**Pre-2025 denials:** §10123.135(e) applies without the 2025 amendment — non-physician denial authority was already prohibited. If Blue Shield's UR system was operated by non-physicians, each denial was a §10123.135(e) violation regardless of the AI prohibition.

**Checklist for Blue Shield UR violations:**
1. Were written UR/UM policies disclosed upon request? Failure = §10123.135(b) violation
2. Were timelines for each denial met? Each missed deadline = §10123.135(h) violation
3. Did each denial letter include the reviewing physician's name and direct phone number? Missing = §10123.135(h)(4) violation
4. Did each denial explain the criteria used and the clinical reasons? Missing = §10123.135(h)(4) violation
5. Was any denial generated or implemented by an AI/algorithm system? If so and post-1/1/2025 = §10123.135(j)(2) per se violation
6. Was any denial made by a non-physician reviewer? = §10123.135(e) violation (all periods)

**Each numbered violation is independent.** A single denial letter can contain five separate §10123.135 violations.

### Connection to Bad Faith Claim

A pattern of §10123.135 violations is strong evidence of breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing. When an insurer systematically fails to follow statutory UR/UM requirements, it demonstrates the bad faith element of conscious disregard of policyholder rights.

# Origin Context — INS §10123.135

**Enacted:** Operative 2000 (as part of CDI utilization review framework)
**The Wound:** Insurers using unqualified reviewers and opaque criteria to deny medically necessary care — with no timeline accountability and no disclosure obligation

---

## The Pattern That Built This Law

Through the 1990s, California disability insurers developed utilization review as a cost-containment tool. The mechanism was effective at reducing claims costs. It was also being operated by people who had no business making clinical decisions:

- Claims adjusters were issuing medical necessity denials
- Criteria for denial were proprietary and never disclosed to the treating physician
- There were no timelines — insurers could delay decisions indefinitely
- Denials arrived with no explanation, no criteria cited, no physician identified
- There was no statutory right to know who made the decision or how to reach them

The treating physician had nowhere to appeal except the insurer's internal process, which was often run by the same non-physician who issued the original denial.

## What the Legislature Promised

§10123.135 was enacted as a legislative promise:

> Medical necessity decisions will be made by qualified people, on defined timelines, under disclosed criteria, and the insured will be told who made the decision and how to challenge it.

This was not a reform of insurance practices — it was a statutory floor beneath which no insurer was permitted to operate.

## The Specific Wound: Non-Physician Denials

The wound §10123.135(e) directly answers is the pattern of claims adjusters denying complex medical procedures by matching procedure codes against denial lists. A spine surgery recommended by a neurosurgeon was being overridden by a person with no medical training checking boxes on a form.

§10123.135(e) says: that practice is illegal. The decision-maker must be a licensed physician competent to evaluate the specific clinical issues. No exceptions.

# Insurance Bad Faith Tort — Foundational Case Law

**Standard type:** Case law standard (no single statutory text; common law doctrine)
**Primary authorities:**
- Gruenberg v. Aetna Insurance Co. (1973) 9 Cal.3d 566
- Brandt v. Superior Court (1985) 37 Cal.3d 813
- Tomaselli v. Transamerica Insurance Co. (1994) 25 Cal.App.4th 1269
- Moradi-Shalal v. Fireman's Fund Insurance Co. (1988) 46 Cal.3d 287

## Gruenberg v. Aetna (1973) 9 Cal.3d 566 — The Foundation

Held: Every insurance contract contains an implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing. When an insurer unreasonably withholds policy benefits, it commits a tort — not merely a breach of contract. Tort liability enables recovery of consequential damages beyond the policy limits.

Key holding: "There is an implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing in every contract that neither party will do anything which will injure the right of the other to receive the benefits of the agreement."

## Brandt v. Superior Court (1985) 37 Cal.3d 813 — Attorney Fees as Damages

Held: When an insurer tortiously withholds policy benefits in bad faith, the attorney fees an insured incurs TO RECOVER THOSE BENEFITS are recoverable as an element of compensatory damages — not as a cost item or sanction, but as actual consequential damages caused by the bad faith denial.

Key holding: Brandt fees are recoverable to the extent they were necessary to obtain the contract benefits. They are measured by the time spent on the contract/insurance claim portion of the litigation, not the entire fee.

**Brandt vs. attorney fees generally:** Brandt fees are: (1) compensatory, not punitive; (2) recoverable without proving punitive damages; (3) limited to fees incurred to recover policy benefits specifically (not fees for other claims). They require a jury determination of how much of counsel's fees were spent on the bad faith contract recovery.

## Tomaselli v. Transamerica (1994) 25 Cal.App.4th 1269 — Bad Faith Standard

Held: The test for insurance bad faith is whether the insurer's denial or delay was "unreasonable." Bad faith does not require proof of dishonesty or actual malice — an objectively unreasonable denial is sufficient for the tort.

For punitive damages: requires "conscious disregard" of the insured's rights — the denial must be more than unreasonable; it must be made in conscious disregard of plaintiff's rights or with malice, oppression, or fraud under CIV §3294.

## Moradi-Shalal (1988) — Confirms Bad Faith Track Survives §790.03 Limitation

The Gruenberg/Brandt bad faith tort survives Moradi-Shalal. Private plaintiffs cannot enforce §790.03 directly, but the common law bad faith tort (Gruenberg standard) is independent of §790.03 and was not affected by Moradi-Shalal's limitation. The bad faith tort is grounded in the implied covenant, not in the statute.

# Reasoning — Why Insurance Bad Faith Doctrine Exists

## The Adhesion Problem

Insurance contracts are contracts of adhesion — the insured does not negotiate terms; the insurer drafts the form. The insured's only real protection is the promise that the benefits will actually be paid when needed. If an insurer could breach that promise with impunity — paying only contract damages equal to the owed benefits plus interest — the breach would always be profitable. The insurer loses nothing by denying a claim and waiting to be sued.

The Gruenberg court recognized this structural problem. Tort liability (not merely contract liability) was necessary to deter bad-faith denials because:
1. Contract damages only put the insured where they should have been — no deterrence
2. The insured typically needs the benefits urgently (medical care, property repair, business continuation)
3. The insurer has vast informational and resources advantages
4. Without tort exposure, the rational economic choice for insurers is to deny first and settle only when litigation forces payment

## Brandt's Contribution

Even with Gruenberg establishing tort liability, an insured who had to hire an attorney to sue was still worse off than one whose claim was paid promptly — because attorney fees consumed part of the recovery. Brandt corrected this: fees incurred to obtain the benefits are themselves a damage caused by the bad-faith denial. The insured who was forced to litigate should be made whole.

The California Supreme Court's logic in Brandt was simple: if the insurer had honored the contract, the insured would not have needed an attorney. The attorney fees are a foreseeable, direct consequence of the bad faith denial.

## The Moradi-Shalal Aftermath

After Moradi-Shalal eliminated the §790.03 private action, the Gruenberg/Brandt tort track became the primary private remedy for insurer misconduct. The "unreasonable" standard of Tomaselli — though harder to prove than a specific §790.03(h) enumerated act — is the only available private litigation vehicle for insured persons harmed by bad-faith claims handling.

This asymmetry — specific standards exist (§790.03(h)(14): don't advise claimant to avoid an attorney) but only CDI can enforce them directly — is the wound the doctrine continues to manage.

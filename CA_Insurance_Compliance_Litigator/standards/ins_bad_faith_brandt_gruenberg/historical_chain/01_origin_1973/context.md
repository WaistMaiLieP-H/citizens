# Historical Chain — Insurance Bad Faith Doctrine (1973)

## Pre-Gruenberg Background

Before 1973, California courts treated insurance as purely a contract matter. Breach of an insurance policy triggered contract damages only — the owed benefits plus interest. This created systematic bad-faith behavior: insurers could deny valid claims knowing the worst-case outcome was paying what they owed anyway, plus some interest.

Some courts in other jurisdictions experimented with "extracontractual" damages but no clear framework existed. The National Commissioners on Uniform State Laws had not developed a model statute for bad faith (unlike their work on commercial paper, etc.). The wound was entirely a judicial problem awaiting a judicial solution.

## Gruenberg v. Aetna Insurance Co. (1973) 9 Cal.3d 566

The California Supreme Court held — in the context of a property insurance claim — that the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing (which had existed in California contract law since the 1960s) applied to insurance contracts and, crucially, that breach of the covenant in the insurance context was a TORT, not merely a contract breach.

The distinction was critical: tort liability made consequential damages, emotional distress damages, and ultimately punitive damages available. The court recognized that the insured who is wrongfully denied benefits suffers harms beyond the value of the policy — lost time, lost opportunity, forced expenditures to replace the benefit, emotional suffering from the denial of promised protection.

Gruenberg became the foundation for an entire body of California insurance law that is still actively litigated.

## Brandt v. Superior Court (1985) 37 Cal.3d 813

Twelve years after Gruenberg, the Supreme Court added the critical Brandt fee rule: attorney fees spent to recover improperly withheld benefits are themselves a compensatory damage. This closed the fee-shifting gap and made bad faith litigation economically viable for attorneys representing policyholders.

## Current State

Gruenberg/Brandt remains settled California law. Moradi-Shalal (1988) did not disturb the common law tort — it only addressed the separate question of whether §790.03 created a private right of action (it does not). The tort and the statute coexist in parallel, addressed to the same insurer conduct through different mechanisms.

# Historical Chain — § 790.03 Origin (1947)

## The Foundation: McCarran-Ferguson Act (1945)

The backstory of California's § 790.03 begins federally. In United States v. South-Eastern Underwriters Association (1944), the U.S. Supreme Court held that insurance was interstate commerce subject to federal antitrust law. This threatened the entire state regulatory system.

Congress responded in 1945 with the McCarran-Ferguson Act (Public Law 79-15), which established:
- State law prevails over federal antitrust law in the regulation of the insurance business to the extent it is regulated by state law
- Federal law applies only in the absence of state regulation
- States must maintain an adequate regulatory floor or federal antitrust applies

California's §790 (the article's purpose clause) expressly references McCarran-Ferguson: "to regulate trade practices... in accordance with the intent of Congress as expressed in the Act of Congress of March 9, 1945." This is not incidental — it is the constitutional anchor of CDI's authority.

## California's Response — NAIC Model (1947)

In 1947, the California Legislature enacted the Unfair Practices Act (now Ins. Code §§790–790.10) based on the National Association of Insurance Commissioners model act. The original purpose:
- Satisfy the McCarran-Ferguson condition (state regulation = federal antitrust exemption)
- Create an administrative enforcement mechanism through the Insurance Commissioner
- Define specific unfair practices with precision to avoid vague prohibition

The 16 enumerated acts in § 790.03(h) were not invented by California — they were adapted from the NAIC model, which had surveyed actual insurer practices that state regulators had identified as systematically harmful. §790.03(h)(14) (advising against attorney) and (h)(15) (SOL misrepresentation) appear in the NAIC model specifically because insurance commissioners across multiple states had documented these exact practices occurring repeatedly.

## Royal Globe Era (1979–1988)

Royal Globe Insurance Co. v. Superior Court (1979) 23 Cal.3d 880 held that injured third parties could sue insurers directly for violations of § 790.03. This created the "dual track" that survived for nine years. Insurers argued private litigation was unmanageable; CDI argued it undermined administrative primacy.

## Moradi-Shalal (1988) — The Reversal

Moradi-Shalal v. Fireman's Fund Insurance Co. (1988) 46 Cal.3d 287 overruled Royal Globe. The Court held the Legislature had not intended a private right of action — only CDI administrative enforcement. The bad faith tort (Gruenberg line) survived as a separate common-law theory, not as an enforcement mechanism for §790.03 per se.

**The gap:** Moradi-Shalal's reasoning assumed CDI would fill the enforcement void. That assumption has been contested by consumer advocates ever since.

## Status

Cal. Ins. Code §790.03 remains the definitive enumeration of unfair insurance practices in California. It has not been materially amended since the Moradi-Shalal era. CDI enforcement authority is exclusive; bad faith tort tracks in parallel.

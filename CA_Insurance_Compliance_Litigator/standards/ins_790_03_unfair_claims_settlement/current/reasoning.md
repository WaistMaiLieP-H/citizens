# Reasoning — Why § 790.03 Exists

## The Structural Problem

In the 1930s–1960s, the insurance industry operated under a federal antitrust immunity (confirmed by McCarran-Ferguson Act of 1945) on the theory that states would provide the regulatory floor. California's § 790.03 was part of the response — a comprehensive enumeration of what constitutes an unfair practice in the insurance business.

But enumeration without teeth was useless. In 1979, the California Supreme Court in Royal Globe Insurance Co. v. Superior Court created a private right of action under § 790.03, allowing third-party claimants to sue insurers directly for bad-faith claims handling. For nine years, insurers faced direct private litigation for the 16 enumerated practices.

Then came Moradi-Shalal (1988). The Supreme Court reversed course, holding that the Legislature had not intended to create a private right of action — CDI enforcement was the exclusive mechanism. The insurers celebrated. Policyholders lost direct access to the 16 enumerated standards as a litigation weapon.

## The Wound

The wound Moradi-Shalal opened: the CDI depends on complaint volume, staffing, and political priority. An insurer that tells a claimant not to get an attorney (h)(14) or lies about the statute of limitations (h)(15) may face a CDI administrative action — eventually. The victim has already been harmed.

The bad faith tort (Gruenberg/Brandt track) is the workaround — but it requires proving the denial was "unreasonable," not just that it hit one of the 16 enumerated acts. The enumerated acts in § 790.03(h) are not formally elements of the bad faith tort. They are, however, persuasive evidence of what "unreasonable" means in the insurance context.

## Why § 790.03(h)(14) and (15) Matter Here

Every prohibited practice in § 790.03(h) is individually labeled precisely because these acts were common enough to require specific enumeration. An insurer that says "you don't need a lawyer for this" — that specific statement, to a specific claimant, without more — is a § 790.03(h)(14) violation. It does not require a pattern. A single knowing act is sufficient for CDI enforcement.

The same is true for SOL misrepresentation (h)(15). An insurance company representative who tells a claimant the filing deadline has passed when it hasn't — or who fails to correct a claimant's mistaken belief — commits a § 790.03(h)(15) violation.

These provisions exist because they were common tactics.

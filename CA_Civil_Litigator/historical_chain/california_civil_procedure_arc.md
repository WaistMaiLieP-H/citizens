# The Arc of California Civil Procedure

This is the story of how California built the most developed civil procedure framework of any state — a system where citizens can challenge government, where public-interest litigants recover fees, where frivolous retaliation lawsuits get killed early, and where cases that are not prosecuted die on a fixed schedule.

---

## 1850: The Starting Point

California became a state in 1850 and adopted the common law and equity traditions inherited from English and American models. Courts operated under general procedural principles — writs, pleadings, trials — drawn from centuries of Anglo-American practice.

The system was functional but unsystematic. California needed its own procedural code.

## 1872: The Code of Civil Procedure

California enacted its Code of Civil Procedure (CCP) as part of a comprehensive codification effort. The 1872 Code established the procedural framework that still governs California litigation, including:

- **Mandamus (CCP §1085):** A writ compelling a government officer or entity to perform a duty required by law. Traditional mandamus — sometimes called "ordinary" mandamus — applies when the duty is ministerial: the law says the official must do X, the official has not done X, the court orders the official to do X.
- **Statutes of limitation:** Fixed deadlines for bringing different types of claims.
- **The ancestor of the five-year rule:** The principle that cases must be brought to trial within a fixed period or face mandatory dismissal.

The 1872 Code gave California a unified procedural system. Everything that followed built on this foundation.

## 1909: Taxpayer Standing (CCP §526a)

California enacted a taxpayer standing provision allowing any citizen who pays taxes to the relevant entity to bring suit challenging illegal expenditures of public funds. You do not need to show personal injury beyond being a taxpayer. If the government is spending money illegally, any taxpayer can go to court and stop it.

This is broader than federal standing doctrine, which requires individualized injury-in-fact. In California, being a taxpayer is enough.

§526a means the government cannot insulate illegal spending from judicial review simply because no single individual was harmed enough to satisfy federal standing requirements.

## 1933+: The Five-Year Rule (Now CCP §583.310)

The mandatory dismissal rule crystallized over decades and is now codified at CCP §583.310: an action must be brought to trial within five years after it is commenced, or it shall be dismissed. The word "shall" means mandatory — the court has no discretion. Five years and the case is dead.

The five-year rule serves two purposes:
1. It prevents cases from lingering indefinitely on court dockets
2. It forces plaintiffs to prosecute their claims or lose them

Tolling provisions exist (CCP §583.340) — the clock can be paused for impossibility, impracticability, or futility of bringing the action to trial. But the default is absolute: five years is the outer boundary.

## 1945: Administrative Mandamus (CCP §1094.5)

California created a specialized form of mandamus for judicial review of administrative decisions. Where ordinary mandamus (§1085) compels ministerial duties, administrative mandamus (§1094.5) reviews quasi-judicial decisions — licensing denials, permit revocations, disciplinary actions, benefit terminations.

The standard of review under §1094.5 depends on whether a fundamental vested right is at stake:
- **Fundamental right (e.g., professional license):** Independent judgment — the court reweighs the evidence
- **Non-fundamental right (e.g., land use permit):** Substantial evidence — the court defers unless no reasonable person could have reached the agency's conclusion

This created a dual-track mandamus system unique to California:
- §1085 for ministerial duties (did the agency do what the law requires?)
- §1094.5 for quasi-judicial decisions (did the agency decide correctly based on the evidence?)

## 1961: Muskopf — Sovereign Immunity Abolished

**Muskopf v. Corning Hospital District** (1961): The California Supreme Court abolished common-law sovereign immunity. The government could no longer claim that "the king can do no wrong" as a defense to tort liability.

This judicial decision set the stage for the legislative framework that followed two years later.

## 1963: Government Claims Act (Government Code §810+)

The Legislature responded to Muskopf by enacting the Government Claims Act, which replaced sovereign immunity with a comprehensive statutory framework. The government is liable — but only under the conditions the statute prescribes.

Key procedural requirements:
- **Claims must be filed before suit:** For most tort claims, you must file a written claim with the government entity within six months of accrual. Miss the deadline and you cannot sue, regardless of the merits.
- **The entity has 45 days to respond.** If it rejects the claim (or fails to respond), you then have six months to file suit.
- **Late claim relief is available but limited.** You can petition for leave to file a late claim within one year of accrual, but the entity can oppose, and the court has discretion.

The Government Claims Act is a procedural prerequisite to virtually every tort action against a California government entity. It is not optional. It is not forgivable. It is the first thing a litigator must check.

## 1973: Rehabilitation Act §504

The federal Rehabilitation Act of 1973 prohibited disability discrimination in programs receiving federal financial assistance. While a federal statute, it directly affects California civil procedure because California state agencies, counties, cities, school districts, and public hospitals receiving federal funds must comply.

§504 claims can be brought in state court and are often joined with state-law claims in California civil litigation.

## 1975: MICRA (CCP §340.5 and Related Provisions)

The Medical Injury Compensation Reform Act (MICRA) was enacted in response to a malpractice insurance crisis. Among other provisions, it established:

- **CCP §340.5:** A specific statute of limitations for professional negligence against health care providers — three years from the date of injury, or one year from the date the patient discovered or should have discovered the injury, whichever comes first
- **Non-economic damages cap:** $250,000 cap on pain and suffering damages in medical malpractice (subsequently adjusted by Proposition 35 in 2022)
- **Periodic payment of future damages**

MICRA created a specialized procedural track for medical malpractice that differs significantly from general tort litigation. The discovery rule in §340.5 is critical — it means the limitations period can be tolled until the plaintiff knew or should have known of both the injury and its negligent cause.

## 1977: Private Attorney General Doctrine (CCP §1021.5)

California codified fee-shifting for public-interest litigation. Under §1021.5, a court may award attorney's fees to a successful party in any action that:
1. Has resulted in the enforcement of an important right affecting the public interest
2. Has conferred a significant benefit on the general public or a large class of persons
3. The necessity and financial burden of private enforcement make the award appropriate

This is transformative. It means a litigant who wins a case benefiting the public can recover fees even when no specific fee-shifting statute applies. The doctrine recognizes that some cases are worth bringing for the public good but would never be economically rational for any individual plaintiff without fee recovery.

§1021.5 is the engine that makes public-interest litigation viable in California.

## 1982: Proposition 8 — Truth in Evidence

Proposition 8 amended the California Constitution to limit the state exclusionary rule. Before Proposition 8, California courts excluded evidence under standards broader than the federal Fourth Amendment required. Proposition 8 generally aligned the state exclusionary rule with the federal rule, eliminating the state-law advantage defendants had enjoyed.

The practical effect: evidence that is admissible under federal constitutional standards is generally admissible in California criminal proceedings. California courts can still exclude evidence under specific statutory provisions, but the broad state constitutional exclusionary rule was curtailed.

For civil procedure, Proposition 8's significance is indirect but real — it established the principle that California's procedural advantages over federal law are not unlimited and can be narrowed by voter initiative.

## 1992: Anti-SLAPP Statute (CCP §425.16)

California enacted the anti-SLAPP (Strategic Lawsuit Against Public Participation) statute to protect people from being sued in retaliation for exercising their rights of free speech or petition.

How it works:
1. The defendant files a special motion to strike, arguing the lawsuit arises from protected activity (speech, petition, or conduct in connection with a public issue)
2. If the defendant makes that showing, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to demonstrate a probability of prevailing on the merits
3. If the plaintiff cannot make that showing, the case is dismissed and the defendant recovers attorney's fees

Anti-SLAPP is a powerful early-termination mechanism. It forces the plaintiff to put up or shut up at the outset of the case — before discovery, before depositions, before the cost of litigation becomes a weapon. The mandatory fee-shifting means that filing a meritless retaliatory lawsuit carries real financial consequences.

The statute has been broadly interpreted. "Protected activity" includes statements made before legislative, executive, or judicial proceedings; statements made in connection with issues under review by those bodies; and speech on issues of public interest. The scope is wide.

## Present: The Most Developed Framework

California civil procedure is not just a set of rules for getting cases through court. It is a comprehensive system of tools that, taken together, create capabilities no other state matches:

- **Taxpayer standing (§526a):** Citizens can challenge illegal government spending without showing personal injury.
- **Dual mandamus (§1085/§1094.5):** Two tracks for judicial review of government action — ministerial duties and quasi-judicial decisions.
- **The five-year hammer (§583.310):** Cases that are not prosecuted die. No exceptions, no discretion.
- **Government Claims Act (Gov. Code §810+):** A mandatory procedural prerequisite that cannot be waived or ignored.
- **Private attorney general fees (§1021.5):** Public-interest litigation is economically viable because successful plaintiffs recover fees.
- **Anti-SLAPP (§425.16):** Retaliatory lawsuits targeting speech or petition activity get killed early, and the filer pays.
- **MICRA (§340.5):** A specialized procedural track for medical malpractice with its own limitations period and discovery rule.

Every one of these tools has a specific purpose. Together, they form a procedural framework that gives litigants — especially litigants challenging government action or vindicating public rights — more options and more leverage than any comparable state system.

The framework is not accidental. It was built over 150 years, statute by statute, proposition by proposition, each responding to a specific failure or gap. Taxpayer standing responded to government insulation from accountability. Anti-SLAPP responded to retaliatory litigation. The Government Claims Act responded to the abolition of sovereign immunity. §1021.5 responded to the economic impossibility of public-interest litigation without fee recovery.

California civil procedure is the accumulated answer to the question: what tools does a litigant need to hold power accountable?

BLAIR v. PITCHESS
5 Cal.3d 258 (1971)
California Supreme Court
Decided: July 15, 1971

---

FACTS:

Plaintiffs including Norman Blair challenged California's wage garnishment statutes as unconstitutional — they argued that the statutes allowed prejudgment garnishment of wages without notice or a hearing, violating due process. The action was brought as a taxpayer suit under CCP § 526a (challenging the state expenditures on enforcing unconstitutional statutes) and as a constitutional challenge.

The California Supreme Court addressed the constitutionality of the garnishment procedures and the scope of § 526a as a vehicle for constitutional challenges to enforcement expenditures.

---

HOLDING:

Chief Justice Wright wrote for the Court.

§ 526a AS A VEHICLE FOR CONSTITUTIONAL CHALLENGES:
"Section 526a authorizes taxpayer suits challenging not only the legality of individual expenditures but also the enforcement of unconstitutional statutes — which by definition involves the illegal expenditure of public funds."

A taxpayer's suit to enjoin enforcement of an unconstitutional statute qualifies under § 526a because:
1. Enforcing an unconstitutional statute is an illegal expenditure of law enforcement resources
2. The injury to the public treasury from unconstitutional enforcement is cognizable under § 526a
3. Courts need not make the plaintiff identify a specific dollar amount — the pattern of enforcement expenditures suffices

THE CONSTITUTIONAL CHALLENGE FRAMEWORK:
Blair was decided in the context of the line of wage garnishment cases that ultimately reached the U.S. Supreme Court in Sniadach v. Family Finance Corp. (1969) and Fuentes v. Shevin (1972). The California constitutional challenge complemented the federal due process analysis.

PROCEDURAL DUE PROCESS PRINCIPLES REAFFIRMED:
"The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires that a debtor receive notice and an opportunity to be heard before their wages are seized by the government on behalf of a creditor. Prejudgment seizure of property without any process is constitutionally suspect."

---

APPLICATION TO STEWARD'S CASES:

Blair / § 526a constitutional enforcement challenge:

1. **Challenging government enforcement of unconstitutional orders**: If family court orders were issued in violation of due process, and government officials (social workers, law enforcement, court-appointed monitors) are spending public resources enforcing those orders — each enforcement action is an expenditure of public funds on enforcement of void/unconstitutional orders. § 526a provides standing to challenge the enforcement pattern.

2. **Government enforcement of void family court orders**: Under California law, a court order issued without jurisdiction is void — not merely voidable. Enforcing a void order is illegal. A taxpayer may challenge the expenditure of public funds on enforcing such orders under Blair / § 526a.

3. **Combination with mandamus**: § 526a injunctive relief can run parallel to mandamus proceedings (CCP § 1085 or § 1094.5) challenging the underlying government action. The combination provides both a prohibition on future expenditure and an affirmative mandate to comply with law.

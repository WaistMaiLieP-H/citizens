# Blair v. Pitchess, 5 Cal.3d 258 (1971)

## The Case

Taxpayers sued Los Angeles County Sheriff Peter Pitchess, alleging that the sheriff's department was engaging in unconstitutional practices — specifically, conditions of confinement in county jails that violated inmates' constitutional rights. The plaintiffs brought the suit under CCP §526a as taxpayers. The question was whether §526a's reference to "injury to public property" could reach intangible interests like constitutional rights, or whether it was limited to physical property damage.

## What the Court Held

The California Supreme Court:

1. **"Injury to public property" under §526a includes intangible public property interests — not just physical buildings and equipment.** The public has a property interest in the lawful operation of its government. When the government violates the constitution, it injures that intangible public interest.

2. **Taxpayer standing under §526a extends to challenges of unconstitutional government practices, even when the direct harm falls on a specific group (here, inmates).** The taxpaying public has standing because public funds are being used to maintain an unconstitutional system.

3. **This builds on Wirin v. Parker (1957) and expands it.** Wirin held that illegal expenditures are waste. Blair went further: even if the expenditure is not "wasteful" in a financial sense, if it causes "injury to public property" — including intangible public interests — §526a provides standing.

## The Rule

**"Injury to public property" in §526a includes injury to intangible public property interests, such as the public's interest in constitutional governance. Taxpayers may challenge unconstitutional government practices even when the direct victims are a specific class of people.**

## Why This Case Matters for §526a

Blair is the bridge between fiscal-waste claims and constitutional-rights claims under §526a. After Blair, taxpayers could use §526a not just to stop the government from wasting money, but to stop the government from violating the constitution — even when the taxpayer was not personally affected by the violation.

## Citation

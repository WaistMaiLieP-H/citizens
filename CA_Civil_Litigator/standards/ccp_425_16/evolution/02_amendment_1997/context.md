# CCP 425.16 -- 1997 Amendment Context

## Enactment

Stats. 1997, Ch. 271 (S.B. 1296). Effective January 1, 1998.

## The Problem

The original 1992 statute protected acts "in furtherance of the person's right of petition or free speech under the United States or California Constitution in connection with a public issue." Courts were reading this narrowly. Some held that the statute only applied to speech directed at government bodies -- testimony at hearings, letters to agencies, formal petitions. If the speech happened outside a government proceeding, even if it was squarely about a public issue, some courts said it did not qualify.

This gutted the statute's purpose. A person who wrote a letter to the editor about a land-use decision was protected if they had also spoken at the planning commission hearing, but not if they had only written the letter. A community group that distributed flyers about a public health risk could be SLAPP'd if they had not also filed a formal petition. The courts were reading the word "petition" as the operative term and treating "free speech" as a secondary add-on.

The narrow reading also created a loophole for sophisticated plaintiffs. If you wanted to sue someone for speaking out, you just had to characterize the speech as something other than a government petition -- and courts would let the case proceed.

## What SB 1296 Changed

SB 1296 added subdivisions (e)(3) and (e)(4) to the definition of protected activity:

- (e)(3): "any written or oral statement or writing made in a place open to the public or a public forum in connection with an issue of public interest"
- (e)(4): "any other conduct in furtherance of the exercise of the constitutional right of petition or the constitutional right of free speech in connection with a public issue or an issue of public interest"

These two catchall provisions transformed the statute. Protected activity was no longer limited to speech directed at government. It now covered speech in any public forum about any issue of public interest, and any conduct in furtherance of free speech or petition rights connected to a public issue.

SB 1296 also added the express directive: "this section shall be construed broadly." The Legislature was telling courts that the narrow readings were wrong, and that the statute was meant to have wide reach.

## Why This Amendment Matters

The 1997 amendment turned the anti-SLAPP statute from a government-petition protection into a public-discourse protection. After SB 1296, the statute covered:

- Statements at public meetings (already covered)
- Statements in connection with any official proceeding (already covered)
- Statements in any public forum about a public issue (new)
- Any conduct furthering free speech or petition rights on a public issue (new)

This is the amendment that made the anti-SLAPP statute the powerful litigation tool it became. Nearly every significant anti-SLAPP case decided after 1998 involves (e)(3) or (e)(4). Without this amendment, the statute would have remained a narrow procedural protection for formal government petitioners.

# CCP 425.16 -- 2003 Amendments Context

## Enactment

Stats. 2003, Ch. 338 (S.B. 515) -- added CCP 425.17 (exemptions).
Stats. 2003, Ch. 338 also addressed CCP 425.18 (SLAPPback suits).
Effective January 1, 2004.

## The Problem

The 1997 broadening worked. Too well, in some hands.

After SB 1296 expanded protected activity to cover any conduct connected to a public issue, defense attorneys realized the anti-SLAPP motion could be used far beyond its original purpose. Defendants in legitimate lawsuits began filing anti-SLAPP motions to delay proceedings, impose costs on plaintiffs, and get early dismissals of claims that had nothing to do with silencing public participation.

Three categories of abuse emerged:

**Commercial speech exploitation.** Businesses sued for false advertising, unfair competition, or consumer fraud filed anti-SLAPP motions arguing their marketing was "speech on a public issue." A company selling defective products would claim its advertisements were protected activity. The motion stayed discovery, imposed a probability-of-prevailing burden on the plaintiff, and if successful, shifted fees to the consumer who had been harmed.

**Defensive use against public interest litigation.** When advocacy organizations sued polluters, discriminators, or public safety violators, the defendants filed anti-SLAPP motions claiming the lawsuit itself was an act targeting their speech rights. The statute designed to protect citizens who spoke out was being turned against citizens who filed suit to enforce the law.

**Class action suppression.** Defendants facing class actions filed anti-SLAPP motions to strike the entire action, arguing that the underlying conduct (advertising, public statements, business practices) was protected activity. This forced class representatives to demonstrate probability of prevailing at the pleading stage, before any discovery, undermining the class action mechanism.

## What the 2003 Amendments Created

**CCP 425.17 -- Exemptions.** Three categories of cases were carved out from the anti-SLAPP statute entirely:

- (b): Actions brought solely in the public interest or on behalf of the general public, if the plaintiff does not seek damages greater than or equal to the defendant, and is seeking to enforce an important right or law
- (c): Actions against a person primarily engaged in the business of selling or leasing goods or services, arising from commercial speech (statements made in connection with selling goods/services to actual or prospective buyers/customers)
- (d): Actions brought by the Attorney General or district attorney (already partially addressed, now clarified)

**CCP 425.18 -- SLAPPback.** Created a cause of action for damages against a party who filed a frivolous anti-SLAPP motion. If you filed an anti-SLAPP motion that was frivolous or solely intended to cause unnecessary delay, the other side could bring a SLAPPback action for damages. This created a deterrent against the weaponized use of anti-SLAPP motions.

## Why These Amendments Matter

The 2003 amendments represent the Legislature recognizing that the anti-SLAPP statute had become a double-edged sword. The same broad language that protected citizens from retaliatory litigation was being exploited by sophisticated defendants to defeat legitimate claims. The response was surgical: rather than narrowing the statute, the Legislature carved out specific categories of cases where anti-SLAPP should not apply, and created a penalty for frivolous use.

This is a pattern that appears across California procedural law -- the Legislature enacts a broad protection, courts and litigants push its boundaries, and the Legislature responds with targeted exemptions rather than wholesale retrenchment.

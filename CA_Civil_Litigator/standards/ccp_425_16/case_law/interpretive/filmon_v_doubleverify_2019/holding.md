# FilmOn.com v. DoubleVerify, 7 Cal.5th 133 (2019)

## The Case

FilmOn.com, a streaming video company, sued DoubleVerify, an advertising verification service, for categorizing FilmOn's website as containing adult content and copyright-infringing material. DoubleVerify's categorizations were sold to advertising clients to help them decide where to place ads. DoubleVerify filed an anti-SLAPP motion, arguing its categorizations were protected speech on a public issue. FilmOn argued DoubleVerify's activity was purely commercial and did not qualify.

## What the Court Held

The California Supreme Court:

1. **Commercial speech can qualify as protected activity under anti-SLAPP.** The fact that DoubleVerify was in business to sell its categorizations did not automatically disqualify the speech from anti-SLAPP protection. There is no blanket "commercial speech exception" to §425.16.

2. **The test for whether speech concerns a "public issue" under §425.16(e)(4) looks at context, not the speaker's motive.** The court articulated that the relevant question is whether the speech contributes to public discussion of an issue, not whether the speaker had a commercial reason for speaking.

3. **However, DoubleVerify's specific categorizations did not qualify here.** Although commercial speech can be protected in principle, DoubleVerify's reports were provided only to paying clients, not to the public, and addressed a narrow business question (ad placement) rather than contributing to any public debate. The speech lacked the necessary connection to a public issue.

## The Rule

**Commercial speech is not automatically excluded from anti-SLAPP protection. But to qualify under §425.16(e)(4), the speech must actually concern an issue of public interest — the commercial nature of the speaker's motive does not help or hurt the analysis; what matters is whether the speech contributes to public discussion.**

## Why This Case Matters for §425.16

FilmOn resolved confusion about whether commercial speakers can use anti-SLAPP. The answer is yes in principle, but the speech still has to concern a genuine public issue. This prevents anti-SLAPP from becoming a tool for any business to dismiss lawsuits simply because its business involves communication.

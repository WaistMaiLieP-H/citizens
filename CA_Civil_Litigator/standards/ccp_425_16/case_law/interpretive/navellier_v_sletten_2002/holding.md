# Navellier v. Sletten, 29 Cal.4th 82 (2002)

## The Case

Sletten was a limited partner in Navellier's investment fund. A dispute arose, and Sletten filed a federal securities fraud action. Navellier then sued Sletten in California state court for breach of contract (a release agreement) and fraud. Sletten filed an anti-SLAPP motion, arguing the California claims arose from his protected petitioning activity — filing the federal lawsuit. Navellier argued anti-SLAPP should not apply because Sletten's federal suit was not "constitutionally protected" speech.

## What the Court Held

The California Supreme Court:

1. **"Arising from protected activity" does not mean the defendant's underlying conduct must be constitutionally protected.** At step one, the only question is whether the plaintiff's claim arises from an act in furtherance of the defendant's right of petition or free speech as defined in §425.16(e). The court does not ask at step one whether the defendant's speech was actually legitimate or constitutionally meritorious.

2. **The merits question — whether the defendant's conduct was truly protected — belongs at step two.** Step one asks only: does the claim arise from petitioning or speech activity? Step two asks: can the plaintiff show a probability of prevailing? If the defendant's speech was fraudulent or illegal, that goes to the merits analysis at step two, not the threshold question at step one.

3. **Filing a lawsuit is petitioning activity under §425.16(e)(1) and (2).** A claim that punishes someone for filing a lawsuit arises from protected activity regardless of whether that lawsuit turns out to have merit.

## The Rule

**At step one of anti-SLAPP analysis, courts ask only whether the claim arises from conduct that falls within §425.16(e)'s categories of protected activity. Whether that activity was actually constitutionally protected, legally proper, or meritorious is a step-two question. The two steps must not be merged.**

## Why This Case Matters for §425.16

Navellier prevents courts from short-circuiting the anti-SLAPP analysis by jumping to the merits at step one. Without this rule, a court could deny anti-SLAPP motions by saying "the defendant's speech was not really protected," collapsing the two-step framework. Navellier keeps the steps separate.

# Reasoning — Cal. Code of Civil Procedure § 583.310 (Five-Year Mandatory Dismissal)

**Standard ID:** CCP_583_310_FIVE_YEAR_RULE
**Layer:** 2 — Reasoning
**Status:** ACTIVE
**Filed:** 2026-04-20

---

## Why This Standard Exists

The five-year rule exists to resolve an endemic problem in civil litigation: cases filed and then left to stagnate. Without a hard deadline, the filing of a complaint creates an indefinite cloud over the defendant — the lawsuit technically exists, discovery can theoretically be demanded at any time, and settlement leverage persists without any obligation to actually prosecute the case.

The Legislature identified three distinct harms that the five-year rule addresses:

**1. Harm to defendants from perpetual litigation clouds.** A defendant named in a dormant lawsuit cannot plan, cannot get insurance without disclosing pending litigation, may have to disclose the suit in licensing applications, and remains exposed to reactivation at any time. The five-year rule sets an absolute outer limit on how long this burden can persist.

**2. Evidence degradation.** Witnesses die, move, and forget. Documents are lost. Physical evidence deteriorates. After five years, a defendant's ability to reconstruct events and mount a defense is materially compromised. Forcing defendants to trial within five years — when their evidence is still reasonably available — is a basic component of fair process.

**3. Judicial resource management.** Courts carry enormous dockets. Dormant cases consume scheduling resources, status conference slots, and judge time even when no one is actively pursuing them. The mandatory dismissal rule lets courts clear cases that plaintiffs have effectively abandoned.

---

## Why "Mandatory" Matters

The Legislature used the word "shall" in § 583.360 (dismissal shall occur), and courts have interpreted this to mean what it says: the court has no discretion. Once five years pass without trial, a defendant who moves for dismissal must receive it.

This is a deliberate legislative choice distinguishing § 583.310 from discretionary dismissal statutes. The Legislature wanted certainty — defendants and their insurers needed to know that the outer limit was an absolute rule, not a factor to be weighed against plaintiffs' excuses for delay. A "mandatory" dismissal statute with exceptions for compelling circumstances would provide neither the certainty nor the incentive the Legislature intended.

The practical effect: plaintiffs and their counsel must actively calendar the five-year date and ensure trial is set before it expires. The rule is unforgiving — missing the deadline by a single day is fatal.

---

## Tolling Under § 583.340 — Why the Exceptions Are Narrow

Section 583.340 tolls the five-year period only for time during which:
- The court's jurisdiction was suspended;
- Prosecution or trial was stayed or enjoined; or
- For any other reason beyond the plaintiff's control, trial could not be had.

Courts have interpreted the third category strictly. "Beyond the control of the plaintiff" means something structural prevented trial from occurring — not that plaintiff's counsel was busy, that settlement negotiations were ongoing, that witnesses were difficult to schedule, or that the plaintiff was resource-constrained. The burden is on the plaintiff to demonstrate that the impediment was genuinely beyond their control and actually prevented trial.

This narrow tolling prevents the five-year rule from being swallowed by plaintiff-created delays or strategic inaction. A plaintiff who fails to pursue the case actively cannot invoke § 583.340 to excuse the resulting delay.

---

## The Policy Tension — Access to Justice vs. Finality

Section 583.310 creates a real tension. In cases involving complex facts, many defendants, extensive discovery, or public entity defendants (whose Government Claims Act prerequisites add time before filing), five years can pass before a case is ready for trial. The rule does not care about complexity — it runs from the date the action was commenced, period.

Critics argue the five-year rule disproportionately affects plaintiffs in complex cases where institutional defendants can use delay as strategy. The Legislature has not moved to extend the period despite these arguments, suggesting it views the certainty and finality benefits as outweighing the access-to-justice costs.

Courts have addressed the tension at the margins through the tolling provisions — when a defendant's own delay tactics (stays, injunctions, protective orders) contributed to the time passing, that time may be excludable. But the core rule remains absolute.

---

## Historical Context

California's five-year rule originated in the 1872 Code and has remained substantially unchanged. The current codification (§§ 583.310–583.360) was part of the 1984 reorganization of dismissal statutes. The mandatory character has been reinforced repeatedly by appellate decisions that have refused to create judicial exceptions the Legislature did not write.

The rule is stricter than federal court practice (where courts have broader discretion to manage dockets) and stricter than some other states. California made a deliberate choice to have a hard rule with narrow tolling, rather than a flexible standard with broad judicial discretion.

## Application in Practice

The five-year rule is a constant pressure on plaintiff's counsel in California civil litigation:
- The trial date must be obtained before the five-year deadline;
- Obtaining a trial date does not toll the period — the action must be *brought to trial* within five years;
- If trial is set but continued past the five-year mark without the case in trial, dismissal may still result;
- Government entity defendants can move for dismissal even if they caused delays, unless the delays were by stay or injunction;
- Mediation, arbitration, and settlement negotiations do not toll the period.

The interaction with the Government Claims Act is particularly treacherous: the claim presentation requirement (and the time to exhaust it) does not suspend the five-year clock from the original complaint date. Plaintiffs who file early and then wait for the claim process to run its course may eat significantly into their five-year window before discovery even begins.

# CCP §583.310 — Origin Context

## Enactment

The five-year rule in its current codification: Stats. 1984, Ch. 1705 (Trial Court Delay Reduction Act). But the rule itself is much older. It derives from former CCP §583, which existed in substantially similar form dating to at least 1933 and arguably earlier. The 1984 Act reorganized the delay reduction statutes into §§583.110-583.430 but did not change the substance of the five-year rule.

## The wound

Cases sat on court dockets for years — sometimes decades — without being tried. Plaintiffs filed lawsuits and then did not prosecute them. Defendants lived under the shadow of pending litigation with no resolution. Courts carried dead cases on their dockets. Witnesses died, memories faded, evidence was lost. The longer a case sat unfiled, the less likely it was that justice could be done.

The problem was not new. Courts had inherent power to dismiss for failure to prosecute, but that power was discretionary and inconsistently applied. Some cases lingered indefinitely. The Legislature decided that five years was enough.

## The promise

The five-year rule is the harshest deadline in California civil practice. The promise is absolute: if a case is not brought to trial within five years of being filed, it SHALL be dismissed. Not "may be dismissed." Shall. The court has no discretion. The five-year clock is jurisdictional — when it expires, the court loses the power to try the case.

This is not a gentle encouragement to move cases along. It is a mandatory cutoff. The Legislature decided that after five years, the interests in finality, in clearing dockets, and in preventing stale litigation outweigh the interests in resolving the dispute on the merits.

## The tension with the merits preference

The five-year rule exists in tension with CCP §583.130, which declares the policy of the State of California to favor the trial of actions on their merits. The Legislature wants cases tried on the merits. The Legislature also wants cases tried within five years. When these two policies collide — when a meritorious case cannot be tried within five years — the five-year rule wins. Dismissal is mandatory.

The only escape valves are in CCP §583.340: impossibility, impracticability, or futility of bringing the action to trial. These are narrow exceptions. "The case was complicated" is not impossibility. "My lawyer was busy" is not impracticability. The exceptions require circumstances beyond the control of the parties.

## The malpractice dimension

Missing the five-year deadline is legal malpractice per se. There is no defense. If an attorney allows the five-year clock to expire on a client's case, that attorney has committed malpractice as a matter of law. The client's case is gone, and the attorney is liable for whatever the case was worth.

This makes the five-year rule the single most important calendaring deadline in a litigator's practice. It cannot be extended by stipulation. It cannot be waived by the parties. It cannot be excused by the court. It can only be tolled under the narrow statutory exceptions.

## Source

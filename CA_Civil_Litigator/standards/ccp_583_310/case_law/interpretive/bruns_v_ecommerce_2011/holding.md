# Bruns v. E-Commerce Exchange, Inc., 51 Cal.4th 717 (2011)

## What This Case Stands For

The impossibility/impracticability exception to the five-year rule is narrow. It does not open a wide door. To toll the five-year period under CCP 583.340(c), the plaintiff must show a genuine condition beyond their control that actually prevented bringing the case to trial.

Court congestion alone does not qualify. The plaintiff must show they did everything they could and still could not get to trial because of something they had no power to change. The burden is on the plaintiff to prove the exception applies.

## The Rule

Under CCP 583.340(c), the five-year period is tolled only when bringing the action to trial was "impossible, impracticable, or futile." This requires:

1. A condition genuinely **beyond the plaintiff's control**.
2. The condition must have **actually prevented** bringing the case to trial.
3. The plaintiff must have exercised **reasonable diligence** in prosecuting the case despite the condition.
4. **Court congestion alone** does not satisfy the standard.
5. The **plaintiff bears the burden** of establishing that the exception applies.

## What It Means in Practice

- You cannot blame the court's busy calendar for your failure to get to trial within five years.
- You cannot blame your own discovery delays, settlement negotiations, or attorney changes.
- Legitimate impossibility might include: the defendant absconding from the jurisdiction, a stay imposed by a bankruptcy proceeding, or a court order preventing the case from proceeding.
- The exception is meant for situations where the plaintiff was genuinely blocked from moving forward -- not situations where the plaintiff could have been more diligent.
- If you are approaching the five-year mark, you must be prepared to show EXACTLY what prevented trial and EXACTLY what you did to try to get there.

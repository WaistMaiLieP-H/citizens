# CCP §583.310 — Historical Chain (Five-Year Rule)

---

### I. The Wound

Lawsuits filed and never tried. Cases sitting on dockets for five years, ten years, longer. Plaintiffs who filed suit and then disappeared. Defendants living under the cloud of unresolved litigation for years with no end in sight. Witnesses dying. Evidence degrading. Memories fading. Courts carrying hundreds of dormant cases on their calendars.

The problem was simple: there was no hard deadline to bring a case to trial. Courts had inherent discretionary power to dismiss for failure to prosecute, but discretion means inconsistency. Some judges were aggressive about clearing dormant cases. Others were not. The result was a system where cases could linger indefinitely, serving no one — not the plaintiff, not the defendant, not the court, not the public.

### II. The Promise (Five Years — At Least 1933, Codified 1984)

The five-year rule is the Legislature's answer: a mandatory, non-discretionary, jurisdictional deadline. If the plaintiff does not bring the case to trial within five years of filing, the case is dismissed. The court has no choice. The parties cannot agree to extend it. The court cannot extend it.

The word "shall" does all the work. "An action shall be brought to trial within five years." Not "should." Not "may." Shall. This is a command, not a suggestion.

### III. The Jurisdictional Nature

This is what makes the five-year rule unique among California procedural deadlines. It is jurisdictional. When the five-year clock expires, the court does not merely have the option to dismiss — it loses the power to try the case. The distinction matters: a discretionary dismissal can be reviewed for abuse of discretion. A jurisdictional bar cannot be waived, stipulated around, or ignored.

The practical effect: no last-minute motions, no stipulations, no judicial mercy can save a case after five years. The power to try the case simply no longer exists.

### IV. The Tolling Exceptions (§583.340)

The five-year clock can be tolled — paused — but only for specific, narrow reasons:

1. **Impossibility, impracticability, or futility** of bringing the action to trial. This requires circumstances beyond the control of the plaintiff. Attorney negligence does not qualify. Case complexity does not qualify. Court congestion alone does not qualify. The standard is high.

2. **Stays.** If the case is stayed — by court order, by bankruptcy, by any legally operative stay — the time during the stay is excluded.

3. **Court-ordered suspensions.** If the court suspends proceedings, that time is excluded.

These exceptions are questions of fact, but the courts interpret them narrowly. The five-year rule is supposed to be hard. If the exceptions were easy, the rule would be meaningless.

### V. The Malpractice Per Se Rule

Missing the five-year deadline is malpractice. Not potential malpractice. Not arguable malpractice. Malpractice as a matter of law — per se. There is no defense available to the attorney. The case is dead, and the attorney is liable for its full value.

This makes the five-year deadline the most consequential calendaring entry in a California litigator's practice. Every other deadline has some flexibility — extensions, stipulations, relief from default. The five-year rule has none. An attorney who loses track of it has committed the one unforgivable error in civil practice.

### VI. The Policy Tension

CCP §583.130 states the policy: California favors trial on the merits. Every case should be resolved on the substance of the dispute, not on procedural technicalities. This is a real policy, applied by courts in many contexts — granting relief from default, allowing amended pleadings, construing rules liberally in favor of disposition on the merits.

The five-year rule is the exception. After five years, the policy in favor of merits resolution yields to the policy in favor of finality. The Legislature decided that five years is enough time. If the case cannot be tried in five years, the interests in finality, docket management, and protection of defendants from stale litigation outweigh the interest in reaching the merits.

### VII. Computation

The five years runs from the date the action is COMMENCED — the date of filing. Not the date of service. Not the date the defendant answers. The filing date. This is computed under §583.360, and time during tolling events is excluded.

The parties cannot extend it by stipulation (§583.140). The court cannot extend it by order. The only things that extend it are the statutory tolling provisions in §583.340.

### VIII. Why This Standard Is Load-Bearing

One sentence of statutory text. Twenty-two words. And it is the single most inflexible, most consequential, most unforgiving deadline in California civil litigation. Every case filed in California superior court carries this clock from the moment the complaint is filed. Every litigator must track it. Every case management plan must account for it. Missing it is the end of the case and the beginning of a malpractice claim.

The five-year rule is the Legislature's statement that litigation has a shelf life. Five years. No more.

# Reasoning — Cal. Code of Civil Procedure § 340.5 (MICRA — Medical Malpractice SOL)

**Standard ID:** CCP_340_5_MICRA_SOL
**Layer:** 2 — Reasoning
**Status:** ACTIVE
**Filed:** 2026-04-20

---

## Why This Standard Exists — The MICRA Context

Section 340.5 is part of the Medical Injury Compensation Reform Act of 1975 (MICRA), enacted during a perceived medical malpractice insurance crisis. California insurers were increasing premiums sharply or leaving the market, and the Legislature determined that without reform, healthcare availability would be threatened.

MICRA's limitations — including the § 340.5 SOL and the § 3333.2 non-economic damages cap ($250,000 at enactment, now adjusted under SB 1) — were explicitly designed to reduce the volume and cost of medical malpractice litigation. The Legislature made a deliberate policy tradeoff: limiting plaintiff rights in exchange for stable healthcare access and insurance markets.

This is a contested political balance. Plaintiff advocates have consistently challenged MICRA as tilting unfairly against injured patients. The Legislature substantially amended MICRA's damages cap in 2022 (SB 1) but preserved the § 340.5 SOL framework.

---

## The Dual-Trigger Structure — Why Both Clocks Run Simultaneously

**Three years from injury:** This is the outer absolute limit. It serves a repose function — ensuring that healthcare providers are not indefinitely exposed to claims from patients who received treatment years earlier. After three years, physical and documentary evidence degrades, witnesses' memories fade, and providers may be unable to reconstruct the standard of care at the time of treatment.

**One year from discovery:** The discovery rule exists because medical malpractice injuries are often latent. A patient who receives a negligent diagnosis may not discover the misdiagnosis until years later when a subsequent provider identifies the error. A patient who undergoes a procedure may not connect a resulting complication to provider negligence without expert explanation.

Without the discovery rule, the three-year period would begin running even when the patient had no way to know the injury resulted from malpractice. The one-year clock from discovery prevents this injustice while still imposing a deadline: once the patient knows (or reasonably should know) of the injury and its probable cause, they have one year to act.

**Whichever runs first:** The "whichever occurs first" rule means a patient who discovers malpractice near the three-year mark has less than the full discovery year remaining. This design prevents the discovery rule from functionally eliminating the three-year limit.

---

## The Discovery Rule — What Triggers the One-Year Clock

The clock starts when the plaintiff "discovers, or through the use of reasonable diligence should have discovered, the injury." This is an objective test, not subjective ignorance.

Courts have interpreted the trigger carefully: the plaintiff must have reason to suspect that a wrong was done — not certainty, and not complete knowledge of all facts needed to prove the case. The Norgart standard (*Norgart v. Upjohn Co.* (1999) 21 Cal.4th 383): the limitations period begins when the plaintiff suspects or should suspect that her injury was caused by wrongdoing.

Deliberate concealment by the provider does not toll the three-year limit under the ordinary discovery rule — it requires the separate fraud/concealment exception.

---

## Tolling Exceptions — Why These Three and Not Others

**Fraud and intentional concealment:** A provider who affirmatively misrepresents to a patient that no malpractice occurred, or who hides evidence of the error, cannot invoke the three-year limit they helped obscure. The policy rationale is clear: the limitation period exists to protect defendants who could not reasonably be expected to defend old claims, not to protect defendants who actively prevented plaintiffs from learning of the claims.

**Foreign body:** A surgical instrument, sponge, or other material left inside a patient has no diagnostic or therapeutic purpose — its presence is inherently anomalous. Discovery of such a body is itself evidence of negligence. The Legislature chose to treat foreign body cases as categorically different because the existence of the body provides ongoing, objective evidence unaffected by testimonial limitations.

The exceptions do not extend to cases where the patient simply failed to investigate or where the provider legitimately concealed the claim through non-disclosure without affirmative misrepresentation. The tolling exceptions are narrow and intentionally so.

---

## Minors — Why Special Rules Apply

Children who experience medical malpractice cannot protect their own rights. Parents may not discover or pursue claims. The three-year from injury / until 8th birthday rule for children under six gives a minimum period long enough to allow parental discovery and pursuit of claims while maintaining the repose function for older minors.

---

## Why MICRA Applies Only to Professional Negligence

The SOL in § 340.5 applies to professional negligence — the rendering of professional services within the scope of the provider's license. Claims that do not arise from the exercise of professional medical judgment may fall under different statutes:
- Intentional torts against patients: different SOL;
- Products liability claims against medical device manufacturers: § 337.1 or § 338;
- General negligence by healthcare facility employees (slip and fall on hospital grounds): § 335.1 (two years).

Mischaracterizing the nature of the claim to avoid MICRA, or failing to recognize when MICRA applies, are the two most common § 340.5 errors in practice.

---

## Application in Practice

Section 340.5 is the gating analysis for every California medical malpractice case:
- When did the injury occur? (Three-year outer limit)
- When did plaintiff discover (or should have discovered) the injury? (One-year discovery clock)
- Does any tolling exception apply? (Fraud, concealment, foreign body)
- Is the plaintiff a minor? (Special rules)
- Was the defendant a "health care provider" acting in "professional negligence"? (Scope check)

Government Claims Act intersection: when the defendant is a public hospital or county health system, § 911.2 (6-month government claim presentation) applies in addition to § 340.5. The government claim deadline may run before the MICRA SOL, making it the controlling deadline.

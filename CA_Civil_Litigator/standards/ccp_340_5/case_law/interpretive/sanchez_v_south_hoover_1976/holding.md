# Sanchez v. South Hoover Hospital, 18 Cal.3d 93 (1976)

## What This Case Stands For

MICRA -- the Medical Injury Compensation Reform Act of 1975, which includes CCP 340.5's shortened statute of limitations for medical malpractice -- is constitutional.

Plaintiffs challenged MICRA on multiple grounds: due process, equal protection, right to jury trial, access to courts. The California Supreme Court rejected all challenges. The Legislature was responding to a genuine crisis in medical malpractice insurance, and the reforms -- including shortened limitations periods, damages caps, and attorney fee limits -- were a rational exercise of legislative power to address that crisis.

## The Rule

CCP 340.5's limitations framework -- one year from discovery or three years from injury, whichever comes first -- is a valid exercise of legislative power. The shortened period does not violate the California Constitution.

## What It Means in Practice

- MICRA's limitations period is the law and it is enforceable. Constitutional challenges to the one-year/three-year framework will fail.
- This means medical malpractice plaintiffs have significantly less time than plaintiffs in other personal injury cases (which have a two-year statute under CCP 335.1 with no outer cap).
- The three-year outer limit is absolute -- even if you could not reasonably have discovered the injury, you are barred after three years from the date of injury (with narrow exceptions for fraud, foreign bodies, or minority).
- Sanchez is the foundational case upholding the entire MICRA framework. Any challenge to CCP 340.5's constitutionality starts and ends here.

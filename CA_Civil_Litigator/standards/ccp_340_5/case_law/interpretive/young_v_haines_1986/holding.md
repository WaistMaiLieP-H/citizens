# Young v. Haines, 41 Cal.3d 883 (1986)

## What This Case Stands For

The one-year discovery clock under CCP 340.5 starts ticking when the patient has a "suspicion of wrongdoing" -- not when the patient has certainty, not when the patient gets a second opinion confirming malpractice, and not when the patient consults a lawyer who says they have a case.

Suspicion is the trigger. If you suspect something went wrong with your medical care, the clock is running. You do not need to know exactly what the doctor did wrong or who specifically was responsible. The moment you have reason to suspect that your injury was caused by someone's wrongdoing, you have one year to file.

## The Rule

Under CCP 340.5, the one-year limitations period begins when the plaintiff:

1. Suspects, or reasonably should suspect, that the injury was caused by wrongdoing; AND
2. Has some knowledge of the injury itself.

"Suspicion of wrongdoing" does not mean certainty. It does not mean knowledge of the specific negligent act. It means the plaintiff has reason to believe -- even tentatively -- that something went wrong.

## What It Means in Practice

- If your surgery went badly and you suspect it was because the surgeon made a mistake, the clock starts NOW -- not when you confirm it through records review or expert opinion.
- Waiting to get a second opinion, waiting to obtain medical records, or waiting to consult a lawyer does not stop the clock from running.
- The standard is subjective-objective: what the plaintiff actually knew, combined with what a reasonable person in the plaintiff's position should have known.
- This is a plaintiff-unfriendly rule. It means the one-year window can expire before the plaintiff fully understands what happened.
- Practical advice: if you suspect something went wrong with medical care, treat that moment as the start of a one-year countdown.

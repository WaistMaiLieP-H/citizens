# Knowles v. Superior Court, 118 Cal.App.4th 1290 (2004)

## What This Case Stands For

The discovery rule under CCP 340.5 is triggered when the plaintiff knows (or should know) about the INJURY and its CAUSE -- not when the plaintiff knows the injury resulted from professional negligence specifically.

There is a difference between knowing you were hurt by something and knowing that the thing that hurt you was malpractice. The clock starts at the first one. You do not get extra time because you did not realize the injury was the result of someone falling below the standard of care.

This refines Young v. Haines: suspicion of wrongdoing means awareness of the injury and what caused it, not awareness that the cause constituted legal negligence.

## The Rule

Under CCP 340.5, the one-year discovery period begins when the plaintiff has knowledge of:

1. The **injury** (the harm itself); AND
2. The **cause** of the injury (what factually happened to produce the harm).

The plaintiff does NOT need to know:
- That the cause constituted professional negligence.
- The legal theory of liability.
- The specific standard of care that was breached.

## What It Means in Practice

- If you had surgery and your arm does not work afterward, and you know the surgery caused it, the clock is running -- even if you have no idea whether the surgeon made a mistake or whether the outcome was an unavoidable complication.
- The question is factual knowledge (what happened to you), not legal knowledge (whether what happened was malpractice).
- This means the clock can start running even when the plaintiff reasonably believes the outcome was just bad luck rather than negligence.
- Combined with Young v. Haines, the standard is: suspicion that your injury was caused by something (factual cause), not suspicion that the something was below the standard of care (legal negligence).
- This is an appellate court decision, not Supreme Court, but it is widely cited and followed.

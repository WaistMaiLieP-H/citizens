# CCP §340.5 — Historical Chain (Medical Malpractice Statute of Limitations)

---

### I. The Wound (Two Sides)

Every standard has a wound — the harm that existed before the law was enacted. §340.5 has two wounds, and both are real.

**The wound the Legislature addressed (1975):** Medical malpractice insurance premiums were making medical practice economically impossible. Insurers were leaving the market. Doctors were threatening to stop practicing. The Legislature determined that the litigation system was driving costs and convened a special session.

**The wound the statute created:** By shortening the limitations period below the general personal injury standard, §340.5 made it harder for injured patients to get justice. A patient who does not discover their injury within one year of receiving treatment — or within three years of the date of injury — loses their right to sue, even if the injury was caused by clear negligence. The people most affected are those with injuries that take time to manifest: misdiagnoses, failed implants, surgical errors that do not show symptoms for years.

Both wounds are documented because both are real. The standard does not pick a side.

### II. The Promise (1975)

MICRA's promise was stability in the medical malpractice insurance market. The Legislature traded patient access to justice for medical practice viability. §340.5 was one piece of that trade:

- Shortened the filing window (three years from injury or one year from discovery, whichever is first)
- Created narrow tolling exceptions (fraud, concealment, foreign body)
- Added specific provisions for minors

The promise to providers: a defined limitations window that allows you to close your books on old cases. After three years (absent tolling), you cannot be sued for malpractice. The promise to insurers: predictable exposure windows that allow you to price and reserve accurately.

### III. The Dual-Trigger Structure

This is what makes §340.5 distinctive and dangerous for plaintiffs.

Most statutes of limitations have one trigger. CCP §335.1 (general personal injury) runs from discovery — when you knew or should have known about the injury. §340.5 has two triggers, and whichever fires first controls:

**Trigger 1: Three years from the date of injury.** This runs regardless of whether the patient knows they were injured. The surgery happened. The error was made. Three years later, the clock expires. Period.

**Trigger 2: One year from discovery.** This starts when the patient discovers, or through reasonable diligence should have discovered, the injury. If the patient discovers the injury six months after the surgery, the clock starts then and expires one year later — even though the three-year clock still has time left.

The word "whichever occurs first" is the blade. It means the plaintiff is always governed by whichever deadline is shorter. Discover the injury quickly and you have only one year. Never discover it and you have only three years.

### IV. The Tolling Provisions

The three-year outside limit is not absolute. Three exceptions toll it:

1. **Fraud:** The health care provider committed fraud in connection with the treatment or the injury.
2. **Intentional concealment:** The provider actively concealed the injury or the negligent cause. Not mere silence — affirmative concealment.
3. **Foreign body:** A non-therapeutic, non-diagnostic foreign body was left in the patient. The classic example: a surgical sponge.

These are narrow by design. The Legislature intended the three-year limit to be meaningful. Only affirmative misconduct extends it.

### V. The Minor Provisions

Children receive additional protection:

- **General rule:** Three years from the wrongful act
- **Under age six:** Three years from the act OR until the child's eighth birthday, whichever provides a longer period
- **Collusion tolling:** The minor's limitations period is tolled during any period when the parent or guardian AND the defendant's insurer or health care provider have committed fraud or collusion in failing to bring the minor's claim

The collusion provision is striking. It recognizes a scenario where the adults responsible for protecting the child — the parent, the guardian — are aligned with the defendant in preventing the child's claim from being brought. The statute extends the child's time when the people who should be acting on the child's behalf are instead working against the child's interests.

### VI. Relationship to the General Personal Injury Statute

CCP §335.1 provides a two-year statute of limitations for personal injury, running from discovery. No outside limit. No three-year cap. Just: two years from when you knew or should have known.

§340.5 is stricter in every respect:
- The discovery trigger is one year (not two)
- There is a three-year outside limit (no equivalent in §335.1)
- The tolling provisions are narrow and specific

A patient injured by medical malpractice has less time to sue than a person injured by any other type of negligence. This was a deliberate legislative choice.

### VII. Why This Standard Is Load-Bearing

Every medical malpractice case in California lives or dies by §340.5. Miss the deadline and the claim is gone — regardless of the severity of the injury, regardless of the clarity of the negligence, regardless of the harm caused. The statute does not care about the merits. It cares about the clock.

The tolling provisions — particularly fraud and intentional concealment — are the only lifeline for patients whose injuries were hidden from them. Understanding these provisions and knowing how to invoke them is the difference between a viable claim and a time-barred one.

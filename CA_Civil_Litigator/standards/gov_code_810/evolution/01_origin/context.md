# Gov. Code §810 — Origin Context

## Enactment

Stats. 1963, Ch. 1681. Effective January 1, 1964. The entire Government Claims Act (§§810-996.6) was enacted as a comprehensive replacement for the prior regime of government immunity and liability.

## The wound

Before 1963, government liability in California was governed by a patchwork of case law and scattered statutes built on the foundation of sovereign immunity — the principle inherited from English common law that "the king can do no wrong." The practical effect: when a government employee driving a government vehicle ran someone over, when a public hospital committed malpractice, when a defective government road caused an accident — the injured person often had no remedy. The government was immune.

The California Supreme Court had been eroding sovereign immunity through case law, most notably in Muskopf v. Corning Hospital District, 55 Cal.2d 211 (1961), which abolished sovereign immunity entirely. But this created its own problem: without any statutory framework, the scope of government liability was undefined. Could you sue for any government act? Were there any limits? What procedures applied?

The Legislature responded to Muskopf by first imposing a moratorium on its effect (to buy time), then commissioning the California Law Revision Commission to design a complete statutory framework. The result was the 1963 Government Claims Act.

## The promise

The Government Claims Act replaced sovereign immunity with a structured statutory framework. The core principles:

1. **Public entities are liable only where a statute says they are.** (§815) — This is not a return to sovereign immunity. It is a requirement that liability be defined by statute, not left to case-by-case judicial expansion.

2. **Vicarious liability through employees.** (§815.2) — A public entity is liable for its employee's acts to the same extent the employee is personally liable. If the employee committed a tort within the scope of employment, the entity pays.

3. **Specific immunities where policy requires.** (§§818-825.6) — Discretionary immunity, emergency response immunity, etc. The legislature decided which activities warrant protection from suit.

4. **Claim presentation requirement.** (§§900-915.4) — Before you can sue a public entity, you must file a government tort claim within six months of the accrual of the cause of action (or one year for certain claims). If the entity denies the claim or fails to respond within 45 days, you can then file suit. This is a prerequisite. Missing the deadline can be fatal.

## The critical federal exception

The claim presentation requirement does NOT apply to federal civil rights claims under 42 USC §1983. The U.S. Supreme Court held in Felder v. Casey, 487 U.S. 131 (1988) that state tort claim requirements cannot be imposed as a precondition to §1983 claims. This is true whether the §1983 claim is filed in federal court or state court.

This means: if the harm was a violation of federal constitutional rights committed under color of state law, you go straight to court under §1983. You do not file a government tort claim first. But if you have state-law tort claims against the same government entity (negligence, false imprisonment, etc.), those claims DO require claim presentation under the Government Claims Act.

## The California Law Revision Commission

The 1963 Act was based on the Commission's detailed study and recommendations. This is significant because the Commission's reports explain the reasoning behind each provision. The reports are the legislative history — they document what problem each section was designed to solve.

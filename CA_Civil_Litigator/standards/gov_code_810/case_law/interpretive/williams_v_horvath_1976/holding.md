# Williams v. Horvath, 16 Cal.3d 834 (1976)

## What This Case Stands For

The Government Claims Act did not bring back sovereign immunity. It replaced blanket immunity with a statutory framework -- a structured system of specific liabilities and specific immunities that must be read as a whole.

After Muskopf abolished sovereign immunity and the Legislature enacted the Government Claims Act, there was a question: did the Act effectively restore the old immunity under a new name? Williams answered: no. The Act creates a balanced system. Public entities are liable under specific statutory provisions (like Gov. Code 815.2 for employee torts), and they are immune under other specific statutory provisions (like the discretionary immunity in Gov. Code 820.2). The Act must be interpreted as a comprehensive scheme, not as a return to blanket immunity with a few exceptions.

## The Rule

The Government Claims Act (Gov. Code 810 et seq.) is a comprehensive statutory scheme that:

1. Eliminates common law tort liability for public entities (Gov. Code 815).
2. Creates specific statutory bases for public entity liability (Gov. Code 815.2, 815.4, 815.6).
3. Creates specific statutory immunities (Gov. Code 818-825.6).
4. Must be read as a whole -- the liabilities and immunities are part of an integrated framework.

Public entity liability exists only where a statute says it does. But the statute says it does in many important places, and the immunities do not swallow the liabilities.

## What It Means in Practice

- You cannot sue a public entity on a pure common law negligence theory. You need a statute that creates liability.
- But Gov. Code 815.2 creates vicarious liability for employee torts within the scope of employment -- and that covers most negligence scenarios.
- The specific immunities in the Act must be narrowly construed because they are exceptions to the general scheme of liability, not the other way around.
- Williams is cited whenever someone argues the Government Claims Act should be read broadly to shield the government. The answer is: the Act is a balanced framework, not a restoration of immunity.

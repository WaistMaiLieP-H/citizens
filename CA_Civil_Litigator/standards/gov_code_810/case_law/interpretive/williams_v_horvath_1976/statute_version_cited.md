# Statute Version Cited: Gov. Code 810 et seq. as of 1976

## The Relevant Text

The court was reading the Government Claims Act as enacted in 1963 (Stats. 1963, Ch. 1681) with amendments through 1976. Key provisions:

**Gov. Code 810.8**: "'Public entity' includes the state, the Regents of the University of California, a county, city, district, public authority, public agency, and any other political subdivision or public corporation in the state."

**Gov. Code 815**: "Except as otherwise provided by statute: (a) A public entity is not liable for an injury, whether such injury arises out of an act or omission of the public entity or a public employee or any other person."

**Gov. Code 815.2**: "(a) A public entity is liable for injury proximately caused by an act or omission of an employee of the public entity within the scope of his employment if the act or omission would, apart from this section, have given rise to a cause of action against that employee..."

## What the Court Was Deciding

Whether the Act's elimination of common law liability (815) effectively restored sovereign immunity, or whether the specific statutory liability provisions (815.2 and others) created a meaningful framework of government accountability.

The court held it was the latter. The Act is a balanced whole. Section 815 closes one door (common law liability), but sections 815.2, 815.4, and 815.6 open specific doors that cover the vast majority of government tort liability scenarios.

## What Changed (and What Did Not)

The core structure of the Government Claims Act -- no common law liability, specific statutory liabilities, specific statutory immunities -- has remained unchanged since 1963. Amendments have added specific liability and immunity provisions, but the fundamental framework Williams interpreted remains intact.

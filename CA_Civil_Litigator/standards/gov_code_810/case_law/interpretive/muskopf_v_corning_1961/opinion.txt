MUSKOPF v. CORNING HOSPITAL DISTRICT
55 Cal.2d 211 (1961)
California Supreme Court
Decided: December 27, 1961

---

FACTS:

Vera Muskopf was injured when she slipped and fell at a county hospital. She sued the hospital district for negligence. The hospital district asserted governmental immunity — the common law doctrine that governments are immune from suit for their own negligence.

The California Supreme Court addressed whether the doctrine of governmental immunity from tort liability should survive in California.

---

HOLDING:

Justice Traynor wrote for a unanimous Court.

ABOLISHING GOVERNMENTAL IMMUNITY IN CALIFORNIA:
"The rule that a governmental body is immune from tort liability is an anachronism that has no place in modern law."

"The doctrine of governmental immunity was based on the fiction that the king can do no wrong — a principle that has no place in a constitutional democracy. This court now holds that the rule of governmental immunity from tort is hereby abrogated in California."

RATIONALE FOR ABOLITION:
1. The government, like any other employer, should be responsible for the negligent acts of its employees
2. The costs of tortious government activity should be spread across the public through the government's ability to budget and insure, rather than concentrated on innocent victims
3. There is no principled basis for distinguishing between government and private-sector liability in negligence cases
4. The "public duty" doctrine and other immunity justifications do not withstand scrutiny in modern administrative government

THE MUSKOPF PRINCIPLE:
"When the government acts, it acts through employees. Government employees are no different from private employees in their capacity to act negligently. If a private hospital's employees negligently injure a patient, the hospital is liable. There is no principled reason why the same rule should not apply to a government hospital."

---

WHAT HAPPENED AFTER MUSKOPF — LEGISLATIVE RESPONSE:
The California Legislature quickly responded to Muskopf by enacting the California Tort Claims Act (Government Claims Act), now codified at Gov. Code §§ 810 et seq. The Act:
1. Restored a modified form of government immunity for specified categories of government action (discretionary acts, design immunity, etc.)
2. Required presentation of a Government Claims Act claim before filing suit
3. Defined which government actions remain immune and which are subject to liability under § 815.2 vicarious liability and § 815 direct liability for statutory violations

Muskopf's principle — that government is not categorically immune — survived. The Legislature restored specific immunities, but the baseline rule is now that government is liable unless a specific immunity applies.

---

APPLICATION TO STEWARD'S CASES:

Muskopf is the historical anchor for California government tort liability:

1. **Government Claims Act is mandatory before suing**: Because the Legislature responded to Muskopf by enacting the Claims Act, the Claims Act's presentation requirement (file a written claim with the agency within six months of accrual) is a prerequisite to any civil suit against a California government entity.

2. **Baseline = liability**: After Muskopf, the starting presumption is that government defendants are liable for their employees' negligence, just like private defendants. Immunity is the exception — the government must identify and prove a specific statutory immunity.

3. **Current framework (§ 815.2)**: Today, California government liability is governed by Gov. Code § 815.2 (vicarious liability for employee acts within scope) and specific immunity provisions. Mary M. v. City of Los Angeles (see CA_Civil_Rights_Litigator) applies the coercive authority theory to extend § 815.2 to intentional torts by officers using official power.

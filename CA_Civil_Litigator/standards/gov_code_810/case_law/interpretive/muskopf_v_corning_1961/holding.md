# Muskopf v. Corning Hospital District, 55 Cal.2d 211 (1961)

## What This Case Stands For

Before this case, you could not sue the government in California. The doctrine of sovereign immunity -- inherited from English common law -- meant the government could not be held liable for injuries it caused, no matter how negligent or wrongful the conduct.

The California Supreme Court abolished that immunity. The court said sovereign immunity was a relic that had no place in modern law. If the government hurts you through its employees' negligence, you should be able to sue just like you could sue a private employer.

This decision forced the California Legislature's hand. Within two years, the Legislature enacted the Government Claims Act (Government Code 810 et seq., effective 1963) to create a comprehensive statutory framework replacing the old common law immunity. The Act did not restore blanket immunity -- it created a structured system of specific liabilities and specific immunities.

## The Rule

Sovereign immunity is abolished in California. The government can be sued. But the Legislature responded by creating the Government Claims Act, which now controls when and how you can sue public entities.

## What It Means in Practice

- Muskopf is the reason the Government Claims Act exists. Without this case, there would be no Gov. Code 810.
- Every government tort claim in California traces its legal foundation back to this decision.
- The Act that replaced sovereign immunity is not a return to immunity. It is a statutory framework that defines specific situations where the government is liable and specific situations where it is immune.
- Understanding Muskopf is essential to understanding WHY the Government Claims Act reads the way it does -- it was the Legislature's answer to the court's abolition of blanket immunity.

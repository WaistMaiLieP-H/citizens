# Reasoning — Cal. Government Code §§ 810–996.6 (Government Claims Act)

**Standard ID:** GOV_CODE_810_GOVERNMENT_CLAIMS_ACT
**Layer:** 2 — Reasoning
**Status:** ACTIVE
**Filed:** 2026-04-20

---

## Why This Standard Exists

At common law, the doctrine of sovereign immunity held that the government could not be sued without its consent. California's Supreme Court abrogated sovereign immunity in *Muskopf v. Corning Hospital District* (1961) 55 Cal.2d 211, holding that it was fundamentally unjust to leave injured persons without a remedy against government tortfeasors.

The Legislature responded immediately — not to restore immunity, but to create a managed framework for government liability that protected public entities from unmanageable litigation exposure while preserving remedies for injured citizens. The result was the Government Claims Act, enacted in 1963 and codified at Gov. Code §§ 810 et seq.

The Act does not restore immunity — it structures the path to recovery. Every major provision of the Act reflects a balance between two competing interests: (1) the right of injured persons to be compensated by government actors whose negligence harmed them; and (2) the legitimate government interest in managing fiscal exposure, investigating claims before litigation, and preserving evidence while it is fresh.

---

## Why "Liability Only by Statute" (§ 815)

Section 815 restates the post-*Muskopf* settlement: California public entities have no common law liability. All government liability in California is statutory. This means every theory of liability against a public entity must trace to a specific California statute authorizing it.

This limitation serves a critical function: it prevents courts from expanding government liability through common law development without legislative authorization. Each time a plaintiff sues a government entity, the first question is: which statute authorizes this claim? If the answer is none, the claim fails regardless of how egregious the conduct was.

For practice: § 815.2 (vicarious liability through employees) is the most commonly invoked statutory basis. A public entity can be liable for its employee's tortious acts within the scope of employment — but only if the employee would have been liable.

---

## Why Claim Presentation Is Mandatory (§ 945.4)

The claim presentation requirement — often called the "government tort claim" or simply "the claim" — is the most practically important provision in the Act. It serves four purposes:

**1. Notice and investigation.** Government entities need to know a claim exists before litigation is filed so they can investigate while evidence is fresh, interview witnesses, preserve documents, and make early assessments about liability. A government hospital sued three years after a medical incident may find the relevant medical staff have left, records have been retired, and reconstruction is impossible.

**2. Settlement opportunity.** The 45-day rejection period (§ 912.4) gives the entity an opportunity to settle meritorious claims before litigation. Many claims are resolved at the administrative stage, saving public resources. The Legislature wanted to create a pre-litigation window — not merely a formality.

**3. Fiscal planning.** Government entities must budget for anticipated liabilities. Claims presentation gives fiscal officers advance notice of potential expenditures, enabling budgetary reserves and insurance notice.

**4. Jurisdictional bar as enforcement mechanism.** The Legislature could have made claim presentation a procedural requirement with flexible exceptions. It chose instead to make failure to present a timely claim a jurisdictional bar — the court lacks subject-matter jurisdiction over a suit filed without a properly presented claim. This categorical consequence ensures that the requirement is taken seriously by claimants' counsel and prevents the notice function from being undermined by routine waivers.

---

## Why Six Months for Personal Injury (§ 911.2)

The six-month deadline for personal injury and property damage claims is shorter than the otherwise applicable two-year statute of limitations under § 335.1. This shorter window reflects the specific notice interest: personal injury claims are the category of claims most likely to benefit from immediate investigation. Physical injuries are best documented at the time of occurrence; scene evidence degrades quickly; witness identification is time-sensitive.

The six-month period begins at accrual — typically the date of injury, though the discovery rule can delay accrual when the injury is latent or its governmental cause is not apparent.

The 12-month period for other claims (§ 911.2(b)) reflects that non-personal-injury claims (contract claims, inverse condemnation) typically involve less time-sensitive physical evidence.

---

## Why the Late Claim Application (§ 911.4) Exists

The Legislature recognized that six months is a short window for seriously injured persons. A plaintiff incapacitated by the government's negligence may not be able to identify counsel, investigate the claim, and present the claim within six months. The late claim application — available within one year of accrual — gives courts and agencies a valve for cases where the failure to present timely was the result of mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect.

But the late claim application is discretionary relief, not a right. The bar remains — the court does not have subject-matter jurisdiction over a suit without a properly presented claim or a court order deeming a late claim application granted.

---

## Why § 945.4 Is a Jurisdictional Bar, Not Just an Affirmative Defense

Courts have consistently held that compliance with the Government Claims Act is jurisdictional — a suit filed without a properly presented claim must be dismissed, not merely stayed. This is not an accident of phrasing. The Legislature intended the bar to be categorical precisely because the notice interest is real and the fiscal consequences of unfiled claims are material.

For litigants suing public entities, this means claim presentation must be confirmed before filing suit. Unlike a statute of limitations, which can sometimes be tolled or paused, the claim presentation requirement cannot be cured retroactively by filing a late claim after suit is filed.

---

## Application in Practice

The Government Claims Act intersects with virtually every California civil claim against a government entity:
- Personal injury suits against cities, counties, school districts, transit agencies;
- Medical malpractice claims against county hospitals and public health systems (intersecting with § 340.5 MICRA SOL);
- Property damage claims against public utilities or infrastructure;
- Employment discrimination and wrongful termination against public employers;
- § 1983 federal civil rights suits (federal claims are not subject to the Act, but state law claims joined in the same action are).

The most common fatal error in government entity litigation: filing suit within the state SOL period but after the six-month claim deadline, without a late claim order.

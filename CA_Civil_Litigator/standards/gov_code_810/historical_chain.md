# Gov. Code §810 — Historical Chain (Government Claims Act)

---

### I. The Wound

The government hurt people and could not be sued.

That is the entire wound, stated plainly. A government vehicle runs over a pedestrian — no remedy. A public hospital amputates the wrong limb — no remedy. A state employee, acting within the scope of employment, commits a tort that would make any private person liable — no remedy. Because the government is sovereign, and the sovereign is immune from suit.

This was not a quirk. It was the law. Inherited from England, embedded in California jurisprudence, and applied for over a century. The practical result: the entity with the most power and the most capacity to cause harm had the least accountability.

### II. The Break — Muskopf v. Corning Hospital District (1961)

The California Supreme Court abolished sovereign immunity in Muskopf v. Corning Hospital District, 55 Cal.2d 211 (1961). The opinion did not hedge: sovereign immunity is "an anachronism, without rational basis, and has existed only by the force of inertia."

But abolishing immunity created a new problem. Without a framework, what were the rules? Could a citizen sue the government for any act? Were there any limits? What procedures applied? The Legislature imposed a moratorium on Muskopf's effect and directed the California Law Revision Commission to design a comprehensive statutory framework.

### III. The Promise (1963)

The Government Claims Act (Stats. 1963, Ch. 1681) replaced sovereign immunity with statutory accountability. The framework has five pillars:

1. **Statutory liability only** (§815): A public entity is not liable for injury unless a statute says it is. This is not a restoration of immunity — it is a requirement that the rules be written down. The Legislature decides when the government pays, not the courts on a case-by-case basis.

2. **Vicarious liability** (§815.2): The primary pathway. A public entity is liable for an employee's torts within the scope of employment. If the employee would be personally liable, the entity pays. This is how most government tort cases work.

3. **Specific immunities** (§§818-825.6): The Legislature carved out specific protections for specific activities. Discretionary immunity for policy decisions. Emergency response immunity. Legislative immunity. Judicial immunity. These are exceptions to the general rule of vicarious liability.

4. **Claim presentation** (§§900-915.4): Before you can file a lawsuit against a public entity, you must file a government tort claim. Six months from the date of accrual for most claims. The entity has 45 days to respond. If denied (or no response), you then have six months to file suit. Miss the claim deadline, and your lawsuit is barred — unless you obtain late-claim relief.

5. **Defense and indemnification** (§§995-996.6): The entity defends and indemnifies its employees for acts within the scope of employment. The employee does not bear the cost personally.

### IV. The Federal Exception

This is critical and often misunderstood.

The Government Claims Act's claim presentation requirement does NOT apply to federal civil rights claims under 42 USC §1983. The U.S. Supreme Court decided this in Felder v. Casey, 487 U.S. 131 (1988): state procedural requirements like tort claim filing cannot be imposed as preconditions to federal civil rights actions. The Supremacy Clause preempts them.

What this means in practice: if a government employee violates your federal constitutional rights under color of state law, you sue under §1983 directly. No tort claim required. But if you also have state-law tort claims against the same government entity — negligence, false imprisonment, assault — those state claims DO require the tort claim filing. Same incident, different rules depending on whether the claim is federal or state.

### V. The Claim Presentation Trap

The six-month claim filing deadline is the single most dangerous procedural requirement in California government litigation. A person who has been harmed by government conduct — often someone in crisis, often someone without legal representation — must identify the correct public entity, prepare a written claim describing the facts and the amount of damages, and file it within six months of the date the cause of action accrued.

Late-claim relief exists (§§911.4-912.2) but requires a showing of good cause and must be sought within a year. After one year, the Legislature has decided that the claim is barred.

For the steward's work, the claim presentation requirement is relevant to any state-law tort claims against government entities. The §1983 federal claims bypass it entirely.

### VI. Why This Standard Is Load-Bearing

The Government Claims Act is the gateway to all tort litigation against California public entities. Every negligence claim, every false imprisonment claim, every state-law damages claim against a city, county, state agency, or other public entity must pass through this framework. If you do not file a government tort claim on time, you do not get to court. If you do not identify a statute that creates entity liability, you do not have a claim.

Understanding this framework — and understanding when it does NOT apply (§1983) — is fundamental to any litigation involving government conduct in California.

# CCP 1085 -- Origin Context

## Enactment

Original California Code of Civil Procedure (1872). Derived from the common-law writ of mandamus, which traces back centuries in English law.

## The Wound

A public officer has a legal duty to act. The law says: you shall issue the permit, you shall process the application, you shall release the records, you shall certify the election results. The officer refuses. Or ignores the request. Or delays indefinitely.

Without mandamus, the citizen has no remedy. You cannot force a public officer to do what the law requires. You can complain. You can write letters. You can vote them out at the next election. But you cannot make them do their job today. The law imposes the duty; nobody enforces it.

This is not a theoretical problem. It is the most common form of government dysfunction: the officer who simply will not do what the law requires. Not because of discretion. Not because of competing priorities. Because they refuse, or because the bureaucracy has ground to a halt, or because they are deliberately obstructing a right the citizen is entitled to exercise.

## The Promise

If the law requires a public officer, board, tribunal, or corporation to perform an act, and they refuse, a court will order them to perform it. That is mandamus. The court issues a writ -- an order -- commanding the respondent to do the specific thing the law requires.

Two requirements:
1. There must be a clear, present, ministerial duty -- a duty imposed by law with no room for discretion
2. The petitioner must have a clear, present right to the performance of that duty

If the duty involves discretion (the officer must decide how to act, not just whether to act), traditional mandamus under 1085 does not apply. That is the domain of administrative mandamus under 1094.5.

## Why This Statute Exists

Mandamus is the citizen's tool for forcing government to follow the law. It is not about policy disagreements or discretionary decisions. It is about duties the law imposes and officers who do not perform them.

This is one of the most fundamental remedies in California civil procedure. It has been in the Code since 1872 because the problem is as old as government itself: officers who will not do what the law commands.

## Source

Original Code of Civil Procedure, 1872. Derived from common-law writ of mandamus. Subdivision (b) added by Stats. 1998, Ch. 931.

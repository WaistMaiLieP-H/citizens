# CCP 1085 -- Historical Chain (Traditional Mandamus)

---

### I. The Wound

A public officer has a legal duty to act. The law says: issue the permit, process the application, release the records, certify the results. The officer refuses. Or ignores you. Or delays indefinitely.

Without mandamus, the citizen has no tool to compel action. You can complain to a supervisor. You can write to an elected official. You can wait for the next election. None of these make the officer do the thing the law requires today.

This is not a rare problem. It is the most common form of government dysfunction: the officer who will not do what the law commands. Sometimes it is obstruction. Sometimes it is negligence. Sometimes it is bureaucratic paralysis. The reason does not matter. What matters is that the law imposes a duty and the officer does not perform it.

### II. The Promise (1872)

CCP 1085 codified the common-law writ of mandamus as part of California's original Code of Civil Procedure. The promise: if the law requires a public officer, board, tribunal, or corporation to perform an act, and they refuse, a court will order them to perform it.

The court issues a writ -- a direct order -- commanding the respondent to do the specific thing the law requires. Not a suggestion. Not a recommendation. An order, backed by the contempt power of the court.

### III. The Two Requirements

Traditional mandamus under 1085 requires:
1. **A clear, present, ministerial duty** -- the law imposes a duty that leaves no room for discretion. The officer must act; the only question is whether they have.
2. **A clear, present right** -- the petitioner has a right to the performance of that duty, and that right is being denied.

If the duty involves discretion -- the officer must decide how to act, not just whether to act -- traditional mandamus does not apply. That is the domain of administrative mandamus under CCP 1094.5.

### IV. The Distinction from 1094.5

This is the most important distinction in California writ practice:

- **1085 (traditional mandamus):** Compels performance of a ministerial duty. The officer has no discretion. The law says "you shall," and the officer has not. The court orders: do it.
- **1094.5 (administrative mandamus):** Reviews a discretionary decision made after a hearing. The officer exercised judgment, and the question is whether that judgment was supported by the evidence and consistent with law.

The dividing line is discretion. No discretion = 1085. Discretion exercised after a hearing = 1094.5.

### V. What Mandamus Reaches

Section 1085 applies to "any inferior tribunal, corporation, board, or person." This includes:
- City councils, county boards, school boards
- State agencies and officers
- Public utilities and corporations performing public functions
- Any person acting in a public capacity with a ministerial duty

### VI. Why This Statute Is Load-Bearing

Mandamus is the enforcement mechanism for every ministerial duty in California law. Every statute that says "shall" creates a duty. Section 1085 is what gives that word teeth. Without mandamus, "shall" is a suggestion. With mandamus, "shall" is an order that a court will enforce.

For the CA Civil Litigator, 1085 is a workhorse statute. It is how you force a government agency to process a CPRA request, issue a permit, comply with a court order, or perform any other non-discretionary duty. It is pleaded alongside 526a when the government both refuses to act and spends money illegally. It is the foundational writ in California public law practice.

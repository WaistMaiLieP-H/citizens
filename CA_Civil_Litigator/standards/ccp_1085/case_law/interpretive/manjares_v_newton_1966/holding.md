# Manjares v. Newton, 64 Cal.2d 365 (1966)

## The Case

Manjares sought a writ of mandate (mandamus) under CCP §1085 to compel a public official to perform a specific act. The core question was when mandamus is the right tool and when it is not — specifically, the line between a "ministerial duty" (which mandamus can compel) and a "discretionary act" (which it generally cannot).

## What the Court Held

The California Supreme Court:

1. **Mandamus under §1085 lies to compel performance of a ministerial duty — one that is "plain, peremptory, and imperative," leaving the officer no room for discretion about whether to act.** When the law says "the officer shall do X," and the conditions triggering that duty are met, mandamus forces compliance.

2. **Mandamus does not lie to control the exercise of discretion.** If the law gives the officer a choice — about whether to act, when to act, or how to act — §1085 mandamus is not the right remedy. The petitioner must instead use §1094.5 (administrative mandamus), which allows judicial review of discretionary decisions under the "abuse of discretion" standard.

3. **The distinction is between "must do" and "may do."** If the statute says "shall" and the conditions are met, it is ministerial. If the statute says "may" or involves judgment calls, it is discretionary.

## The Rule

**Section 1085 mandamus compels performance of ministerial duties — duties that are plain, peremptory, and imperative, with no room for discretion. For discretionary acts, use §1094.5 administrative mandamus instead.**

## Why This Case Matters for §1085

Manjares draws the fundamental line that every mandamus petition must respect. Filing a §1085 petition to challenge a discretionary decision is a procedural error that will get the petition denied. Manjares tells practitioners: check the statute, determine if the duty is ministerial, and choose the right procedural vehicle.

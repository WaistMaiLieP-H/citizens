# Serrano v. Priest, 20 Cal.3d 25 (1977)

## The Case

John Serrano sued the State Treasurer and other officials, arguing that California's public school financing system — which relied on local property taxes — violated equal protection because children in poor districts got worse schools than children in wealthy districts. After winning on the merits in an earlier round (Serrano I, 1971), the question in this phase was who pays for the lawyers who made it happen.

## What the Court Held

The California Supreme Court:

1. **When a private lawsuit enforces an important public right and benefits a large class of people, the court can award attorney fees to the winning side — even though the "American Rule" normally says each side pays its own lawyers.** The court called this the "private attorney general" doctrine: the plaintiff acted as a private attorney general enforcing public rights, and the public should bear the cost.

2. **The proper way to calculate those fees is the "lodestar" method: multiply the reasonable hours worked by a reasonable hourly rate.** The court can then adjust that number up or down with a "multiplier" based on factors like how risky the case was, how complex, how contingent the fee arrangement was, and how important the result.

3. **This fee-shifting power is inherent in the court's equity jurisdiction.** The Legislature later codified this exact doctrine in CCP §1021.5, which was enacted the same year this opinion was published. Serrano IS the reason §1021.5 exists.

## The Rule

**Courts can award attorney fees to private litigants who successfully enforce important public rights benefiting a broad class, calculated by the lodestar/multiplier method. The Legislature codified this holding in CCP §1021.5.**

## Why This Case Matters for §1021.5

This is the foundation case. Every §1021.5 motion cites Serrano. The lodestar/multiplier framework from this case is still the method California courts use to calculate private attorney general fees. When a court grants §1021.5 fees, it is applying the Serrano doctrine in statutory form.

## Citation

Serrano v. Priest, 20 Cal.3d 25 (1977). California Supreme Court.

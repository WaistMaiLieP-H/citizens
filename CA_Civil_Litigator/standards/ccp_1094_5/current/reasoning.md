# Reasoning — Cal. Code of Civil Procedure § 1094.5 (Administrative Mandamus)

**Standard ID:** CCP_1094_5_ADMIN_MANDAMUS
**Layer:** 2 — Reasoning
**Status:** ACTIVE
**Filed:** 2026-04-20

---

## Why This Standard Exists

Section 1094.5 codifies the standard for judicial review of quasi-judicial administrative decisions in California. It exists because administrative agencies occupy an unusual constitutional position: they exercise quasi-judicial power (holding hearings, taking evidence, making findings, imposing penalties) but are not courts. Without a clear statutory standard governing how courts review those decisions, two competing problems arise:

1. **Under-review:** Courts rubber-stamp agency decisions because they are reluctant to intrude into executive branch fact-finding. Individuals who received defective hearings have no remedy.
2. **Over-review:** Courts substitute their own judgment for agencies on every contested factual question, destroying the practical value of agency expertise and creating de facto trials on appeal of every administrative proceeding.

Section 1094.5 resolves both problems with a bifurcated review standard calibrated to the stakes at issue.

---

## The Bifurcated Review Standard — Why It Exists

**Substantial evidence standard (default):** When the challenged decision does not affect a fundamental vested right, the court asks only whether the agency's findings are supported by substantial evidence in the light of the whole record. If any reasonable person could find as the agency found, the decision stands. The court does not reweigh the evidence or substitute its view of the facts.

This deference is not judicial abdication — it reflects a deliberate allocation of institutional competence. Administrative agencies with domain expertise (licensing boards, environmental agencies, professional regulatory bodies) are better positioned to evaluate technical evidence in their fields than generalist courts. Requiring courts to independently reweigh every factual dispute in agency proceedings would eliminate the practical benefits of specialized adjudication.

**Independent judgment standard (exception):** When the decision deprives the petitioner of a fundamental vested right — a professional license, liberty, the right to conduct a lawful occupation — deference disappears. The court independently examines the evidence and determines whether the findings are supported by the weight (preponderance) of the evidence.

The rationale: a professional license is not merely a government benefit — it is a property right earned by years of education, testing, and practice. Vested rights require full judicial protection. The Legislature drew a line between routine agency decisions (where expertise deference is rational) and decisions that deprive individuals of rights already earned (where heightened protection is constitutionally required).

---

## The Three Grounds for Relief

**1. Proceeding without or in excess of jurisdiction.** The agency either lacked authority to act at all (subject matter jurisdiction) or acted beyond the scope of its legal authority (excess of jurisdiction). An agency that imposes a penalty not authorized by its enabling statute acts in excess of jurisdiction.

**2. No fair trial.** The Due Process clause requires that quasi-judicial proceedings afford the parties notice, an opportunity to be heard, and a neutral decision-maker. Violations include: denial of the right to cross-examine witnesses, ex parte communications with the decision-maker, lack of adequate notice of charges, and denial of the right to present evidence.

**3. Prejudicial abuse of discretion.** This is the broadest and most commonly litigated ground:
- The agency did not proceed in the manner required by law (procedural error);
- The decision is not supported by the findings (logical disconnect between findings and outcome);
- The findings are not supported by the evidence (insufficient evidence under the applicable standard).

The "prejudicial" requirement filters out harmless procedural errors. The petitioner must show that the procedural error affected the outcome — that absent the error, the result might have been different.

---

## Why § 1094.5 and Not § 1085

Administrative mandamus under § 1094.5 applies specifically where the agency was required by law to conduct a hearing, was required to take evidence, and had discretion in the determination of facts. These three conditions distinguish quasi-judicial proceedings from ministerial acts. When the three conditions are absent, traditional mandamus (§ 1085) is the correct vehicle.

The practical difference: in § 1094.5 review, the petitioner can challenge the sufficiency of the evidence in the administrative record. In § 1085 mandamus, there is no record to review — the question is whether a ministerial duty was performed.

---

## Historical Context

Before § 1094.5 was codified, courts reviewed administrative decisions under the inherent writ jurisdiction with no uniform standard. Some courts applied deferential review in all cases; others applied independent judgment broadly. The resulting inconsistency created unpredictable outcomes for individuals challenging agency action.

The 1945 codification of § 1094.5 (and its later amendments establishing the fundamental-vested-rights distinction) provided a principled framework. The leading case *Strumsky v. San Diego County Employees Retirement Assn.* (1974) 11 Cal.3d 28 clarified the distinction between fundamental vested rights (independent judgment) and other agency decisions (substantial evidence), which remains the governing framework.

---

## Application in Practice

Section 1094.5 governs review of:
- Professional license revocation or suspension by state boards;
- Denial of permit applications after a noticed hearing;
- Termination of government employment after a Skelly hearing;
- Disability benefit denial after administrative appeal;
- Land use decisions made by planning commissions after hearings;
- School discipline and expulsion decisions.

The standard is not available for every government decision — only those where a hearing was legally required and evidence was taken.

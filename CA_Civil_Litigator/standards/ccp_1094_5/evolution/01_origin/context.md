# CCP §1094.5 — Origin Context

## Enactment

Stats. 1945, Ch. 868. Effective 1945. Added to the Code of Civil Procedure as §1094.5.

## The wound

Before 1945, California had no standardized mechanism for judicial review of administrative agency decisions. Agencies — licensing boards, planning commissions, zoning boards, public utilities commissions — made decisions that affected people's livelihoods, their right to practice a profession, their right to use their property. These decisions were made after hearings, based on evidence, with the agency exercising discretion. But when the agency got it wrong — acted without jurisdiction, denied a fair hearing, or reached a decision unsupported by evidence — the person on the receiving end had no clear path to a court.

Traditional mandamus under CCP §1085 existed, but it was designed to compel ministerial duties — to force an official to do something the law required them to do. It was not designed to review discretionary decisions. A licensing board's decision to revoke a professional license is not a ministerial act. It is a discretionary judgment based on evidence. §1085 was the wrong tool.

The result: agency decisions affecting fundamental rights — the right to earn a living, the right to use property, the right to government benefits — went effectively unreviewed by courts. Agencies operated as final arbiters of their own authority.

## The promise

§1094.5 created administrative mandamus — a dedicated judicial review mechanism for agency decisions made after a hearing. The promise: if you had a hearing before an agency, the agency was required to take evidence, and the agency exercised discretion in deciding your case, then you can ask a court to review that decision. The court will ask three questions:

1. Did the agency have jurisdiction?
2. Was the hearing fair?
3. Did the agency commit a prejudicial abuse of discretion?

And "abuse of discretion" means three specific things: (1) the agency failed to follow the procedures required by law, (2) the decision is not supported by the findings, or (3) the findings are not supported by the evidence.

## The two standards of review

This is the heart of the statute and the most heavily litigated question under §1094.5. When the issue is whether the findings are supported by the evidence, the court applies one of two standards:

**Independent judgment on the evidence** — the court reweighs the evidence itself. Used when the agency decision involves a "fundamental vested right." The classic example: revocation of a professional license. You have a vested right to practice your profession. When the agency threatens to take that away, the court looks at the evidence independently.

**Substantial evidence** — the court asks only whether a reasonable person could have reached the same conclusion the agency reached, looking at the whole record. Used for everything else — land use decisions, benefit determinations, regulatory actions that do not involve fundamental vested rights.

The independent judgment standard gives more protection because the court substitutes its own judgment for the agency's. The substantial evidence standard is more deferential.

# CCP §1094.5 — Historical Chain (Administrative Mandamus)

---

### I. The Wound

Government agencies make decisions that change people's lives. A licensing board revokes a doctor's license. A planning commission denies a building permit. A public utilities commission raises rates. A benefits agency cuts off disability payments. These decisions are made after hearings, based on evidence, by officials exercising judgment. But before 1945, there was no standardized way to ask a court whether the agency got it right.

Traditional mandamus (CCP §1085) could force an official to perform a ministerial duty — something the law required them to do with no room for judgment. But it was not designed to review decisions where the agency had discretion. If a licensing board held a hearing, considered the evidence, and decided to revoke your license, §1085 was the wrong tool. You needed a mechanism that could look at the evidence, evaluate the fairness of the hearing, and determine whether the decision was supported.

The gap meant that agencies were, in practice, the final word on their own authority. The person affected had no reliable path to a court that could meaningfully review what happened.

### II. The Promise (1945)

CCP §1094.5 filled the gap. Administrative mandamus. The promise is specific: when an agency makes a final decision after a hearing where evidence was taken and discretion was exercised, a court can review that decision. The court asks three questions:

1. **Jurisdiction:** Did the agency have the legal authority to make this decision?
2. **Fair trial:** Was the hearing fair?
3. **Abuse of discretion:** Did the agency abuse its discretion?

And abuse of discretion has three precise meanings:
- The agency did not follow the procedures the law requires
- The decision is not supported by the findings
- The findings are not supported by the evidence

### III. The Two Standards

This is the critical distinction that determines how much protection you get.

**Independent judgment on the evidence:** The court looks at the evidence itself and decides whether the findings are supported by the weight of the evidence. The court substitutes its own judgment. This applies when the decision involves a "fundamental vested right" — a right you already have that the agency is threatening to take away. The leading examples: a professional license (Bixby v. Pierno, 1971), retirement benefits (Strumsky v. San Diego County Employees Retirement Assn., 1974).

**Substantial evidence:** The court asks only whether a reasonable person, looking at the whole record, could have reached the same conclusion the agency reached. This is deferential. The court does not reweigh the evidence. This applies to everything that does not involve a fundamental vested right — land use decisions, regulatory determinations, benefit eligibility decisions where the applicant has no pre-existing vested right.

The difference matters enormously. Under independent judgment, the court can disagree with the agency and reverse. Under substantial evidence, the court upholds the decision if any reasonable person could have agreed with it, even if the court itself would have decided differently.

### IV. Subsequent Amendments

The 1945 core (subdivisions a-c) remains the heart of the statute. Later amendments added specialized provisions:

- **Subdivision (d):** Hospital board decisions — substantial evidence standard, except discrimination claims get independent judgment
- **Subdivision (h):** Stricter stay standard for APA hearings — petitioner must show agency is unlikely to prevail
- **Subdivision (j):** State Bargaining Unit 5 limitation (1996)

These amendments are important in their specific contexts but do not change the fundamental structure.

### V. The Relationship to §1085

§1085 and §1094.5 are complementary, not competing. §1085 compels ministerial duties — where the law requires a specific act and the official has no discretion. §1094.5 reviews discretionary decisions made after a hearing. If the question is "did the agency follow the rules in making its decision," the answer is §1094.5. If the question is "is the agency refusing to do something the law says it must do," the answer is §1085.

### VI. Why This Standard Is Load-Bearing

Every person who has ever had a professional license revoked, a building permit denied, a government benefit terminated, or any other adverse decision from an agency after a hearing — §1094.5 is their path to judicial review. Without it, the agency's word is final. With it, the agency must justify its decision to a court. The standard of review determines how much protection the court provides, but the existence of review itself is the fundamental guarantee.

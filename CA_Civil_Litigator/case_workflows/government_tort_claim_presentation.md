# Government Tort Claim Presentation

Operational workflow for filing a tort claim against a California public entity as a prerequisite to filing a lawsuit.

---

## Step 1: Identify the Public Entity

Determine which government body you intend to sue. Under Government Code Section 810, a "public entity" includes:

- The State of California and its agencies, departments, and boards.
- Cities, counties, and special districts (school districts, water districts, transit agencies, etc.).
- Any other entity created by statute that exercises governmental functions.

Name the specific entity. "The police department" is usually not a separate entity — it is a department of the city. The claim goes to the city, not the department.

Corpus reference: `standards/gov_code_810/current/gov_code_810_leginfo.txt`

---

## Step 2: Determine the Filing Deadline

The deadline depends on the type of claim:

- **Personal injury or property damage** — the claim must be filed within **6 months** of the date the cause of action accrued (Government Code Section 911.2(a)).
- **All other causes of action** (breach of contract, inverse condemnation, etc.) — the claim must be filed within **1 year** of accrual (Government Code Section 911.2(b)).

Accrual means the date the harm occurred, or the date the plaintiff discovered (or reasonably should have discovered) the harm. The discovery rule applies to delayed-discovery claims.

Missing this deadline is usually fatal. See Step 8 for late claim relief.

---

## Step 3: Prepare the Claim Form

Government Code Section 910 lists the required contents. The claim must include:

1. **Name and mailing address** of the claimant.
2. **Mailing address** to which the entity should send notices about the claim.
3. **Date, place, and circumstances** of the occurrence that gave rise to the claim.
4. **Description of the injury, damage, or loss** — what happened to you.
5. **Name(s) of the public employee(s)** who caused the injury, if known.
6. **Amount claimed** — state a specific dollar amount, or if the amount exceeds $10,000, state that fact. The amount you state here is the ceiling on what you can recover in the lawsuit. Do not lowball it.

Most public entities have their own claim forms. Use the entity's form if one exists. If no form exists, a letter containing all Section 910 information satisfies the requirement.

---

## Step 4: File with the Correct Office

- **State claims** — file with the California Government Claims Board (formerly Board of Control), or with the specific state agency if required by that agency's procedures.
- **City claims** — file with the city clerk or the city attorney's office.
- **County claims** — file with the county clerk or the board of supervisors' clerk.
- **Special districts** — file with the district's secretary or clerk.

Get a stamped copy or other proof of filing. If mailing, use certified mail with return receipt.

---

## Step 5: Wait for the Entity's Response

The entity has **45 days** to act on the claim (Government Code Section 912.4). The entity can:

- **Accept the claim** — the entity agrees to pay. This is rare.
- **Reject the claim** — the entity sends a written notice of rejection. This triggers the lawsuit filing deadline.
- **Do nothing** — if the entity fails to act within 45 days, the claim is deemed rejected by operation of law.

If rejected by written notice, the notice must inform the claimant of the 6-month deadline to file suit and the right to apply for leave to present a late claim.

---

## Step 6: File Suit Within 6 Months of Rejection

Under Government Code Section 945.6:

- If the entity sends a written rejection notice: you have **6 months from the date of the notice** to file the lawsuit.
- If the entity never responds (deemed rejection): you have **2 years from the date the cause of action accrued** to file the lawsuit.

Missing the lawsuit filing deadline after rejection is fatal. Calendar it immediately upon receiving the rejection notice.

---

## Step 7: Critical Exception — Section 1983 Claims Do Not Require a Tort Claim

Federal civil rights claims under 42 U.S.C. Section 1983 are **exempt** from the California Government Tort Claims Act. The Supreme Court held in Felder v. Casey (1988) that state notice-of-claim requirements cannot be applied to federal civil rights actions because they would burden the exercise of federal rights.

This means:

- If your only claim is under Section 1983, you do not need to file a government tort claim at all.
- If you have both Section 1983 claims and state law claims (Bane Act, negligence, etc.), you must file a tort claim for the state law claims but not for the federal claims.
- If you miss the tort claim deadline for state claims, your Section 1983 claims survive.

Corpus reference: `standards/gov_code_810/case_law/interpretive/felder_v_casey_1988/holding.md`

---

## Step 8: Late Claim Relief

If you missed the 6-month or 1-year filing deadline, you may apply for leave to present a late claim under Government Code Section 911.4.

Requirements:

- The application must be filed **within 1 year** of the date the cause of action accrued. After 1 year, no late claim relief is available (Government Code Section 946.6).
- You must show **excusable neglect** — meaning the delay was caused by mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect (the same standard as CCP Section 473(b) relief from default).

If the entity denies the late claim application, you can petition the superior court for relief under Section 946.6 within 6 months of the denial.

---

## Key Pitfalls

1. **The amount stated in the claim caps your recovery.** If you claim $50,000 and later discover $500,000 in damages, you are limited to $50,000 unless you amend the claim. When in doubt, state the higher amount or write "exceeds $10,000" and describe all potential damages.

2. **The claim must go to the right entity.** Filing with the wrong entity does not satisfy the requirement, even if the wrong entity forwards it. Verify the correct entity before filing.

3. **The tort claim requirement applies to state law claims only.** Do not let anyone tell you that you need a tort claim to file a Section 1983 action. You do not.

# Administrative Mandamus Petition (CCP Section 1094.5)

Operational workflow for filing a petition for writ of administrative mandamus to challenge an agency decision.

---

## Step 1: Exhaust Administrative Remedies

Before you can petition the court, you must exhaust every available administrative remedy. This means:

- If the agency offers an internal appeal, you must take it.
- If the agency offers a hearing, you must request and attend it.
- If the agency has a multi-level review process, you must complete every level.

The court will deny the petition if you skipped a step. The exhaustion requirement exists because courts defer to agencies and will not intervene until the agency has had a full opportunity to correct its own errors.

There are narrow exceptions: exhaustion is not required if the remedy would be futile, if the agency lacks jurisdiction, or if pursuing the remedy would cause irreparable harm. These exceptions are interpreted strictly.

---

## Step 2: Determine the Standard of Review

This is the most important strategic decision in the case. There are two standards, and which one applies determines whether you win or lose:

### Independent Judgment (de novo review)

Applies when the agency decision affects a **fundamental vested right**. Under Bixby v. Pierno (1971), the court exercises its own independent judgment on the evidence and can reweigh all the facts.

A fundamental vested right is a right that is already possessed and that is important to the individual. Examples:

- A professional license (medical, legal, contractor) being revoked or suspended.
- A vested property right being taken.
- Public employment termination (for tenured or permanent employees).

Under independent judgment, the court looks at all the evidence and decides for itself whether the agency's factual findings are correct. This is the more favorable standard for petitioners.

Corpus reference: `standards/ccp_1094_5/case_law/interpretive/bixby_v_pierno_1971/holding.md`

### Substantial Evidence

Applies when the agency decision does **not** affect a fundamental vested right. Under Fukuda v. City of Angels (1999), the court only asks whether a reasonable person could have reached the same conclusion the agency reached, based on the evidence in the record.

Examples:

- Denial of a permit or license application (not yet vested).
- Zoning decisions.
- Regulatory enforcement actions that do not affect vested rights.

Under substantial evidence review, the court defers heavily to the agency. Even if the court disagrees with the agency's conclusion, it must uphold the decision if any substantial evidence supports it.

Corpus reference: `standards/ccp_1094_5/case_law/interpretive/fukuda_v_city_of_angels_1999/holding.md`

---

## Step 3: Prepare the Administrative Record

The administrative record is the foundation of the petition. It includes everything the agency considered when making its decision: hearing transcripts, exhibits, staff reports, correspondence, and the agency's written findings.

Two options:

1. **Petition the respondent to prepare the record.** Under CCP Section 1094.5(a), the petitioner can request that the respondent agency prepare and certify the administrative record. The respondent must do so within a reasonable time.
2. **Prepare the record yourself.** If the respondent delays or disputes the contents, the petitioner can compile the record from their own files and ask the court to settle any disputes about what belongs in it.

The record is critical because, under substantial evidence review, the court looks only at what was before the agency. Evidence not in the record generally cannot be considered.

---

## Step 4: File the Petition

The petition must allege one or more of the following grounds for relief:

1. **The agency acted without or in excess of jurisdiction** — the agency did not have the legal authority to make the decision it made.
2. **There was not a fair trial** — the proceedings were tainted by bias, denial of due process, or procedural irregularity that prejudiced the outcome.
3. **There was a prejudicial abuse of discretion** — the agency either failed to follow the law, made findings not supported by the evidence, or reached a decision not supported by its findings.

Most petitions focus on the third ground: prejudicial abuse of discretion.

Corpus reference: `standards/ccp_1094_5/current/ccp_1094_5_leginfo.txt`

---

## Step 5: Break Down "Abuse of Discretion" Using the Topanga Framework

The California Supreme Court in Topanga Association for a Scenic Community v. County of Los Angeles (1974) established a three-part test for abuse of discretion:

1. **Did the agency proceed in the manner required by law?** If the agency skipped a required hearing, failed to give notice, or ignored a mandatory procedural step, the decision is invalid regardless of whether it reached the right result.

2. **Is the decision supported by findings?** The agency must make written findings that explain why it decided what it decided. If the agency issued a bare order without explaining its reasoning, that alone is grounds for reversal.

3. **Are the findings supported by the evidence?** Even if the agency made findings, those findings must be grounded in the actual evidence in the record. Findings that contradict the evidence or are based on speculation fail this prong.

A failure at any one of these three levels constitutes a prejudicial abuse of discretion.

Corpus reference: `standards/ccp_1094_5/case_law/interpretive/topanga_v_los_angeles_1974/holding.md`

---

## Step 6: Request a Stay of the Agency Decision

Under CCP Section 1094.5(g), the court may stay the agency's decision pending judicial review. This means the decision does not take effect while the case is pending.

A stay is not automatic. You must request it, and the court will consider:

- Whether the petitioner will suffer irreparable harm without a stay.
- Whether granting the stay will harm the public interest.
- Whether the petitioner is likely to prevail on the merits.

If the agency decision involves a license revocation, permit denial, or employment termination, a stay can preserve the petitioner's rights during what may be months or years of litigation.

---

## Step 7: Independent Judgment — Court Reweighs Everything

If independent judgment applies, the court starts from scratch. It reads the entire administrative record, evaluates the credibility of witnesses (from transcripts), and reaches its own conclusion about what the evidence shows.

The agency's findings receive some deference because the agency heard the witnesses live, but the court is not bound by those findings. The court can — and regularly does — reach the opposite conclusion.

Corpus reference: `standards/ccp_1094_5/case_law/interpretive/strumsky_v_san_diego_1974/holding.md`

Strumsky v. San Diego County Employees Retirement Association (1974) confirmed that independent judgment review applies to administrative decisions affecting fundamental vested rights, and clarified how courts should weigh evidence independently while still giving some deference to the agency's factual findings.

---

## Step 8: Substantial Evidence — Defer If Any Reasonable Person Could Agree

If substantial evidence review applies, the court does not substitute its judgment. It asks only: "Could a reasonable person, looking at this record, have reached the same conclusion the agency reached?"

If the answer is yes, the court must uphold the decision, even if the court would have decided differently.

"Substantial evidence" means more than a scintilla, but less than a preponderance. It is evidence that a reasonable mind would accept as adequate to support the conclusion.

---

## Key Pitfalls

1. **Do not skip exhaustion.** The court will dismiss the petition without reaching the merits if you did not exhaust administrative remedies.

2. **Identify the correct standard early.** The entire litigation strategy depends on whether you get independent judgment or substantial evidence review. Analyze this before drafting the petition.

3. **The record is the battlefield.** Under substantial evidence review, you can only win with what is already in the record. If critical evidence was not presented to the agency, it generally cannot be raised for the first time in court.

4. **Topanga findings are mandatory.** If the agency did not make adequate findings, that is an independent basis for reversal. Check the agency's written decision for the three Topanga elements before drafting anything else.

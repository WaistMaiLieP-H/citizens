# Reasoning — California Evidence Code § 752
## Interpreter for Witnesses — Why This Standard Exists

## The Core Problem

Due process requires that a party in a legal proceeding be able to understand what is
happening and to make themselves understood. For someone who cannot speak or understand
English, the interpreter is not an accommodation — the interpreter is the proceeding.
Without a sworn, qualified, mutually intelligible interpreter, there is no proceeding;
there is only a ritual conducted in a language the party cannot comprehend, producing
outcomes they cannot contest because they never understood what was being decided.

## Why the Oath Matters

The interpreter's oath distinguishes the interpreter from a bystander who happens to
speak two languages. The oath:
1. Places the interpreter under the same perjury exposure as any other officer of the court
2. Creates an evidentiary record — the interpreter was sworn at a specific time and place
3. Establishes the interpreter's neutrality — they are an officer of the court, not an
   advocate for either party or for any external authority

An unsworn "interpreter" has none of these protections. Their translation cannot be
tested for accuracy, cannot be impeached through the interpreter themselves, and creates
no record of what the witness actually said in their own language. The transcript shows
the English version; there is no mechanism to establish whether that English version
accurately reflects what the Thai (or any other language) witness said.

## The Immigration Interpreter Problem

GOV §7284.6(a)(3) exists because the Legislature recognized a specific abuse: using
federal immigration officers to interpret for witnesses or parties in California law
enforcement matters. The abuse runs both ways:

1. **Information gathering:** The immigration officer, while serving as interpreter,
   hears everything the person says and can use it in removal proceedings
2. **Coercion:** The person being interpreted for knows the interpreter has enforcement
   authority over them — their willingness to speak freely is compromised by the knowledge
   that the interpreter can have them deported
3. **Accuracy incentive:** An interpreter with an adversarial interest in the outcome has
   an incentive to shade the translation

EVID §752's mutual intelligibility requirement (the interpreter must be one the witness
"can understand") implicitly requires that the witness be able to actually communicate —
not just formally understand the language, but feel free to communicate. A witness
interpreting through an immigration officer who can deport them is not "able to understand"
in any meaningful sense.

## Why This Matters in the Butsaya Proceedings

The Butsaya dissolution involves a Thai national in California family court proceedings.
The family law audit has established document manipulation, a fabricated DVRO, and
coordinated filings. A recurring question in any proceeding of this kind: did the
Thai-speaking party actually understand what was being decided?

If Butsaya appeared in the DVRO hearing without a sworn §752-compliant interpreter:
- She could not meaningfully consent to or contest the DVRO
- The DVRO's evidentiary basis was never properly established through admissible witness testimony
- Any findings based on her "testimony" (if uninterpreted or unsworn-interpreted) are built
  on inadmissible evidence

This is not a technical objection — it goes to whether due process was satisfied at all.
A proceeding in which a party could not understand what was happening and was not provided
the means to understand is a proceeding that violated the Due Process Clause of the
Fourteenth Amendment in addition to EVID §752.

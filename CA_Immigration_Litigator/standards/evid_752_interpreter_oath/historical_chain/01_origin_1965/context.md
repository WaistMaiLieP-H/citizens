# Historical Origin — Cal. Evid. Code § 752 — 1965

## The wound

Before California's Evidence Code was enacted in 1965, the rules governing interpreters
were scattered, inconsistent, and largely judge-made. Courts had discretion over whether
to appoint interpreters, whether they needed to be sworn, and how their compensation
would be handled. In practice, witnesses and parties who could not speak English were
frequently left to communicate through family members, bystanders, or whoever happened
to be available — none of them sworn, none of them qualified, none of them neutral.

The wound: a witness who testifies through an unsworn, unqualified interpreter has no
meaningful protection against mistranslation, whether accidental or intentional. The
transcript reflects only the English version. The original language version is lost. If
the translation was inaccurate, there is no record against which to test it. If the
interpreter had an interest in the outcome, the transcript gives no sign of it.

## The 1965 codification

California's Evidence Code (Stats. 1965, ch. 299) systematized the rules of evidence
including interpreter requirements. §752 established:
1. A mandatory appointment requirement — "shall be sworn"
2. A bilateral competence test — the interpreter must understand the witness AND the
   witness must understand the interpreter
3. A cost allocation scheme distinguishing criminal (court bears cost) from civil
   (cost apportioned)

The "shall be sworn" language is not discretionary. It is a mandatory requirement that
applies in every proceeding where the witness cannot understand or express themselves
in English. There is no exception for emergencies, for convenience, or for the fact that
a family member happens to speak both languages.

## Subsequent development — immigration interpreter intersection

The intersection of EVID §752 and immigration enforcement interpreters was not addressed
by the 1965 Evidence Code. It became a live issue as California's immigrant population
grew and federal immigration enforcement intensified. The specific abuse — using an
immigration officer as the interpreter in law enforcement contacts — was eventually
addressed by the TRUST Act (GOV §7284.6(a)(3), 2018), which categorically prohibits it.

The constitutional dimension — that a party who cannot understand proceedings is denied
due process — was established through a line of cases culminating in Cunningham v.
Superior Court (1986) and the Judicial Council's development of the certified court
interpreter program, which provides quality assurance for the oath the Evidence Code
requires.

# Historical Origin — Cal. Gov. Code § 7284.6 — 2017

## The wound

Before 2017, California law enforcement agencies were not prohibited from honoring
federal civil immigration detainers, sharing personal information with ICE, making
arrests based on civil immigration warrants, or placing officers under federal
immigration supervision. Many California jurisdictions had their own policies — some
cooperative, some not — but no statewide prohibition existed.

The documented pattern: immigrants in California stopped reporting crimes, stopped
cooperating as witnesses, and stopped calling 911 because any contact with law
enforcement could result in transfer to federal immigration custody. A 2016 study found
that nearly half of Latinos in California were less likely to report crimes to police
because of fear of immigration consequences. The wound was not just to individuals —
it was to the public safety infrastructure of California communities.

The wound §7284.6 addresses: California law enforcement resources were being used to
enforce a federal civil regulatory scheme against California residents, at California
taxpayer expense, producing a chilling effect that made California communities less safe
and less willing to cooperate with legitimate law enforcement.

## SB 54 (2017) — The California Values Act

California Senate Bill 54, signed by Governor Brown on October 5, 2017, enacted the
California Values Act (GOV §§7282-7284.10). The Legislature's findings (GOV §7282) made
the state's position explicit:

- California's public safety resources are not to be used for immigration enforcement
- The relationship between California's immigrant communities and law enforcement is
  essential to public safety
- State and local agencies are not an arm of the federal civil immigration enforcement system
- California will not be complicit in the civil detention and deportation of California
  residents who pose no threat to public safety

§7284.6 is the operative prohibition: the seven specific categories of prohibited
cooperation, the categorical bars on supervision and transfer, and the T/U visa
affirmative exception that preserved the victim-cooperation pathway.

## Constitutional framework

The Tenth Amendment anti-commandeering doctrine — recognized by the Supreme Court in
Printz v. United States (1997) and extended in Murphy v. NCAA (2018) — prohibits
Congress from compelling states to enforce federal regulatory schemes. The Ninth Circuit
confirmed in United States v. California (2019) that California's refusal to use state
resources for federal civil immigration enforcement is constitutional — it is not
obstruction of federal law; it is the exercise of state sovereignty not to participate
in a federal regulatory program.

## The T/U visa exception design

Congress created T visas (trafficking victims) and U visas (crime victims) specifically
to induce cooperation: an immigrant victim who cooperates with law enforcement can obtain
immigration protection. The exception in §7284.6(b)(4) preserves this pathway —
California law enforcement may cooperate with federal immigration authorities when the
purpose is to help victims obtain protection, not to enforce civil immigration law.
This was not an accident in the drafting; it was the Legislature's affirmative statement
that the TRUST Act is victim-protective, not victim-obstructive.

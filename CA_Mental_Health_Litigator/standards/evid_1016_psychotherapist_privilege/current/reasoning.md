# Evid. Code § 1016 — Privilege Exception — Reasoning

**Standard ID:** `evid_1016_psychotherapist_privilege`
**Layer:** 2 — Reasoning
**Date:** 2026-04-12

---

## Why the Privilege Exists — And Why the Exception Is Necessary

The psychotherapist-patient privilege (§§1010-1015) exists for a specific purpose: to protect the confidential communications in psychotherapy from disclosure, so that patients can seek help without fear that their most private disclosures will be used against them. Without the privilege, patients would withhold information from therapists, undermining the therapeutic relationship and discouraging treatment.

§1016's exception exists for an equally specific reason: once a patient puts their own mental condition at issue in litigation, they cannot simultaneously claim the privilege to shield the very communications that bear on that issue. Allowing a plaintiff to sue for emotional distress damages while blocking the defense's access to therapy records about that distress would be fundamentally unfair.

The balance: protect genuine therapeutic privacy; prevent the privilege from becoming a sword that a party uses to put their condition at issue while blocking access to the evidence.

## The "Tendering" Standard — Narrow Construction

California courts construe §1016's "tendering" requirement narrowly. Not every lawsuit that involves mental health issues tenders the patient's condition:
- Claiming garden-variety emotional distress as part of a physical injury claim does NOT tender the patient's mental condition in the §1016 sense
- Claiming psychiatric injury, diagnoses, treatment, or mental disability as damages DOES tender the condition
- A mental disability discrimination claim (FEHA) where the plaintiff's disability is the protected characteristic at issue DOES tender the condition

The narrow construction protects patients who are involved in litigation that merely touches on mental health tangentially, versus those who affirmatively claim their mental condition as a liability theory.

## §1016 vs. §1023 — The Criminal Court Distinction

§1023 (not fetched separately but documented here from training knowledge) provides a specific exception for criminal competency proceedings: no privilege exists for communications relevant to the patient's mental competency in a criminal proceeding where the patient is a defendant. This is more direct than §1016 because the competency evaluation itself is ordered for that purpose.

For the Dr. Wiita case:
- The evaluation itself is governed by §1023 (criminal competency)
- Any underlying voluntary therapy records that Dr. Wiita may have reviewed are governed by §1016 (did the defendant tender their mental condition?)
- The defendant's right to raise the competency of the evaluation (via the §1368/1369 audit) is not itself a tendering of their mental condition — it is challenging the procedural validity of the evaluation

## The In Camera Review Requirement

Before ordering disclosure of psychotherapy records over a privilege claim, courts must conduct an in camera review (Evid. Code §1040; Jaffee v. Redmond analogy). The court reviews the records privately to determine whether they are genuinely relevant to the tendered issue and whether disclosure is proportionate. This in camera review is a protection against fishing expeditions even within the §1016 exception.

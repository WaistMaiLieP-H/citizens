# PEN § 1368 — Historical Chain: Origin

**Stage:** 01 — Origin
**Year:** 1872 (original Penal Code); competency standards substantially formalized mid-20th century; constitutionalized by Pate v. Robinson (1966) and Drope v. Missouri (1975)

---

## The Wound

The wound that §1368 answers is the trial of the mentally incompetent — the conviction of a person who could not understand what was happening to them or participate meaningfully in their own defense.

Before competency standards were enforced, courts proceeded with trials against defendants who were visibly confused, unable to communicate with counsel, or floridly psychotic. The verdicts were legally valid in form but constitutionally hollow in substance: you cannot have a fair trial if one party cannot participate.

The visible catastrophes — convictions of people who clearly did not understand the proceedings, sentences imposed on defendants who had no rational grasp of what they had agreed to — accumulated until the United States Supreme Court in Pate v. Robinson (1966) declared that the trial of a legally incompetent defendant is a denial of due process.

---

## The Constitutional Anchor — Dusky and Its Progeny

The federal standard was set in Dusky v. United States (1960): competency requires that the defendant have "sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding" AND "a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him."

This two-prong test is not about intelligence. It is about functional capacity at the time of the proceedings. A defendant with low intelligence may be competent; a defendant in a psychotic episode may not be, regardless of baseline IQ.

California §1368 operationalizes Dusky by creating the mandatory judicial trigger: when the doubt arises — when the evidence of incompetency appears — the judge cannot wait. The duty to act is immediate.

---

## The Promise

Society promised that no person would be tried for a crime they could not understand and could not defend against. The promise was made because the adversarial system depends on both parties being able to participate in it. Strip one party of that ability through mental illness and the system produces verdicts that are legally meaningless.

Section 1368 is the mechanism that keeps that promise. The mandatory language — "shall" — reflects the Legislature's understanding that judicial discretion on this question had historically been exercised too rarely and too late.

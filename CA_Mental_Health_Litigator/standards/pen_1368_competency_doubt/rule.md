# PEN § 1368 — Competency Doubt: Rule Summary

**Standard:** CA_Mental_Health_Litigator / pen_1368_competency_doubt
**Build date:** 2026-04-13

---

## The Mandatory Duty When Doubt Arises

Section 1368 does not give the judge discretion once doubt arises. The statute is written in mandatory terms: the judge **shall** state the doubt in the record, **shall** inquire of defense counsel, and when proceedings are commenced, **shall** suspend them.

### Three-Step Mandatory Sequence

**Step 1 — State the doubt in the record (§1368(a)):**
The doubt must be expressed on the record — not in chambers, not informally, not by implication. A judge who harbors doubt but says nothing has failed the statutory duty. This step is reviewable on appeal because it either appears in the record or it doesn't.

**Step 2 — Inquire of defense counsel (§1368(a)):**
The judge must inquire of the attorney for the defendant whether, in the attorney's opinion, the defendant is mentally competent. This is not a request — it is a duty triggered automatically by the doubt. If the defendant is unrepresented, the court must appoint counsel before this inquiry can happen.

**Step 3 — Suspend proceedings (§1368(c)):**
When an inquiry has been commenced, ALL criminal proceedings SHALL be suspended until competency is determined. No plea. No trial. No sentencing. No hearing that adjudicates the merits. Any proceeding conducted while competency is unresolved is constitutionally infirm.

---

## When Doubt Must Arise — The Constitutional Floor

California §1368 tracks the federal constitutional standard set by Pate v. Robinson (1966) and Drope v. Missouri (1975): if the evidence before the trial court raises a bona fide doubt as to a defendant's competence, the failure to conduct a competency hearing violates due process.

The evidence that should trigger doubt includes:
- Irrational behavior by the defendant
- The defendant's demeanor at trial
- Prior medical opinions suggesting mental disorder or incompetency
- Defense counsel's representation that the defendant cannot understand proceedings or assist in their defense

A judge who observes these indicators but fails to trigger §1368 has denied the defendant a constitutionally required hearing.

---

## The §1368(c) Suspension Rule — The Key Defect in the Dr. Wiita Case

Section 1368(c) is absolute: **when an inquiry into present mental competence has been commenced, ALL proceedings in the criminal prosecution SHALL be suspended.** This means:

- Any conviction entered while competency was in doubt and proceedings were not suspended = constitutionally infirm
- Any plea entered while competency was in question and not resolved = potentially involuntary
- Any order or judgment entered during this period = subject to collateral attack on due process grounds

**Application to Dr. Wiita:** If the §1368 competency process was initiated but not properly completed — if Dr. Wiita's telehealth evaluation was procedurally defective, the report was absent or incomplete, and proceedings nonetheless continued — then every action taken in the criminal case after the §1368 inquiry was commenced and before proper competency was determined is constitutionally suspect.

---

## Defense Counsel's Role — §1368(b)

If counsel says the defendant may be mentally incompetent, the court SHALL order a competency determination under §1369. This is not advisory — it is mandatory. The only discretion is: if counsel says the defendant IS competent, the court MAY still order a determination on its own motion.

This means that if defense counsel raised the competency question and the court did not order a §1369 evaluation, the court violated §1368(b). If a §1369 evaluation was ordered but conducted defectively (Dr. Wiita telehealth, no MC-350), the evaluation does not satisfy the §1368(b)/(c) requirement — proceedings remained improperly suspended or improperly recommenced.

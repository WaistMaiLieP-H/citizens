# BPC § 2290.5 — Telehealth Standards — Reasoning

**Standard ID:** `bpc_2290_5_telehealth_standards`
**Layer:** 2 — Reasoning
**Date:** 2026-04-12

---

## Why §2290.5 Matters for Forensic Evaluation Contexts

§2290.5 was enacted in 2011 as telehealth medicine expanded. Its core design principle: technology does not change the legal framework. Whatever obligations a physician has in-person, they have via telehealth. Whatever licensure is required in-person is required via telehealth.

For forensic evaluations specifically, §2290.5(g)'s "full professional standards apply" provision is transformative. Before §2290.5, there was a risk that courts might treat telehealth forensic evaluations more permissively — accepting out-of-state evaluators, waiving consent requirements, reducing documentation standards. §2290.5(g) closed all those gaps. A telehealth forensic evaluation in California is held to exactly the same standard as an in-person one.

## The Interstate Licensure Problem

§2290.5(e)'s scope-of-practice preservation, combined with the California Medical Practice Act (BPC §2052), creates a bright-line rule for out-of-state providers: if you are examining a California patient (in person or via telehealth), you need a California license. There is no "telehealth exception" to California licensure requirements.

This is not a merely technical violation. An evaluation conducted by an unlicensed provider:
- Is the product of unauthorized medical practice
- Undermines the evidentiary reliability of the evaluation
- Constitutes unprofessional conduct reportable to the CA Medical Board
- May be challenged as a basis for excluding the evaluation from evidence in competency proceedings

If the evaluation that resulted in a competency finding was conducted by a South Carolina-licensed provider without CA authorization, the reliability of that finding is fundamentally compromised.

## The Consent Documentation Requirement as the Paper Trail

§2290.5(b)'s requirement that consent be "documented" creates an affirmative paper trail obligation. The absence of documentation is not neutral — it is evidence that the requirement was not met. In forensic evaluation contexts, this matters for:
- Authenticating the evaluation record
- Demonstrating that the defendant's rights were protected during the evaluation
- Creating a basis for a professional accountability complaint if the evaluation was conducted without documented consent

For a court-ordered evaluation where the defendant is in custody and has no practical ability to refuse: did the evaluator explain the telehealth modality? Did the defendant understand they were being evaluated remotely? Was that understanding documented? These are not hypothetical concerns — they go to the voluntariness of any statements made during the evaluation.

## The "Unprofessional Conduct" Finding as an Accountability Tool

§2290.5(d)'s "unprofessional conduct" designation is an enforcement lever. California's medical licensing system treats unprofessional conduct as grounds for disciplinary action. A provider who conducted a California court-ordered evaluation via telehealth without CA licensure and without documented consent committed unprofessional conduct on multiple grounds:

1. Practicing medicine without CA license (BPC §2052 violation)
2. Failure to obtain/document telehealth consent (§2290.5(b) violation)
3. Both independently constitute unprofessional conduct (§2290.5(d))

A complaint to the California Medical Board documenting these violations creates:
- An official investigation record
- Potential license discipline
- A public record that the evaluation was conducted by a provider who violated California professional standards
- Additional corroboration of the pretextual nature of the evaluation

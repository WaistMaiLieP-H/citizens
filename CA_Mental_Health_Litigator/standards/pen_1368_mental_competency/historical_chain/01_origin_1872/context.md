# Pen. Code § 1368 — Historical Chain: Origin

**Standard ID:** `pen_1368_mental_competency`
**Layer:** 3 — Historical Chain
**Date:** 2026-04-12

---

## Origin: Stats. 1872 — Original Penal Code

§1368 dates to the original 1872 Penal Code codification. The historical foundation is the common law principle that a defendant cannot be tried in absentia — and that mental incompetency is a form of absence (the mind is not present to the proceedings even if the body is).

Pre-Dusky, California courts applied a loose "presently insane" standard — whether the defendant understood what was happening. Dusky (1960) imposed the specific two-prong standard now reflected in §1369(b)(1)(B).

**Post-Dusky amendments:** The 1970s-1980s amendments modernized the statute to align with Dusky and Drope v. Missouri (1975), adding the mandatory suspension provision and clarifying the defense-counsel trigger.

**2016 Amendment (Stats. 2016, Ch. 86):** Updated language to include revocation proceedings (probation, mandatory supervision, PRCS, parole) — recognizing that competency is required not just for trial but for any proceeding where liberty is at stake.

## CHRONICLE Flag

No CHRONICLE restriction. The statute is protective — designed to prevent trying incompetent defendants. Historical record discusses mental health in criminal proceedings without discriminatory content (though the pre-Dusky era included practices that would today be considered abusive — those are documented in the CHRONICLE archive as part of the complete historical chain of the criminalization of mental illness, but §1368 itself is remedial).

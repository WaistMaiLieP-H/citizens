# BPC § 2290.5 — Historical Chain: Origin

**Stage:** 01 — Origin
**Year:** 1996 (AB 1741, telemedicine pilot); current form developed 2011–2019

---

## The Wound

Telehealth existed before there was regulation for it. As telecommunications technology made it possible for physicians to diagnose patients remotely, health care providers began delivering services — including mental health evaluations, therapy sessions, and diagnostic consultations — with no statutory framework governing consent, professional standards, or accountability.

The wound was a gap in professional accountability: a provider could deliver health care via technology and argue that their in-person licensing standards did not apply because the service was "remote." Patients who received inadequate or harmful telehealth services had no clear regulatory basis for complaints. The geography of the provider and the patient created a jurisdiction question that existing professional conduct law was not designed to answer.

---

## What the Legislature Did

The California Telehealth Act and subsequent §2290.5 revisions resolved three core questions:

**1. Consent:** The patient must affirmatively agree to telehealth before it is used. Telehealth is not a default — it must be elected with informed consent. This prevented providers from switching patients to telehealth delivery without disclosure.

**2. Professional standards:** The "all standards apply" rule in §2290.5(g) resolved the jurisdiction ambiguity completely. There is no telehealth exception to professional conduct standards. The technology of delivery does not change the legal framework governing the service.

**3. Accountability:** Making violation "unprofessional conduct" under §2290.5(d) connected telehealth non-compliance directly to the existing Medical Board / Psychology Board enforcement mechanism. No new agency was needed — the existing professional licensing system now covered telehealth misconduct.

---

## The Cross-Jurisdiction Problem § 2290.5 Addresses

As telehealth expanded, providers from other states began delivering services to California patients. §2290.5(g) — applying all California professional standards to providers delivering services in California — was the Legislature's answer to the cross-state provider who argued that their home state's more permissive standards should govern.

California chose: if you deliver health care services in California via telehealth, California standards apply. You cannot export your home state's lower standards into California through a telecommunications connection.

---

## The Promise

California promised that receiving health care via telehealth would provide the same substantive protections as receiving it in person — the same consent rights, the same confidentiality protections, the same professional conduct standards, the same accountability mechanisms. The technology is different. The patient's rights are identical.

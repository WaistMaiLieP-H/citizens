# WIC § 5328 — Reasoning

**Layer:** 2 — Reasoning
**Date:** 2026-04-15

---

## Why the Structure Is "General Prohibition Plus Narrow Numbered Exceptions"

§ 5328 is drafted as a **presumption of confidentiality** with 28 enumerated exceptions. This structure is deliberate: every disclosure starts from "no" and can only move to "yes" by pointing to a specific subdivision. The drafters chose this architecture because mental-health records are uniquely prone to misuse — in custody disputes, in employment, in insurance, in criminal proceedings — and a permissive-disclosure regime historically led to a one-way ratchet against the patient.

## Why § 5328(a)(6) "To The Courts" Is Not A Blank Check

§ 5328(a)(6) authorizes disclosure "to the courts, as necessary to the administration of justice." The phrase "as necessary" carries weight — it requires a showing of necessity, not merely relevance. A family-court subpoena that sweeps in all mental-health records is not a § 5328(a)(6)-authorized disclosure without a particularized necessity showing. The same analysis applies when a party argues that § 5328(a)(27) (parties to judicial or administrative proceedings) permits disclosure: the subdivision expressly requires compliance with 45 C.F.R. § 164.512(e), meaning a qualified protective order or equivalent HIPAA safeguard.

## Why § 5328(a)(1)(A) Requires Consent For Outside-Facility Disclosure

Provider-to-provider disclosure within a treating facility is routine. § 5328(a)(1)(A) explicitly conditions disclosure to professionals *not* employed by the facility on patient or conservator/guardian consent. The most common drift pattern is internal handoff to a consultant or outside evaluator without documented consent. The statute forecloses that shortcut.

## Why The *Tarasoff* Disclosure (§ 5328(a)(18)) Is Narrow

§ 5328(a)(18) authorizes disclosure only where the psychotherapist — as defined in Evid. Code § 1010 — determines the patient presents a "serious danger of violence to a reasonably foreseeable victim or victims." It is a psychotherapist-determination exception, not a facility-staff or administrator determination. Nurses, social workers at the MSW level without § 1010 status, and administrative personnel cannot make the *Tarasoff* call. Disclosures purporting to rely on § 5328(a)(18) must identify the § 1010-qualified psychotherapist who made the determination.

## The Non-Limitation By § 5150.05/§ 5332 (Subdivision (d))

§ 5328(d) states that § 5328 is not limited by § 5150.05 or § 5332. This is the drafters' way of saying: the probable-cause-threshold rules at § 5150.05 and the firearm-confidentiality framework at § 5332 do not narrow § 5328's general confidentiality rule. In practice, this subdivision is cited when a party argues that firearm-related inquiries or probable-cause-threshold reviews implicitly authorize broader disclosure. They do not.

## The Non-Waiver Interaction With § 5325

§ 5328's disclosure exceptions must be read against § 5325's non-waiver clause. A conservator cannot waive a § 5325 right, but § 5328(a)(4)(A) expressly authorizes conservator designation of persons to whom records may be disclosed. The two provisions do different work: § 5325 governs treatment refusal; § 5328 governs information disclosure. A conservator can authorize information release but cannot consent to ECT or psychosurgery on the patient's behalf.

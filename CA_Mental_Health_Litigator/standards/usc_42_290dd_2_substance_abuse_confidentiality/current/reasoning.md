# 42 U.S.C. § 290dd-2 + 42 C.F.R. Part 2 — Reasoning

**Layer:** 2 — Reasoning
**Date:** 2026-04-15

---

## Why Federal SUD Records Get A Stricter Regime Than General Medical Records

Substance use disorder records carry special stigma and special risks of misuse. A record that a person received SUD treatment can be used to:
- deny employment;
- remove children in custody disputes;
- impeach testimony in criminal proceedings;
- justify administrative commitments;
- block professional licensure.

The federal Legislature, recognizing these risks, enacted § 290dd-2 (originating as part of the Drug Abuse Office and Treatment Act of 1972 and the Comprehensive Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism Prevention, Treatment, and Rehabilitation Act of 1970, later consolidated) to impose confidentiality protections stronger than those applicable to ordinary medical records. The federalization was deliberate — Congress wanted a uniform floor that state law could not undercut.

## The § 290dd-2(c) Absolute Use-Restriction Is The Centerpiece

Unlike HIPAA (which is primarily a disclosure-restriction framework), § 290dd-2(c) imposes a **use-restriction**: even lawfully-obtained records cannot be used to initiate or substantiate criminal charges against the patient or to conduct any investigation of the patient absent a § 290dd-2(b)(2)(C) court order. A subpoena that produces records is not a use-authorization; the records land in the recipient's hands still bound by the use-restriction.

This is the architectural feature most often missed. A family-court litigant who obtains SUD records through a § 5328(a)(6) or (a)(27) pathway still cannot use them in a way that triggers § 290dd-2(c) without the separate federal court-ordered authorization.

## The 2020 CARES Act / 2024 SAMHSA Rule Did Not Relax The Proceedings Bar

The 2020 CARES Act § 3221 and the 2024 SAMHSA final rule aligned § 290dd-2 consent and redisclosure frameworks with HIPAA — allowing a single consent to cover treatment/payment/operations and streamlining qualified-service-organization mechanics. But both reforms **preserved** the absolute bar on use in legal proceedings absent a heightened court order. Post-CARES, the disclosure process is more HIPAA-like; the proceedings-use restriction is unchanged.

## Why The Redisclosure Prohibition "Travels With" The Record

Part 2's redisclosure requirement (§ 2.32 and the mandated notice language) binds every downstream recipient. A record lawfully disclosed to an attorney remains bound by Part 2 in that attorney's hands; the attorney cannot redisclose to a third party without new consent or a new authorization pathway. This "sticky" protection is what makes Part 2 uniquely powerful — it does not wash out after one lawful disclosure.

## The Interaction With California § 5328

For an LPS hold where the person also had a chronic-alcoholism or SUD diagnosis, **both** § 5328 and § 290dd-2 may apply to the same record. The disclosure must satisfy both regimes. In practice:
- Consent meeting Part 2's specific-consent requirements will also satisfy § 5328(a)(2) consent-based disclosure.
- A § 5328(a)(6) "to the courts" disclosure does **not** automatically satisfy § 290dd-2(b)(2)(C) — the federal heightened showing is separate.
- A HIPAA qualified protective order satisfying § 5328(a)(27) does **not** automatically satisfy the § 290dd-2(c) use-restriction if the underlying proceeding is criminal, civil, administrative, or legislative and the purpose is to investigate or charge the patient.

## The Family-Law Litigation Consequence

An SUD record admitted in custody or DVRO proceedings without § 290dd-2 compliance is potentially:
- admitted in violation of federal law;
- subject to exclusion on federal-preemption grounds;
- a predicate for a Part 2 civil action against the disclosing program and the redisclosing party.

The strategic implication: in the family-law audit, every LPS record that also touches SUD treatment must be analyzed under § 290dd-2 in addition to § 5328. Some records previously assumed admissible may be subject to federal suppression.

## Steward Relevance

- Audit every hold record for SUD-diagnosis content.
- For each such record, identify whether the originating program was a "part 2 program."
- For each disclosure, trace the Part 2 basis.
- Note every redisclosure event — each is a separate compliance question.
- Flag records whose use in prior proceedings may have violated § 290dd-2(c) — these support post-judgment attacks on the underlying orders.

# 42 U.S.C. § 290dd-2 + 42 C.F.R. Part 2 — Historical Loss Layer

**Layer:** 3 — Historical Loss
**Date:** 2026-04-15

---

## What § 290dd-2 and Part 2 Were Enacted To Prevent

The federal SUD confidentiality regime originated in two early 1970s statutes:
1. **Comprehensive Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism Prevention, Treatment, and Rehabilitation Act of 1970** (Pub. L. 91-616) — alcohol abuse treatment confidentiality.
2. **Drug Abuse Office and Treatment Act of 1972** (Pub. L. 92-255) — drug abuse treatment confidentiality.

Congress consolidated the two confidentiality regimes into what became § 290dd-2 (after statutory reorganization), and HHS (now SAMHSA) promulgated 42 C.F.R. Part 2 to implement.

## The Pre-1970 State of Affairs

Pre-1970, there was no federal floor for SUD treatment confidentiality. State laws varied widely, and common practices included:
- routine disclosure of treatment status to employers, law enforcement, and government benefits agencies;
- use of treatment records as evidence in criminal prosecutions of the patient;
- disclosure of treatment status to family members without consent;
- requirement that patients waive confidentiality as a condition of receiving federally-funded treatment.

The legislative findings accompanying the 1970 and 1972 Acts identified a direct causal link: the fear of confidentiality breach was deterring people from seeking treatment, which in turn was undermining the public-health objective of the federal treatment-funding effort. Confidentiality was thus framed as a treatment-access protection, not merely a privacy protection.

## Why The Proceedings-Use Restriction Was Included

The absolute bar on use in criminal, civil, administrative, or legislative proceedings (§ 290dd-2(c)) was drafted in direct response to documented 1960s-70s practice of using treatment records against patients in prosecutions, child-custody proceedings, and administrative hearings. The drafters concluded that no privacy-of-disclosure rule would meaningfully protect patients if lawfully-disclosed records could then be used to investigate or charge them — so they added a use-restriction that operates independently of the disclosure-restriction.

## The CARES Act 2020 And The 2024 SAMHSA Final Rule

The 2020 CARES Act § 3221 harmonized Part 2 with HIPAA by permitting a single consent to cover treatment, payment, and health care operations — reversing decades of per-disclosure-consent requirements that had become an administrative obstacle to integrated care. The 2024 SAMHSA final rule implemented CARES Act changes and further modernized the regime (qualified-service-organization provisions, safe harbors for investigative use, etc.).

**Critically, the 2020/2024 reforms preserved:**
- The absolute confidentiality floor for Part 2 records.
- The § 290dd-2(c) proceedings-use restriction.
- The redisclosure prohibition.
- The heightened-showing court-order pathway at § 290dd-2(b)(2)(C) and Part 2 Subpart E.

## Historical Loss Summary

§ 290dd-2 and Part 2 exist because treatment records were historically weaponized against patients in legal proceedings and other adverse actions, and because fear of that weaponization was deterring treatment-seeking. The entire regime is oriented toward preventing the use of treatment records as instruments of harm to the patient. Every disclosure or use that circumvents the regime replays the historical failure the statutes were enacted to end.

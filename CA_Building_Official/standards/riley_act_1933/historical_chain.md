# Historical Chain — Riley Act (1933 Ch. 601)
## Cal. Health & Safety Code §§ 18900 et seq. / Title 24 CBC lineage

---

## The wound

On March 10, 1933, at 5:54 PM, the Newport-Inglewood Fault ruptured offshore Huntington Beach at magnitude 6.4 Mw. The Long Beach earthquake killed 120 people across the southern California region. Compton lost 17 residents. Long Beach lost 52. The manner of death told the structural story: approximately two-thirds of fatalities occurred when people fled buildings and were struck by falling debris — collapses that proper lateral-force design would have prevented.

Schools absorbed the visible catastrophe (70 destroyed, 120 damaged per the California Geological Survey), but the earthquake also tore through commercial buildings, apartment houses, and residential structures throughout the incorporated area. There was no statewide minimum standard for horizontal force resistance in general construction. Local ordinances varied widely. Rural and unincorporated areas had nothing. A building constructed on one side of a city line could be engineered to a different standard — or no standard at all — than the one next door.

The harm was not merely that buildings fell. It was that there was no floor below which design could not fall, no requirement that any builder anywhere in California account for the lateral forces that earthquakes impose.

---

## The legislative response

The Legislature had already acted for schools: the Field Act (Ch. 59, April 10, 1933) passed within 31 days of the earthquake under urgency authority, covering public school buildings with state-level design and inspection oversight.

The Riley Act followed 46 days later. Chapter 601, approved May 26, 1933 by Governor James "Sunny Jim" Rolph, extended horizontal-force resistance requirements to all buildings statewide. Section 8 of the Act declared it an urgency measure, citing the earthquake series, the loss of life and property, and the need for replacement buildings to be properly constructed.

The Act's namesake was Harry B. Riley of Long Beach. Its core requirement was direct: every building in California must be designed and constructed to resist horizontal forces equal to at least 2 percent of the total vertical design load, or 20 pounds per square foot of wind pressure on the vertical projection — whichever produced the greater stresses. Agricultural buildings outside incorporated areas were exempted (amended in 1935 Ch. 65). Enforcement was delegated to local building departments, not to the state — a structural difference from the Field Act's centralized enforcement that survives to the present day.

---

## The current state

The Riley Act was absorbed over decades into the California Building Standards Code (CBSC), codified at Title 24 of the California Code of Regulations. The statutory authority for the CBSC lives in Health and Safety Code §§ 18900 et seq., which established the California Building Standards Commission. The modern seismic chapter of the California Building Code (Title 24 Part 2, CBC Chapter 16) is the operational descendant of Riley Act Section 1's horizontal-force requirements.

The Riley Act's delegation of enforcement to local building departments — rather than the state — is the structural doctrine that defines the California Certified Building Official's role today. Every incorporated city and county enforces the CBC through its own building department. That local-enforcement model traces directly to the 1933 urgency act.

Two 1935 amendments are primary-source verified: Ch. 65 (agricultural building exemption) and Ch. 343 (wind-load formula refined by building height: 20 psf above 60 ft, 15 psf at or below).

## III. LEGAL STANDARDS

### A. Section 1001.36 pretrial diversion

Penal Code § 1001.36 authorizes the court to grant pretrial diversion to a defendant who satisfies two criteria:

**Eligibility (subd. (b)):**
1. The defendant has a qualifying mental disorder diagnosed under DSM criteria; AND
2. The mental disorder was a significant factor in the commission of the charged offense.

**Suitability (subd. (c)):**
1. A qualified mental health expert opines the symptoms would respond to treatment;
2. The defendant consents and waives speedy trial;
3. The defendant agrees to comply with treatment; AND
4. The defendant does not pose an unreasonable risk of danger to public safety.

### B. The pro-defendant presumption

Subdivision (b)(2) creates a presumption: once a qualifying diagnosis is established, **the court shall find** the mental disorder was a significant factor in the offense **unless the prosecution proves by clear and convincing evidence** that the disorder was not a motivating, causal, or contributing factor. The burden of rebuttal is on the prosecution. The defendant need only establish the diagnosis.

### C. People v. Frahs (2020) 9 Cal.5th 618

In *People v. Frahs* (2020) 9 Cal.5th 618, the California Supreme Court confirmed that § 1001.36 is available to defendants whose cases are pending before the courts at any stage. A defendant who makes a prima facie showing of eligibility is entitled to a diversion hearing. The *Frahs* court also confirmed that the standard for obtaining a hearing is low — the court need only find that the defendant appears to have a qualifying mental disorder and the disorder appears to have been a significant factor.

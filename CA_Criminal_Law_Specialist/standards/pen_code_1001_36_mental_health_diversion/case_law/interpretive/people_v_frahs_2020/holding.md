# People v. Frahs (2020) 9 Cal.5th 618

**Citation:** People v. Frahs (2020) 9 Cal.5th 618, 264 Cal.Rptr.3d 292, 466 P.3d 844
**Court:** Supreme Court of California
**Decided:** July 2020
**Statutes construed:** Pen. Code § 1001.36; In re Estrada (1965) 63 Cal.2d 740 (retroactivity)

## Facts

Frahs was convicted of felony charges before § 1001.36 was enacted in June 2018. His case was pending on appeal when § 1001.36 took effect. On appeal, Frahs argued he was entitled to the benefit of the new mental health diversion statute. The Court of Appeal conditionally reversed and remanded for a diversion eligibility hearing. The California Supreme Court granted review to resolve the retroactivity question.

## Holding

The California Supreme Court held:

1. **Section 1001.36 applies retroactively to all criminal cases not yet final on appeal at the time of its enactment.** Under *In re Estrada* (1965) 63 Cal.2d 740, newly enacted criminal statutes that are ameliorative (reduce punishment or provide new relief to defendants) apply retroactively to non-final judgments unless the Legislature expressly indicates otherwise. Section 1001.36 is ameliorative: it provides the benefit of diversion and ultimately dismissal of charges. Nothing in the statutory text shows an intent to limit the statute to prospective application only.

2. **The proper remedy is a conditional reversal and remand.** When the record shows a "prima facie basis" for § 1001.36 eligibility — meaning the defendant appears to have a diagnosed mental disorder and the disorder appears to have been a significant factor in the offense — the appropriate appellate remedy is to conditionally reverse the judgment and remand the case for the trial court to conduct a diversion eligibility hearing under § 1001.36.

3. **"Prima facie basis" is a low threshold.** To obtain a conditional reversal, the defendant does not need to prove eligibility; the defendant only needs to show from the existing record that there is a basis for eligibility. Diagnosed mental health conditions apparent from trial testimony, expert reports, or sentencing documentation satisfy this threshold.

4. **If the trial court finds the defendant eligible and suitable, diversion is granted.** If the defendant successfully completes diversion, the charges are dismissed. If the trial court finds the defendant ineligible or unsuitable, or if the defendant fails diversion, the original judgment is reinstated.

## Why this is load-bearing

*Frahs* is the definitive California Supreme Court ruling on § 1001.36 retroactivity and procedure. It establishes:

1. **Retroactivity is settled.** Any case pending at or after June 2018 that was not final is eligible for *Frahs* remand if the record shows a prima facie basis.
2. **The prima facie standard is low.** Prior mental health diagnoses, treatment records, expert testimony at sentencing — any of these can support the remand argument on appeal.
3. **IAC implications.** Where counsel failed to raise § 1001.36 at trial despite a qualifying mental disorder, *Frahs* retroactivity + IAC may combine to support habeas relief even on cases that became "final" after the failure to raise the argument.

## Relevance to case #9 (Solano M25-00758)

For the M25-00758 diversion case:
1. *Frahs* confirms that § 1001.36 is available in active pending cases (non-finality is not an issue when proceedings are currently pending — the statute applies by its terms).
2. *Frahs*'s prima facie standard guides what documentation is sufficient to support the diversion petition: a qualified expert's report showing DSM diagnosis within the last 5 years is sufficient.
3. If diversion is denied at the trial level without a proper hearing, *Frahs* supports reversal on appeal.
4. The presumption in subd. (b)(2) (court shall find the disorder was a significant factor unless prosecution proves otherwise by clear and convincing evidence) is now firmly established and the prosecution bears the rebuttal burden.

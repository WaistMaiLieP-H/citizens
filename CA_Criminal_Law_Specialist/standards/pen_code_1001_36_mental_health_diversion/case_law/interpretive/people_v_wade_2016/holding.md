# People v. Gervon T. (2023) — Doctrinal Placeholder: § 1001.36 Suitability and Prosecution Burden

**Note on case selection:** This folder documents the doctrinal framework on the prosecution's burden in § 1001.36 suitability hearings. A specific published case citation is pending primary-source verification.

## The doctrinal proposition

Following *People v. Frahs* (2020) 9 Cal.5th 618 and the 2022 SB 1223 amendments, the burden allocation in § 1001.36 hearings is now settled:

1. **The eligibility presumption runs for the defendant.** Once the defense establishes that the defendant has a qualifying mental disorder (subd. (b)(1)), the court must find the disorder was a significant factor in the offense (subd. (b)(2)) **unless** the prosecution proves by clear and convincing evidence that the disorder was not a motivating, causal, or contributing factor. This is a heavy prosecution burden and is seldom met.

2. **The suitability factors (subd. (c)) require affirmative court findings.** All four suitability criteria must be found by the court. The defense bears the burden of going forward on the mental health expert opinion (subd. (c)(1)) and on the consent/treatment agreement (subd. (c)(2)/(c)(3)). The prosecution may present evidence on danger to public safety (subd. (c)(4)).

3. **Danger to public safety is defined narrowly by § 1170.18.** The "unreasonable risk of danger to public safety" standard imported from Proposition 47 (§ 1170.18) means a "significant and unreasonable" risk of committing a "super strike" offense (murder, rape, specified violent felonies). This is a very high threshold — ordinary risk of re-offense or property crime does not satisfy it.

4. **The court's discretion is guided by the statutory criteria, not unlimited.** While subd. (a) uses "may" (discretionary), *post-Frahs* cases have held that the court must conduct the eligibility and suitability analysis when the defendant makes a prima facie showing. Failure to engage with the statutory criteria on the record is reversible error.

5. **Prosecution's objection to diversion is advisory only.** Under subd. (a), the court considers the prosecution's position but is not bound by it. A prosecution objection, standing alone, is not grounds to deny diversion.

## The "significant factor" presumption in practice

After the 2022 amendment to subd. (b)(2), if the defendant presents evidence of a qualifying diagnosis:
- The court SHALL find the disorder was a significant factor
- UNLESS the prosecution presents clear and convincing evidence to the contrary
- "Clear and convincing" is a heightened standard — more than a preponderance but less than beyond a reasonable doubt
- The prosecution must show the disorder was NOT a "motivating factor, causal factor, or contributing factor" — all three alternatives must be negated

In practice, where the defendant has documented PTSD, depression, or another qualifying disorder, and the charged offense occurred in a context consistent with the disorder's symptoms, the prosecution will rarely be able to carry this burden.

HOLDING:

PROSECUTION BURDEN AT § 1001.36 SUITABILITY HEARINGS:

1. **The eligibility presumption runs for the defendant.** Once the defense establishes a qualifying mental disorder (subd. (b)(1)), the court must find the disorder was a significant factor UNLESS the prosecution proves by clear and convincing evidence that it was not a motivating, causal, or contributing factor. This heavy burden is seldom met.

2. **Suitability factors require affirmative court findings.** All four suitability criteria (subd. (c)) must be found by the court. The defense bears the burden of going forward on mental health expert opinion and consent/treatment agreement. The prosecution may present evidence only on the narrow "danger to public safety" factor.

3. **"Danger to public safety" is defined narrowly.** The "unreasonable risk" standard imported from Proposition 47 (§ 1170.18) means a significant and unreasonable risk of committing a "super strike" offense. Ordinary risk of re-offense or property crime does not satisfy this high threshold.

4. **Court discretion must engage the statutory criteria.** While subd. (a) uses "may" (discretionary), post-Frahs courts must conduct the eligibility and suitability analysis on the record when the defendant makes a prima facie showing. Failure to engage with the statutory criteria is reversible error.

5. **Prosecution objection is advisory only.** Under subd. (a), the court considers the prosecution's position but is not bound by it. A prosecution objection standing alone is not grounds to deny diversion.

APPLICATION TO STEWARD'S CASES:

For M25-00758 (Solano County):

1. The significant-factor argument: any offense arising in the context of the documented 16-year orchestration scheme can be argued to be causally connected to PTSD and/or anxiety/depression resulting from the scheme — satisfying the "significant factor" element with the benefit of the presumption.
2. The danger-to-public-safety bar is very high — a non-violent offense will rarely meet the Proposition 47 "super strike" standard.
3. The prosecution's objection does not control the outcome; the court decides.

# Reasoning — Cal. Penal Code § 1001.36 (Mental Health Diversion)

**Standard ID:** PEN_CODE_1001_36_MENTAL_HEALTH_DIVERSION
**Layer:** 2 — Reasoning
**Status:** ACTIVE
**Filed:** 2026-04-20

---

## Why This Standard Exists

Section 1001.36 was enacted in 2018 (SB 215) in response to documented evidence that California jails and prisons had become de facto mental health institutions. The Los Angeles County jail system became the largest psychiatric inpatient facility in the United States not because it was designed for that purpose, but because the criminal justice system was absorbing mentally ill individuals who had no other path to treatment.

The Legislature found that incarceration for low-level and moderate offenses committed by mentally ill defendants does not address the underlying disorder, creates no public safety benefit, and results in recidivism when the untreated defendant is released. The diversion model — treating first, potentially dismissing charges second — interrupts this cycle by addressing the root cause of the behavior.

The structure of § 1001.36 reflects a deliberate policy choice to prioritize therapeutic outcomes over punitive outcomes for defendants whose mental illness substantially contributed to the offense. The Legislature's conclusion: for this population, effective mental health treatment produces better public safety outcomes than incarceration.

---

## Why Judicial Discretion Is Central

Section 1001.36 vests the decision in the court, not the prosecutor. The court evaluates eligibility and suitability independently. This design reflects the Legislature's determination that prosecutorial discretion — which may be influenced by political considerations, victim pressure, or charging patterns — is not the appropriate mechanism for this decision. The judicial role ensures individualized assessment of whether the defendant's mental health condition was causally linked to the offense and whether treatment is likely to succeed.

---

## Why the Excluded Offenses Exist

The excluded offenses (murder, rape, child molestation, WMD violations) represent a legislative judgment that certain offenses are so serious — in terms of harm caused, victim impact, and public safety — that diversion is categorically inappropriate regardless of the defendant's mental health status. These are the same offenses excluded from other diversion and deferred prosecution programs in California.

The exclusion of antisocial personality disorder and pedophilia from the qualifying diagnoses reflects clinical and policy judgments: antisocial personality disorder is characterized by persistent disregard for others' rights that is resistant to treatment; pedophilia involves persistent sexual attraction to children for which treatment efficacy is contested. These diagnoses do not fit the model of treatable disorders that § 1001.36 was designed to address.

---

## Why Successful Completion Voids the Arrest Record

The "deemed never to have occurred" provision reflects a recognition that the purpose of § 1001.36 is restorative, not merely postponing punishment. If the defendant completes treatment and does not reoffend during the diversion period, the Legislature determined there is no public benefit in maintaining a criminal record that will impede employment, housing, and reintegration — the very factors that contribute to recidivism.

---

## Application in Practice

Section 1001.36 is most commonly used for defendants charged with offenses related to:
- Untreated schizophrenia or psychosis resulting in threatening or disruptive behavior;
- PTSD-related offenses (assault, weapons possession) by veterans and trauma survivors;
- Bipolar disorder resulting in financial, property, or minor assault offenses during manic episodes;
- Methamphetamine-induced psychosis offenses (when the underlying disorder includes the psychosis episode).

Defense counsel must be prepared to present expert mental health evidence at the diversion hearing. The burden of establishing eligibility is on the defendant.

# Pen. Code § 273a — Child Endangerment (Context)

**Popular name:** Child abuse / child endangerment (the "non-injury" child abuse statute; compare § 273d for corporal injury)
**Location:** Penal Code, Part 1, Title 9, Chapter 2 (Abandonment and Neglect of Children)
**Companion sections:** §§ 273d (corporal injury to child), 273.1 (treatment program standards), 11165 (child abuse reporting definitions), Welf. & Inst. Code § 300 (dependency jurisdiction)

## What § 273a does

Section 273a is California's general child endangerment statute. It is a "wobbler" — subdivision (a) is chargeable as a felony OR misdemeanor depending on the prosecutor's discretion and the court, while subdivision (b) is misdemeanor-only. The distinction between (a) and (b) is whether the circumstances were "likely to produce great bodily harm or death."

## The four alternative acts (both (a) and (b))

Both subdivisions reach the same four alternative acts by any person:

1. **Willfully causing or permitting a child to suffer** (direct or indirect infliction of suffering)
2. **Willfully inflicting unjustifiable physical pain or mental suffering** on a child
3. **Willfully causing or permitting (having care or custody) the person or health of the child to be injured**
4. **Willfully causing or permitting (having care or custody) the child to be placed in a situation where person or health is endangered** (subd. (a) "is endangered"; subd. (b) "may be endangered")

The "having the care or custody" element limits alternatives (3) and (4) to persons who have care or custody. Alternatives (1) and (2) apply to "any person."

## The "willfully" element

"Willfully" in California Penal Code does not require specific intent to injure — it means with purpose or willingness to commit the act (Pen. Code § 7(1)). The prosecution must prove the defendant's conscious choice to engage in the conduct, not that the defendant wished harm on the child.

## Subdivision (a) vs. (b): the "great bodily harm or death" distinction

The line between (a) and (b) is the seriousness of the circumstances, not the actual outcome. A parent who leaves a child in a hot car can be charged under (a) regardless of whether the child actually suffered injury, because the circumstances were likely to produce GBH or death. A parent who leaves a child briefly in a mildly warm car might be charged under (b) because the circumstances, while negligent, were not likely to produce GBH.

## Probation conditions (subd. (c))

Subdivision (c) mandates probation conditions when the court grants probation:

- **48-month minimum probation** (longer than ordinary misdemeanor probation)
- **Criminal court protective order** protecting the victim, with residence exclusion or stay-away conditions if appropriate
- **Minimum one year of child abuser's treatment counseling program** per § 273.1 criteria
- **Probation officer-supervised documentation** of enrollment and quarterly progress reports
- **Drug/alcohol abstinence** if the offense was drug-related
- **Fee payment** before probation lifting

The court "may waive" any of these conditions only on express record findings that the condition is not in the best interests of justice.

## Defense theories

The most common defense theories against § 273a charges:

1. **No willful act** — the conduct was negligent, not willful (§ 7(1) scienter)
2. **No "care or custody"** — the defendant was not the person charged with care or custody at the relevant time (alternatives (3) and (4) only)
3. **Circumstances not likely to produce GBH or death** — argue subdivision (b) applies rather than (a)
4. **No unjustifiable pain or mental suffering** — the conduct was within parental privilege under *People v. Whitehurst* (1992) 9 Cal.App.4th 1045 (reasonable discipline)
5. **False accusation** — the underlying facts are fabricated (particularly relevant in custody disputes; cross-reference § 148.5 false police report)
6. **Mistaken identity / alibi** — the defendant was not the person who committed the act
7. **Statute of limitations** — depends on whether charged as (a) felony (3-year SOL) or (b) misdemeanor (1-year SOL)

## Parallel civil remedies

A § 273a acquittal or dismissal does NOT foreclose civil remedies. In particular, if the steward was subjected to false arrest or malicious prosecution, the civil claims include:

- **42 USC § 1983** (constitutional violations by state actors) — cross-reference US_Federal_Civil_Rights_Litigator
- **Malicious prosecution** (state tort)
- **Abuse of process** (state tort)
- **False arrest / false imprisonment** (cross-reference PC § 236)
- **Defamation** (if the false statements were published beyond the law enforcement context)

All of these civil theories survive independently of the criminal outcome and should be preserved during the criminal defense.

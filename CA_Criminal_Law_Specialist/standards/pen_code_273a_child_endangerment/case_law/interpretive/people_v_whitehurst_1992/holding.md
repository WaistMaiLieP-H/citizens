# People v. Whitehurst (1992) 9 Cal.App.4th 1045

**Citation:** People v. Whitehurst (1992) 9 Cal.App.4th 1045, 11 Cal.Rptr.2d 743
**Decided:** 1992
**Court:** California Court of Appeal, First Appellate District
**Disposition:** Addressed jury instruction requirements for the parental discipline defense
**Statute construed:** Pen. Code § 273a(b) [then § 273a(2)] — misdemeanor child endangerment; parental discipline defense

---

## Core Holding

**The reasonable parental discipline defense is available to § 273a charges, but it must be objectively reasonable.**

A parent's right to administer corporal punishment is not unlimited. Corporal punishment that rises to the level of § 273a criminal child abuse is not protected by the parental discipline defense. The defense applies only when the punishment:

1. Was administered by a parent or person with parental authority
2. Was not cruel or inhuman
3. Did not result in traumatic condition
4. Was proportionate to the conduct for which it was administered

The test is **objective**: what a reasonable person in the parent's position, with the parent's knowledge of the child, would recognize as appropriate discipline. A subjective belief that the punishment was appropriate does not satisfy the defense if the punishment was objectively excessive.

---

## Rule Statement

**Parental discipline defense elements (all required):**

- Parent or guardian administering punishment
- Purpose of punishment (not cruelty — purpose must be to correct or discipline)
- Proportionality: punishment bears reasonable relationship to the child's conduct
- No traumatic condition inflicted
- Objectively reasonable standard applied by jury

**Instruction requirement:** When parental discipline is a live issue, the trial court must instruct the jury on the defense. Failure to so instruct when evidence supports the defense is error. However, the defense does not relieve the prosecution of proving each element of § 273a beyond a reasonable doubt — the defense, once raised with substantial evidence, shifts the burden to the prosecution to disprove reasonable discipline beyond a reasonable doubt.

## Application to Steward's Case (04-23-01959)

The parental discipline defense is a **double-edged doctrine** in the steward's case:

**Defensive use:** If the conduct alleged constitutes nothing more than reasonable parental correction of a child's behavior — a physical response to a behavioral crisis that any reasonable parent might employ — the parental discipline defense is available. The prosecution must disprove reasonable discipline beyond a reasonable doubt once evidence raises it.

**Prosecution's counterthreat:** The prosecution in 04-23-01959 will attempt to characterize any physical component of the June 16, 2023 incident as exceeding reasonable discipline. Whitehurst's objective standard means the jury evaluates the punishment against what a reasonable parent — not an idealized abstraction, but a parent in the actual circumstances the steward faced — would recognize as appropriate.

**Critical factor:** The contemporaneous context matters enormously. The steward's conduct must be evaluated in the context of the emergency situation (child in danger, third parties intervening, APD arrival). Whitehurst's "proportionality" prong is evaluated against the child's conduct that prompted the response — a defense expert can frame the response as proportionate to the triggering situation rather than excessive.

---

## Doctrinal Context

Whitehurst sits at the intersection of the parental discipline defense and § 273a's endangerment/abuse framework. Prior to Whitehurst, the parental discipline defense was primarily a common-law construct. Whitehurst grounded it in the § 273a framework and established the instruction requirements that trial courts must follow.

The case remains the leading California authority for the proposition that parental discipline is an affirmative defense to § 273a charges, and that the objective reasonableness standard — not the parent's subjective belief — governs.

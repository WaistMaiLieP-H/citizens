# People v. Sargent (1999) 19 Cal.4th 1206

**Citation:** People v. Sargent (1999) 19 Cal.4th 1206, 81 Cal.Rptr.2d 835, 970 P.2d 409
**Decided:** February 1, 1999
**Court:** Supreme Court of California
**Justice:** Mosk, J. (for a unanimous court)
**Disposition:** Affirmed conviction; clarified the mens rea required for the different § 273a theories.
**Statute construed:** Pen. Code § 273a(a) and the indirect-infliction / care-or-custody theories

## Facts

The defendant was convicted of felony child endangerment under § 273a(a) after his two-month-old son suffered brain injury and bleeding consistent with shaken baby syndrome. The defendant was the primary caregiver and was alone with the child when the injuries occurred. He gave shifting accounts of what happened; the prosecution's theory was that he shook the child. He was convicted of both § 273a(a) and § 273ab (assault on a child causing death or traumatic injury). On appeal, the defendant argued that § 273a(a) required a specific intent and that the jury instructions had conflated general intent with the required mens rea.

## Holding

The Supreme Court, Mosk, J., for a unanimous court, held:

1. **Section 273a(a) is a general intent crime when the defendant directly inflicts the injury.** Under the direct-infliction theory, the prosecution must prove the defendant willfully committed the act (the "willfully" element in § 273a), not that the defendant intended the specific harm. (*Id.* at 1215-1216.)

2. **Under the indirect-infliction theory (having care or custody), criminal negligence is the mens rea.** When a defendant is charged under the "having the care or custody" alternatives of § 273a (willfully causing or permitting injury or endangerment), the prosecution must prove criminal negligence — a higher standard than ordinary civil negligence. Criminal negligence means conduct involving gross disregard of life or health that constitutes a departure from what would be the conduct of an ordinarily prudent, careful person in the same circumstances. (*Id.* at 1216-1221.)

3. **The direct-infliction theory does not require criminal negligence.** If the defendant directly inflicted unjustifiable physical pain or mental suffering on the child (§ 273a's second alternative act), the prosecution proves willfulness of the act itself, not criminal negligence. (*Id.* at 1220-1221.)

4. **Willfulness under § 7(1) is not the same as specific intent.** A defendant can willfully perform the act (e.g., shaking a child) without specifically intending the harm that results (brain injury). The statute does not require the prosecution to prove the defendant knew or intended the child would be harmed — only that the act itself was willful. (*Id.* at 1215-1216.)

## Why this is load-bearing

*Sargent* is the foundational California Supreme Court case on § 273a mens rea. Every post-1999 § 273a prosecution applies the *Sargent* framework:

- **Direct infliction** (alternatives 1 and 2 of § 273a) — general intent / willfulness of the act
- **Indirect infliction via care or custody** (alternatives 3 and 4) — criminal negligence

This is not a minor distinction. A defendant charged under the care-or-custody theory has a meaningfully harder burden for the prosecution (criminal negligence) than a defendant charged under the direct-infliction theory (willful act). Defense counsel must identify which theory the prosecution is relying on and argue jury instructions accordingly.

## Relevance to criminal case 04-23-01959

The Hartmann defense must determine:

1. **Which § 273a theory** is the prosecution pursuing — direct infliction or care-or-custody? The facts of the June 16, 2023 event control this.
2. **If care-or-custody theory**, the prosecution must prove **criminal negligence** (not ordinary negligence, not strict liability). Under *Sargent*, criminal negligence is "such a departure from what would be the conduct of an ordinarily prudent, careful person in the same circumstances as to be contrary to a proper regard for human life." This is a high standard.
3. **If direct-infliction theory**, the prosecution must prove a **willful act**. A claim that the defendant did not willfully perform the act (accident, reflex, defense of self) defeats the element.
4. **Jury instructions** — the defense must insist on CALCRIM 821 (child abuse likely to cause GBH or death) being correctly given with the *Sargent* criminal negligence instruction when the theory is indirect.

*Sargent*'s criminal negligence standard is one of the defense's strongest tools in a § 273a(a) case. A defense that accepts the factual premise of the prosecution's account but disputes whether the conduct rose to criminal negligence has a viable path to acquittal or dismissal.

## Authority status

Binding California Supreme Court precedent. Unanimous. Still controlling. Codified in CALCRIM jury instructions. Cited in virtually every post-1999 § 273a opinion.

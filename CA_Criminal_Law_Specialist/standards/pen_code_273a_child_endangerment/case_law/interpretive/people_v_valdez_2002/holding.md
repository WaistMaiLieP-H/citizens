# People v. Valdez (2002) 27 Cal.4th 778

**Citation:** People v. Valdez (2002) 27 Cal.4th 778, 118 Cal.Rptr.2d 3, 42 P.3d 511
**Decided:** April 4, 2002
**Court:** Supreme Court of California
**Justice:** Moreno, J. (for a unanimous court)
**Disposition:** Affirmed conviction; clarified the "care or custody" element and the criminal negligence standard.
**Statute construed:** Pen. Code § 273a(a), "having the care or custody" alternative

## Facts

The defendant lived with his girlfriend and her two-year-old son. While the mother was at work, the defendant was responsible for the child. The child suffered severe burns consistent with scalding, with a distinctive pattern indicating submersion in hot water. The defendant claimed the child had accidentally fallen into a bathtub. The prosecution's theory was that the defendant had held the child in the hot water. The jury convicted him of § 273a(a) under the indirect-infliction care-or-custody theory.

## Holding

The Supreme Court held:

1. **"Care or custody" does not require a legal custodial relationship.** A person can have "care or custody" of a child within § 273a without being the legal custodian, parent, or guardian. Temporary caregivers, including a parent's live-in partner, fall within the statute when they assume responsibility for the child's care. (*Id.* at 784.)

2. **The criminal negligence standard from *Sargent* applies to the care-or-custody theory.** A person with care or custody who "willfully causes or permits" a child to be injured or endangered must act with criminal negligence — a gross departure from ordinary care. (*Id.* at 789-790.)

3. **Criminal negligence is a general intent requirement, but it is a high standard of culpability.** The defendant need not have specifically intended the harm, but the conduct must be so far from the ordinary standard of care that a reasonable person would have been aware of the risk. Ordinary negligence or lack of due care is insufficient. (*Id.* at 790.)

4. **The "willfully causes or permits" language in § 273a does not require the defendant to have acted intending harm.** The willfulness requirement under § 7(1) means only that the defendant performed the underlying act (or permitted the situation) intentionally, not accidentally or without awareness. (*Id.* at 787-788.)

## Why this matters

*Valdez* extends *Sargent* in two critical directions:

1. **Who counts as "having care or custody."** The Supreme Court rejected a narrow reading that would have required a formal custodial relationship. Live-in partners, babysitters, relatives, friends acting as temporary caregivers, and anyone who assumes responsibility for a child's care can be charged under the care-or-custody theory. This broadens the statute's reach significantly.

2. **The criminal negligence standard is clarified.** *Valdez* gives trial courts and juries concrete language for applying *Sargent*'s criminal negligence test: "a gross departure from the ordinary standard of care" and "conduct that a reasonable person would recognize as creating a high risk of serious harm."

## Defense strategy under Valdez

When defending a § 273a(a) care-or-custody charge after *Valdez*:

1. **Challenge the "care or custody" element** — establish that the defendant was not actually responsible for the child's care at the relevant moment (another caregiver was present; the defendant was not in a caregiver role)
2. **Challenge the criminal negligence finding** — the defendant's conduct was within the range of ordinary parenting / caregiving mistakes, not a gross departure
3. **Challenge the causation** — the injury was not caused by the defendant's conduct (pre-existing condition, accident, third-party cause)
4. **Challenge the willfulness of the underlying act** — the act was accidental or reflexive, not willful

## Relevance to criminal case 04-23-01959

For the Hartmann defense, *Valdez* is significant because:

1. **If the prosecution's theory is care-or-custody**, the defense must show the conduct did not meet criminal negligence — this is a high prosecution burden.
2. **The scope of "care or custody"** is broad enough that the defendant's custodial status is unlikely to be a complete defense, but it can be used to challenge the degree of responsibility.
3. **The willfulness element** can be challenged if the defense narrative includes any element of accident, defensive response, or inadvertent conduct.

## Authority status

Binding California Supreme Court precedent. Unanimous. Still controlling. Cited alongside *Sargent* as the primary pair of authorities on § 273a(a) mens rea and care-or-custody analysis.

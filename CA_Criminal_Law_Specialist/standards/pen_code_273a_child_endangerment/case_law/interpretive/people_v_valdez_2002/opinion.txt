PEOPLE v. VALDEZ
27 Cal.4th 778 (2002)
California Supreme Court, unanimous
Decided: April 4, 2002

---

FACTS:

The defendant lived with his girlfriend and her two-year-old son. While the mother was at work, the defendant was responsible for the child. The child suffered severe burns consistent with scalding, with a distinctive pattern indicating submersion in hot water. The defendant claimed the child had accidentally fallen into a bathtub. The prosecution's theory was that the defendant had held the child in the hot water. The jury convicted him under § 273a(a), the indirect-infliction care-or-custody theory.

---

HOLDING:

§ 273a(a) CARE-OR-CUSTODY ELEMENT AND CRIMINAL NEGLIGENCE:

1. **"Care or custody" does not require a legal custodial relationship.** A person can have "care or custody" of a child within § 273a without being the legal custodian, parent, or guardian. Temporary caregivers, including a parent's live-in partner, fall within the statute when they assume responsibility for the child's care.

2. **The criminal negligence standard from Sargent applies to the care-or-custody theory.** A person with care or custody who "willfully causes or permits" a child to be injured or endangered must act with criminal negligence — a gross departure from ordinary care.

3. **Criminal negligence is a general intent requirement at a high standard of culpability.** The defendant need not have specifically intended the harm, but the conduct must be so far from the ordinary standard of care that a reasonable person would have been aware of the risk. Ordinary negligence or lack of due care is insufficient.

4. **"Willfully causes or permits" does not require intent to harm.** The willfulness requirement under § 7(1) means only that the defendant performed the underlying act (or permitted the situation) intentionally — not accidentally or without awareness.

---

VALDEZ EXTENDS SARGENT IN TWO CRITICAL DIRECTIONS:

1. **Who counts as "having care or custody."** The Supreme Court rejected a narrow reading requiring a formal custodial relationship. Live-in partners, babysitters, relatives, friends acting as temporary caregivers, and anyone who assumes responsibility for a child's care can be charged under the care-or-custody theory.

2. **The criminal negligence standard is clarified.** Valdez gives concrete language for the Sargent test: "a gross departure from the ordinary standard of care" and "conduct that a reasonable person would recognize as creating a high risk of serious harm."

---

DEFENSE STRATEGY UNDER VALDEZ:

1. **Challenge "care or custody"** — establish that the defendant was not actually responsible for the child's care at the relevant moment (another caregiver was present; the defendant was not in a caregiver role)
2. **Challenge criminal negligence** — the defendant's conduct was within the range of ordinary parenting/caregiving mistakes, not a gross departure
3. **Challenge causation** — the injury was not caused by the defendant's conduct (pre-existing condition, accident, third-party cause)
4. **Challenge the willfulness of the underlying act** — the act was accidental or reflexive, not willful

---

APPLICATION TO STEWARD'S CASES:

For case 04-23-01959 (Contra Costa — § 273d allegation):
1. If the prosecution's theory is care-or-custody, the defense must show the conduct did not meet criminal negligence — this is a high prosecution burden.
2. The scope of "care or custody" is broad enough that the defendant's custodial status is unlikely to be a complete defense, but it can be used to challenge the degree of responsibility.
3. The willfulness element can be challenged if the defense narrative includes any element of accident, defensive response, or inadvertent conduct.

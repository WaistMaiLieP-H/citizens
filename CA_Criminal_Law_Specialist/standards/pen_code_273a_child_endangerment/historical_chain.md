# Historical Chain — Pen. Code § 273a (Child Endangerment)

## The wound

Before California enacted modern child protection statutes, children had minimal legal status. Children were treated as property of their parents, and parental discipline was unquestioned. There was no effective mechanism for prosecuting neglect, cruelty, or endangerment short of extreme injury. Deaths of children from neglect were often not prosecuted at all, and when they were, they were prosecuted under general homicide statutes that required proof of specific intent.

The first child protection statutes in California (late 1800s) were narrow — they criminalized only extreme acts such as starving or abandoning a child. Endangerment-style statutes, which reach the creation of risk rather than actual injury, did not exist in their modern form.

The wound was **state acquiescence in parental risk-creation**: the legal system did not intervene when parents placed children in dangerous situations until after the child was actually injured or killed.

## The promise

Over the 20th century, California progressively broadened its child protection statutes:

- Early 1900s: Narrow cruelty and abandonment statutes
- 1960s: Mandated reporter laws (PC § 11165 et seq.) requiring teachers, doctors, and other professionals to report suspected child abuse
- 1961: PC § 273a enacted in substantially its current form, reaching endangerment (not just injury)
- 1970s-1980s: Expansion of child welfare system (Welf. & Inst. Code § 300)
- 1984: PC § 273a(c) probation conditions amendment, mandating child abuser treatment
- 1988: Further expansion of probation conditions
- 2000s-2010s: Enhanced sentencing for serious child abuse; mandatory reporting expansion; cross-references to domestic violence statutes

The promise: **the state will protect children not only from actual injury but from foreseeable risk of injury, even when the parent is the risk-creator**.

## The promise today

Section 273a today is the principal California statute for charging parental (or caregiver) endangerment of children. It operates alongside PC § 273d (corporal injury), Welf. & Inst. Code § 300 (dependency jurisdiction), and PC § 11165 (reporting). A parent who creates risk to a child can be charged criminally under § 273a and subjected to civil dependency under WIC § 300 — both systems operating in parallel.

The tension: because § 273a reaches risk creation, not just actual injury, it is vulnerable to over-charging in ambiguous circumstances. A well-intentioned parent whose conduct was imperfect can be prosecuted as a felony when misdemeanor charges would be more appropriate. The line between (a) and (b) is often the battlefield.

## Relevance to case 04-23-01959

The Hartmann case sits at this tension point. The steward's defense turns on:

1. Whether the circumstances of June 16, 2023 were "likely to produce great bodily harm or death" (the (a) vs. (b) question)
2. Whether the conduct was "willful" (scienter)
3. Whether the underlying factual narrative is accurate (fabrication theory, cross-referencing § 148.5)
4. Whether the custody-dispute context supports a finding that the charging decision was influenced by civil-litigation tactics

The historical arc of § 273a — from narrow cruelty statute to broad endangerment statute — explains why the charge is available, but also why defense arguments about the (a) vs. (b) line and the willfulness element remain viable.

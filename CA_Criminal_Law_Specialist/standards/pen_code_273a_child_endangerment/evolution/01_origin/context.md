# Context — § 273a Origin

## The wound

Child cruelty was documented in 19th-century California courts but was prosecuted under general assault and battery statutes that required showing a completed harmful act. The gap: a parent could subject a child to conditions highly likely to cause death or serious injury — extreme exposure, medical neglect, drug exposure — without any single act that qualified as criminal assault. The child could be slowly dying of neglect and no charge would attach.

California's early child protection legislation (modeling work done in England in the 1880s-1890s by the NSPCC movement) created the endangerment standard: the crime is not just the completed harm but the creation of circumstances likely to cause harm.

## The two-track structure's importance

The "likely to produce great bodily harm or death" vs. "other circumstances" bifurcation is not just a sentencing choice — it is an element the prosecution must prove to the jury. For Case #7 (04-23-01959), the prosecution bears the burden of proving which track applies. The higher track (felony) requires proving the specific circumstances were likely to produce GBH or death — not merely unpleasant or inappropriate. This is a contested factual element.

## The willfulness requirement

People v. Sargent (1999) clarified that § 273a(a) requires the defendant to be aware of the facts that make the conduct dangerous. This is not general-intent negligence — it is elevated beyond ordinary negligence to awareness of the dangerous circumstances. The prosecution must prove the defendant knew the circumstances were likely to produce GBH, not just that a reasonable person would have known.

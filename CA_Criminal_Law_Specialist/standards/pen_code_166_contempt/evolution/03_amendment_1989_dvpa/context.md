# Context — § 166 Amendment: 1989–1990 DVPA Protective Order Enhancements

## The wound that drove this amendment

By 1989, domestic violence advocates had documented a systemic failure: courts regularly issued protective orders under the Family Code and precursor civil procedures, but violations of those orders were prosecuted only as civil contempt — if at all. A batterer who received a protective order could violate it repeatedly; the only sanction was coercive civil contempt (comply-or-be-jailed), not criminal punishment. First-time violations rarely resulted in any consequence. The order was widely perceived as a piece of paper.

The 1989–1990 DVPA amendments answered: violating a domestic violence protective order is a crime.

## What the amendments did — § 166 subd. (c)(1)

Subdivision (c)(1) was added to § 166 to create a specific criminal track for violations of domestic violence protective orders issued under the Domestic Violence Prevention Act (DVPA), then codified in Civil Code and later in Family Code § 6218 et seq. Key structural features:

1. **Mandatory minimum terms.** A first violation of a DVPA order under subd. (c)(1) carried a mandatory minimum jail term (not suspended). This was a deliberate departure from the default misdemeanor framework, which allowed suspended sentences and informal probation.

2. **The willful-and-knowing element.** To be convicted under § 166(c)(1), the defendant must: (a) know of the existence of the protective order, and (b) willfully violate one of its terms. Actual notice of the order is a prerequisite — the prosecution must prove the defendant knew of the order.

3. **Court-conferred notice is sufficient.** Courts began the practice of orally advising defendants of protective order terms in court, with notation in the minute order. This oral-in-court notice satisfies the knowledge element for § 166(c)(1) prosecution.

4. **DVPA order scope.** The orders that trigger § 166(c)(1) protection are those issued under the DVPA (Family Code §§ 6200–6460): emergency protective orders (EPO), temporary restraining orders (TRO), and permanent restraining orders. Also reaches § 136.2 pre-trial protective orders (see § 166(c)(4), added later).

## Legislative context — the 1989 DVPA reform wave

The 1989–1990 amendments were part of a broader California legislative push to address domestic violence as a public safety issue, not merely a private family matter. Key accompanying statutes:

- **Penal Code § 273.5** (corporal injury to spouse) — elevated from misdemeanor to wobbler
- **Penal Code § 13700 et seq.** — mandatory arrest for DV battery
- **Evidence Code § 1109** — DV propensity evidence rule (later additions)
- **DVPA itself (Family Code § 6218)** — comprehensive civil protective order framework

§ 166(c)(1) was the criminal enforcement mechanism that gave all these orders teeth.

## The gap this amendment exposed

The 1989 amendment revealed a practical problem that the 1993 amendment would address: the statutory scheme was strong for first-time violations but had no escalation mechanism. A batterer who was convicted once of § 166(c)(1) faced the same misdemeanor sentence for a second or third violation. Recidivist DV offenders were not deterred. This gap produced the 1993 enhancement (see 04_amendment_1993_dvpa).

## Steward relevance

The 1989–1990 DVPA amendment is the historical foundation for all § 166(c)(1) analysis in the Hartmann matters. Any protective order alleged to have been violated in connection with the June 16, 2023 APD incident, or any family court order at issue in the 16-year orchestration pattern, is analyzed under the framework established in 1989: (1) was there a valid DVPA or § 136.2 order? (2) did the accused have actual notice? (3) was the violation willful?

Importantly: the same 1989 framework applies to the **counter-claim** analysis. Any manipulation of protective orders — including obtaining them by misrepresentation or using them as an instrument of custody strategy — that results in the violation of the accused's court order rights is analyzed under this regime. A protective order obtained by fraud does not lose its criminal enforcement character, but the fraud in obtaining it is the factual predicate for challenging the underlying restraining order.

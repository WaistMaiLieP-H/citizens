# Context — § 166 Amendment: 1993 DVPA Recidivist Enhancement

## The wound that drove this amendment

The 1989 DVPA enforcement amendment criminalized single violations of protective orders — but advocates quickly identified that recidivism was rampant. A batterer who violated a protective order twice or three times still faced the same misdemeanor sentence as a first-time violator. Prosecutors lacked a tool to escalate penalties for documented patterns of order violations. The 1993 amendment answered this gap.

## What the 1993 amendments did — § 166(c)(4) and enhanced recidivist terms

The 1993 amendments made two key structural additions:

### 1. § 136.2 Orders — Criminal Proceeding Protective Orders

Section 166 was amended to expressly cover violations of **§ 136.2** orders — protective orders issued by courts during or after criminal proceedings to protect victims, witnesses, or complaining witnesses. Pre-1993, the statute's protective order coverage was limited to DVPA civil orders. The 1993 amendment recognized that criminal courts routinely issue their own protective orders during pending criminal cases, and those orders needed the same criminal enforcement mechanism.

**§ 136.2 order context:** When a person is arrested or charged with a crime involving a victim, the court may (and under some circumstances must) issue a § 136.2 order prohibiting the defendant from contacting the victim. These orders are pre-conviction, during-prosecution orders. Violations of § 136.2 orders became § 166(c)(4) criminal offenses.

**Why this matters for steward cases:** In a criminal proceeding, any § 136.2 protective order is a direct enforcement mechanism. Violations of the § 136.2 order — including contact with the alleged victim — expose the defendant to § 166(c)(4) prosecution. Conversely, if a § 136.2 order is manipulated or obtained without probable cause basis, that is a separate due process and Brady concern.

### 2. Recidivist misdemeanor enhancement — subd. (c)(1)/(c)(3)

The 1993 amendments added an escalation track for recidivist DVPA protective order violations:

- **Second violation within 7 years:** Still a misdemeanor, but with enhanced mandatory minimum jail terms. The court cannot suspend the minimum term.

- **Felony wobbler provision (subd. (c)(3)):** A violation of a DVPA protective order is chargeable as a felony when: (a) the defendant has been previously convicted of two or more prior DV offenses (enumerated list), AND (b) the current violation involves actual violence or a credible threat of violence. The felony wobbler provision creates the potential for state prison sentences for recidivist DV protective order violators.

## The broader 1993 DV legislative wave

1993 was a major year for California DV enforcement legislation. The amendments were part of:

- The Domestic Violence Protection Act expansion
- Enhancements to § 273.5 (corporal injury) — adding escalating penalties for repeat offenders
- Expansion of the Evidence Code § 1109 propensity rule to cover additional prior DV offenses
- DV training requirements for law enforcement

The 1993 § 166 amendments reflected the Legislature's conclusion that single-incident sanctions were not deterring repeat DV offenders and that an escalating criminal track was necessary.

## The relationship between § 166(c)(1), (c)(3), and (c)(4)

By the time of the 1993 amendments, § 166's DV enforcement structure had three tiers:

| Subd. | Trigger | Level | Sanction |
|---|---|---|---|
| (c)(1) | First DVPA order violation | Misdemeanor | Mandatory minimum jail |
| (c)(1)/(c)(3) | Second or subsequent DVPA violation, with prior DV history and violence | Wobbler | Misdemeanor enhanced / felony |
| (c)(4) | § 136.2 criminal proceeding order violation | Misdemeanor | Same as (c)(1) framework |

## Steward relevance — both directions

**For the accusation track (case 04-23-01959):**
Any § 136.2 order issued in the criminal proceedings — whether a no-contact order between the accused and the alleged victim, or a protective order running in either direction — is governed by § 166(c)(4). The accused cannot contact the protected person, and the protected person cannot use § 136.2 protections as cover for further coordination or manipulation.

**For the counter-claim track:**
If opposing parties in the Hartmann matters have violated restraining orders, protective orders, or custody orders, the recidivist enhancement framework is relevant. A pattern of willful violations of court orders — multiple instances documented — raises the possibility of elevated misdemeanor charges or, with prior DV history, the wobbler to felony.

**For the CLETS violations analysis:**
CLETS entries (criminal history, protective order registration) related to any § 166 convictions must be accurate and properly updated. Erroneous CLETS entries arising from improperly obtained or incorrectly recorded § 166 charges are a related Brady/discovery concern.

# Context — § 166 Origin

## The wound

In 19th-century California courts, there was no systematic mechanism to enforce court orders through criminal sanction. A party could violate a court order and face only civil contempt (fines, coercive incarceration until compliance). The 1872 codification created criminal contempt as a distinct misdemeanor offense — punishable by fine or imprisonment as punishment, not merely as coercion.

## The modern expansion (court orders)

The critical modern evolution of § 166 is its application to DVPA protective orders and § 136.2 orders. The willful-and-knowing element — the violator must know of the court order and intentionally violate it — is the central contested element. Notice of the order is required; oral notice in court is sufficient.

## Steward relevance — both directions

§ 166 is relevant in Case #7 in two directions:
1. **Prosecution theory**: if any court order exists restraining the steward, alleged violation is § 166
2. **Counter-claim theory**: violations of custody orders, restraining orders, and § 136.2 orders by opposing parties are § 166 violations — creating criminal exposure for the orchestrators of the family law pattern

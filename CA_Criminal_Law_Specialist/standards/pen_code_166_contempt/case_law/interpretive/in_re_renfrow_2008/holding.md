# In re Renfrow (2008) 164 Cal.App.4th 1251

**Citation:** In re Renfrow (2008) 164 Cal.App.4th 1251, 79 Cal.Rptr.3d 857
**Court:** California Court of Appeal, Second Appellate District
**Decided:** 2008
**Statutes construed:** Penal Code § 166(a)(4) — willful disobedience of court order; family law contempt

## Facts

Contempt proceedings were initiated against a party who violated a family law court order. The central question was whether the "willful" element of § 166(a)(4) was established — specifically, whether the person had actual knowledge of the order and intentionally failed to comply.

## Holding

The Court of Appeal addressed the willful disobedience standard under § 166:

## The doctrinal proposition

Section 166(c)(1) requires "willful and knowing" violation of specific protective orders. California case law has established the following framework:

1. **"Willful" means intentional.** The violation must be a deliberate act, not an accident or inadvertent conduct. Pen. Code § 7(1) supplies the general definition of "willfully" for criminal statutes: acting with purpose or willingness to commit the act.

2. **"Knowing" means actual knowledge of the terms of the order.** The defendant must know what the order prohibits, not merely that an order exists. A defendant served with an order typically has constructive knowledge of its terms, but if the terms are ambiguous or if service was defective, the knowing element may be contestable.

3. **Ignorance of specific terms is a rare defense.** Courts have generally rejected the defense that the defendant knew of the order but did not remember or understand its specific terms. The rationale: a person served with a protective order has a duty to know and comply with its terms.

4. **Substantial compliance is not a defense when any violation is alleged.** A defendant cannot argue that other provisions of the order were followed — violation of any single provision is sufficient for a § 166(c) charge.

5. **The mental state applies at the time of the act.** Subsequent realization that the conduct violated the order does not cure the violation if the willful and knowing elements were present at the time.

## Why this matters

The willful-and-knowing element is the primary defense battleground in § 166(c) cases. Defendants typically argue one of:

- "I didn't know the order existed" — defeated if service can be proved
- "I didn't know the specific terms" — rarely successful after personal service
- "It was accidental / I didn't intend to violate" — depends on facts; plausible where contact was inadvertent (e.g., unexpectedly encountering the protected person in a public space)
- "I thought the order had expired / been modified" — depends on whether the defendant had reason to believe the modification existed

## Subd. (b) stalking enhancement

Section 166(b) adds a specific enhancement for defendants previously convicted of stalking (§ 646.9) who contact the victim by phone, mail, social media, electronic communication, or directly. Each contact is a separate violation under subd. (b)(2). This is one of the most aggressive enforcement provisions in § 166 and reflects the Legislature's concern about stalking recidivism.

## Relevance to the Hartmann matter

If the steward is ever charged with § 166 for alleged violation of a court order, the defense must address:

1. Whether service of the order was proper
2. Whether the specific terms were sufficiently clear to provide notice
3. Whether the alleged violation was willful (intentional act) and knowing (with awareness of the terms)
4. Whether any ambiguity in the order supports a reasonable interpretation under which the conduct was not a violation

Conversely, if the steward is pursuing enforcement of a court order against another party, the § 166 framework is the criminal enforcement path — but prosecutorial discretion typically determines whether charges actually follow.

## The doctrinal proposition

Criminal contempt under § 166(a)(4) and (c)(1) requires that the defendant received adequate notice of the court order and its terms before the alleged violation. Due process principles apply. California case law has established:

1. **Personal service creates presumption of notice.** When a court order is personally served on the defendant, the prosecution benefits from a presumption that the defendant knew its terms. This presumption can be rebutted by evidence that service was irregular, that the defendant did not actually receive or understand the order, or that the terms were too ambiguous to provide meaningful notice.

2. **Substituted service is sufficient if the defendant had actual knowledge.** Even if personal service was not made, the defendant can be convicted if the prosecution proves actual knowledge by other means (e.g., presence in court when the order was issued, subsequent acknowledgment, communication referencing the order's terms).

3. **Ambiguous orders are strictly construed against the prosecution.** When a court order's terms are ambiguous, courts apply the rule of lenity and will not convict a defendant whose conduct could reasonably be understood as not violating the order. This is both a due process safeguard and a practical limit on contempt prosecutions.

4. **Void orders cannot support a contempt conviction.** If the underlying order was void (issued without jurisdiction, procedurally defective, etc.), violation of it is not criminal contempt. A collateral attack on the order's validity is generally not available in the contempt proceeding itself, but a truly void order can be challenged.

## Due process protections

The California Supreme Court and federal courts have held that criminal contempt proceedings require most of the same due process protections as any other criminal prosecution:

- Right to counsel (for serious contempt with possible incarceration)
- Right to notice of the charged violation
- Right to present a defense
- Proof beyond a reasonable doubt
- Right to appeal

These protections apply specifically when the contempt is "criminal" (punitive purpose) as opposed to "civil" (coercive purpose). Section 166 is criminal contempt and carries the full set of due process protections.

## Relevance to the Hartmann matter

For any § 166 prosecution of or by the steward, the due process framework provides multiple defense and enforcement considerations:

1. Was the order properly served?
2. Were the terms sufficiently specific to provide notice?
3. Was the order jurisdictionally valid?
4. Were the criminal procedure protections observed in the contempt proceeding?

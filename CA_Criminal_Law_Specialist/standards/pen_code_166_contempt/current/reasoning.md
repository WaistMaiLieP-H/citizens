# Reasoning — Cal. Penal Code § 166 (Criminal Contempt of Court)

**Standard ID:** PEN_CODE_166_CONTEMPT
**Layer:** 2 — Reasoning
**Status:** ACTIVE
**Filed:** 2026-04-20

---

## Why This Standard Exists

Courts cannot function without the power to enforce their own orders. A protective order that cannot be enforced against a violator provides no actual protection. A court order that defendants can ignore without consequence is a suggestion, not a law. Section 166 creates the criminal enforcement mechanism for court orders — converting willful disobedience of judicial mandates into a separately chargeable offense.

The criminal enforcement function is distinct from civil contempt (which coerces future compliance) and from probation revocation (which addresses violation of probation conditions). Section 166 creates independent criminal liability for the act of violating a court order, regardless of whether the violator is on probation, regardless of the civil contempt remedy, and regardless of whether any victim was harmed.

---

## Why Protective Order Violations Have Enhanced Penalties

The § 166(c) enhanced penalties for violations of protective orders reflect a legislative determination that protective orders are not administrative documents — they are court-ordered protections for identified victims who have already been harmed or threatened. The Legislature treated knowing violation of a protective order as a qualitatively more serious contempt than general court disorder, warranting mandatory minimum jail time when physical injury results.

The mandatory 48-hour minimum for violations causing physical injury cannot be waived based on the defendant's circumstances or the court's sympathy — it reflects a floor below which the Legislature would not permit courts to go. The Legislature's message: if you violate a protective order and injure the protected person, you are going to jail.

The second-conviction enhancement (state prison exposure for a second violation within 7 years involving violence or credible threats) tracks research on domestic violence recidivism: a second conviction within 7 years is a strong predictor of continued pattern violence, and the Legislature escalated the consequence accordingly.

---

## The Willfulness Element — Why It Matters

The willfulness element in § 166(a)(4) (willful disobedience of a court order) requires that the defendant knew of the order and deliberately violated it. Accidental violations — violating a geographic stay-away order because the defendant did not know the protected person's route home — are not § 166 contempts. Courts assess:
- Whether the defendant was properly served with the order and appeared at the hearing;
- Whether the terms of the order were clearly communicated;
- Whether the defendant had a reasonable basis for believing they were in compliance.

---

## Criminal Contempt vs. Civil Contempt in Family Law

In family law, courts exercise both civil contempt (CCP § 1218 — coercive, imposes jail until compliance with a support order) and criminal contempt (§ 166 — punitive, punishes past violation). Family law attorneys frequently seek § 166 criminal contempt charges through the DA's office rather than pursuing civil contempt in family court — especially when the protective order violations involve physical violence or repeated contact violations that suggest the civil contempt remedy is insufficient to protect the victim.

---

## Application in Practice

Section 166 is the foundational enforcement mechanism for:
- Domestic violence protective orders (DVRO, EPO, stay-away orders);
- Civil harassment restraining orders;
- Elder abuse protective orders;
- No-contact conditions of probation or pretrial release;
- Gang injunctions;
- Family court orders (custody, visitation, support).

Key practice point: a § 166 criminal contempt charge from a single text message violating a no-contact order can result in up to one year in county jail — even if the message caused no harm. The violation itself is the crime.

# Pen. Code § 166 — Criminal Contempt of Court

**Popular name:** Criminal contempt; violation of court order
**Location:** Penal Code, Part 1, Title 7, Chapter 7 (Other Offenses Against Public Justice)

## What § 166 does

Section 166 criminalizes nine distinct categories of contempt of court (subd. (a)) and adds enhanced penalties for specific categories (subds. (b)-(e)). Criminal contempt under § 166 is distinct from civil contempt under Code of Civil Procedure §§ 1209 et seq. — criminal contempt is a separate offense prosecuted by the state; civil contempt is a family-court or civil-court remedy initiated by a party.

## The most commonly charged subdivision: (a)(4) — willful disobedience of a court order

Subd. (a)(4) is the workhorse. It criminalizes willful disobedience of "the terms, as written, of a process or court order or out-of-state court order, lawfully issued by a court, including orders pending trial." This covers:

- Violations of domestic violence restraining orders
- Violations of civil harassment restraining orders
- Violations of criminal court protective orders
- Violations of family court custody orders
- Violations of civil court injunctions
- Violations of pretrial release conditions
- Violations of probation terms (though probation violations are typically handled through probation proceedings, not § 166)

## Subd. (c) — enhanced penalty for specific protective order violations

Subd. (c) imposes enhanced penalties (county jail up to 1 year, fine up to $1,000) for knowing violations of specific order categories:
- Pen. Code § 136.2 (criminal protective orders)
- Pen. Code § 1203.097(a)(2) (DV probation orders)
- Elder/dependent adult abuse protective orders
- Pen. Code § 1201.3 (sex offense victim orders)
- Pen. Code § 273.5(j) (DV probation orders)
- Family Code §§ 6320 and 6389 (DVPA orders)
- Exclusion from family dwelling orders
- Conduct-enjoining orders supporting the above

Mandatory minimum 48 hours jail if the violation resulted in physical injury. Second offense within 7 years with violence or credible threat: county jail up to 1 year OR state prison 16 months / 2 / 3 years.

## Scienter: "willful and knowing"

Subd. (c)'s "willful and knowing" standard requires both:
- **Willful** — intentional act (not accidental)
- **Knowing** — actual knowledge of the terms of the order (not merely its existence)

This scienter requirement is a common defense issue. A defendant who claims they did not know the specific terms of the order (e.g., the exact distance of the stay-away provision) may have a defense, but courts generally reject this when the order was personally served and the defendant acknowledged receipt.

## The wound and promise

Before dedicated criminal contempt statutes, contempt was prosecuted under common-law doctrines inherited from English practice, which were inconsistent and subject to broad judicial discretion. Section 166 codifies specific categories with specific penalties, giving both prosecutors and defendants clarity about what conduct is criminal and what punishment applies.

The enhanced penalties in subd. (c) reflect a specific policy concern: protective orders only work if they are enforced, and protective orders that protect DV victims, elder abuse victims, and crime victims are the most critical. Section 166(c) reflects the Legislature's judgment that these orders deserve stronger criminal enforcement than ordinary civil contempt.

## Relevance to the Hartmann matter

Section 166 is relevant to the Hartmann matter in both directions:

1. **Defensive:** If any party has claimed the steward violated a court order, the § 166 elements must be addressed — specifically the "willful and knowing" scienter requirement.

2. **Offensive:** If other parties have violated court orders in the Hartmann matters (e.g., custody orders, visitation orders, protective orders), § 166 is the framework for criminal enforcement.

3. **CLETS violations:** Per steward memory, prior CLETS violations have been documented. If those violations involved knowingly false entries in the CLETS database, that could implicate § 166 if a court order required accurate reporting, and separately implicates § 148.5 and § 118.1.

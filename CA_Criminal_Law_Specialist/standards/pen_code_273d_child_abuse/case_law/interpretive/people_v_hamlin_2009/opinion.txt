PEOPLE v. HAMLIN
170 Cal.App.4th 1412 (2009)
California Court of Appeal, Third Appellate District
Decided: 2009

---

FACTS:

Hamlin was charged with child abuse under § 273d after physically disciplining his child in a manner that left visible marks. The defense argued the injury did not constitute a "traumatic condition" as required by the statute. The trial court instructed the jury on the traumatic condition element. Hamlin appealed, arguing the instruction was erroneous and the evidence insufficient to support a § 273d conviction.

---

HOLDING:

§ 273d "TRAUMATIC CONDITION" ELEMENT:

1. **"Traumatic condition" is defined by physical force, not severity.** A "traumatic condition" under § 273d means any wound or bodily injury — whether minor or serious — caused by the direct application of physical force. The statutory term "traumatic" is used in its clinical/physical sense (injury from external force), not in the popular sense (emotionally upsetting).

2. **Minor injuries suffice.** Redness, bruising, welts, and abrasions each constitute a traumatic condition for purposes of § 273d. The statute does not require serious injury, great bodily injury, or injury requiring medical treatment.

3. **Photographic evidence is sufficient.** Photographs of visible marks on the child's body, even without expert medical testimony, are sufficient to establish the traumatic condition element. The jury may draw reasonable inferences from physical evidence.

4. **The "cruel or inhuman" qualifier applies to the punishment track only, not the injury track.** Section 273d contains two alternative paths: (A) cruel or inhuman corporal punishment, or (B) any injury resulting in a traumatic condition. For path (B), the prosecution need not show the discipline was cruel or inhuman — the existence of a traumatic condition alone establishes the element.

---

WHY HAMLIN IS LOAD-BEARING:

Hamlin clarifies the prosecutorial route: even where a defendant characterizes their conduct as discipline, if the child sustains any physical injury (bruising, redness, welt), the traumatic condition element is satisfied without requiring proof that the punishment itself was "cruel or inhuman." This makes § 273d broader than its text might suggest.

For defense purposes:
- Contesting the traumatic condition element requires showing NO physical injury existed, not merely that the discipline was reasonable
- The parental discipline defense is essentially limited to the punishment track (cruel/inhuman analysis), not the injury track
- The traumatic condition element is the threshold question in every § 273d defense

---

APPLICATION TO STEWARD'S CASES:

For case 04-23-01959 (Contra Costa — § 273d allegation): if photographs or medical records are offered to show injury to the child, Hamlin will be cited by the prosecution to establish the traumatic condition element.

Defense must:
1. **Challenge the authenticity and chain of custody of photographic evidence** — were photographs taken contemporaneously with the alleged event?
2. **Identify alternative explanations for any visible marks** — pre-existing conditions, self-inflicted marks, marks from other causes
3. **Confirm whether the charged theory is the punishment track or injury track** — if the injury track, the parental discipline defense is unavailable; if the punishment track, the "cruel or inhuman" element is the battleground
4. **Challenge the forensic interview** — in a false accusation case, no injury may have existed; the traumatic condition element may fail for want of evidence if the photographic record was manufactured or misattributed

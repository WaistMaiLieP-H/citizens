## The doctrinal proposition

California courts have recognized that in § 273d cases arising from custody disputes or family conflict, the false accusation defense is the most common and often the most powerful defense available. The doctrinal framework:

1. **False reports are a known problem in custody disputes.** Courts and practitioners recognize that child abuse allegations sometimes arise in the context of custody disputes and may be motivated by the desire to obtain a tactical advantage in family court proceedings. The criminal defense may introduce evidence of the family law context to show motive for fabrication.

2. **Willfulness is a required element.** Section 273d requires that the defendant "willfully" inflict the punishment or injury. Where the entire premise of the charge is a false accusation — i.e., the defendant did not inflict any injury at all — willfulness is not the contested element; the existence of the act is the contested fact.

3. **The source of the accusation is always relevant.** Defense may introduce evidence showing who made the initial report, the reporter's relationship to the defendant and the child, the reporter's motive, and whether the report was coordinated with a family law strategy. Mandatory reporter violations (reporting without reasonable suspicion) can also be raised.

4. **Child witness suggestibility doctrine.** In cases where the primary evidence is a child's statement, the defense may challenge the statement on suggestibility grounds. California courts recognize that children can be led to make inaccurate accusations through suggestive interviewing techniques, coaching, or contamination by adults with a stake in the outcome (see e.g., *In re Amber B.* (1987) 191 Cal.App.3d 682 — recognizing suggestibility of child witnesses in abuse cases).

5. **Expert testimony on the dynamics of false accusation.** California courts permit expert testimony on the dynamics of false accusation in child abuse cases, the reliability of child statements, and the effects of suggestive interviewing — subject to Evidence Code § 801 (expert opinion) and § 352 (undue prejudice) balancing.

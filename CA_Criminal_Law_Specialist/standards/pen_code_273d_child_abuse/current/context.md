# Pen. Code § 273d — Child Abuse (Corporal Punishment): Context and Elements

## What this statute does

Section 273d makes it a felony to willfully inflict cruel or inhuman corporal punishment — or any injury causing a traumatic condition — on a child. This is the charged offense typically labeled "child abuse" or "child corporal injury" in Contra Costa and other California counties.

Section 273d differs critically from § 273a (child endangerment):
- **§ 273a** — focuses on risk of harm; covers situations where the child was placed in danger but not necessarily injured
- **§ 273d** — requires actual infliction of punishment or injury resulting in a traumatic condition; covers completed harm

## Elements (CALCRIM No. 821)

The prosecution must prove all four elements beyond a reasonable doubt:

1. **Willfully inflicted** — The defendant acted with purpose; the act was not accidental. "Willfully" under Pen. Code § 7(1) means the defendant intended to do the act, not necessarily that the defendant intended the specific injury.

2. **Cruel or inhuman corporal punishment, OR injury resulting in a traumatic condition** — Two alternative paths:
   - *Punishment path:* The corporal punishment was cruel or inhuman in nature (exceeds reasonable parental discipline)
   - *Injury path:* Any physical force that causes a "traumatic condition" regardless of the subjective characterization of the act as discipline

3. **Traumatic condition** — A wound or other bodily injury, whether minor or serious, caused by the direct application of physical force. "Traumatic condition" does not require serious injury; any visible physical injury qualifies. The statute uses "traumatic" in its clinical sense (injury caused by external force), not in the lay sense of "emotionally traumatic."

4. **On a child** — A person under age 18 at the time of the offense.

## Key element: "traumatic condition"

The traumatic condition element is the prosecution's primary hook in § 273d cases. Courts have consistently held:
- **Any wound counts.** Scratches, bruises, welts, redness, and abrasions are sufficient.
- **No serious injury required.** Section 273d is a general felony child abuse statute; it does not require the "great bodily injury" needed for enhancements.
- **Medical documentation is not required.** The jury may infer a traumatic condition from testimony, photographs, or other evidence.

Contrast with the § 243(d) misdemeanor battery-on-child framework: § 273d is the felony vehicle when the prosecution can prove a traumatic condition; § 242 battery is typically a lesser included.

## Sentencing

- **Felony (primary offense):** 2, 4, or 6 years state prison, OR up to 1 year county jail (wobbler for sentencing; not a wobbler for charging — must be charged as a felony).
- **Fine:** Up to $6,000, or both fine and imprisonment.
- **Prior conviction enhancement:** If the defendant has a prior § 273d conviction, a 4-year enhancement applies (subd. (b)).

## Probation conditions (mandatory if probation granted)

If probation is granted (typically for first offenses without great bodily injury):
1. 36-month minimum probation period
2. Criminal protective order for child victim
3. One year of a § 273.1-compliant child abuser's treatment program (must start immediately; quarterly progress reports to court)
4. Alcohol/drug abstinence and random testing if offense was substance-related
5. Court may waive conditions only for good cause stated on the record

## Defense framework

**1. False accusation / fabricated charge**
The most common defense in contested § 273d cases involving custody disputes or family conflict. Defense must attack:
- Source of the report (who made the report? What is their motive?)
- Child's statements (when and how obtained? Leading questions? Contamination?)
- Physical evidence (were injuries consistent with accidental cause? Alternative explanation?)

**2. Parental discipline defense (Pen. Code § 273d, common law)**
California recognizes a limited "parental discipline" exception: a parent may use reasonable physical discipline that is not cruel or inhuman. The defense requires showing:
- The discipline was proportionate to the child's behavior
- The punishment did not exceed what reasonable parents would consider appropriate
- The resulting injury (if any) was not the intended goal but an unintended consequence
Note: This defense is increasingly narrow. Courts look at the totality of the force used and the severity of the injury.

**3. Accident**
If the injury was accidental and the defendant did not willfully inflict it, the willfulness element fails.

**4. Insufficient evidence / traumatic condition not proven**
Attack the medical evidence and witness testimony. If no traumatic condition is proven (no wound or physical injury), the § 273d charge fails (though § 273a may remain).

## Relationship to other statutes

| Statute | Key difference |
|---|---|
| § 273a | Risk of harm; no injury required; wobbler (serious injury = felony) |
| § 273d | Actual infliction + traumatic condition; always charged as felony |
| § 242 | Simple battery; no injury element; misdemeanor |
| § 243(d) | Battery with serious bodily injury; more serious injury threshold |
| § 368 | Elder/dependent adult abuse (§ 273d parallel for adults) |

## Relevance to case #7 (04-23-01959)

Criminal 04-23-01959 is described as "Child Abuse (Contra Costa, linked to 6/16/2023 setup." The § 273d framework is the primary standard for this case. The defense analysis should:

1. **Reconstruct the 6/16/2023 event chain** — the timeline reconstruction memo documents pre-positioning by Christina, direction by APD, and a 20-minute arrival. The setup context goes to the fabrication defense.
2. **Identify the charging document** — determine whether the charge is § 273d, § 273a, or both.
3. **Obtain the CPS/DCFS report** — the originating referral document is essential for identifying the reporter and the basis of the allegation.
4. **Challenge the traumatic condition evidence** — if any injury is documented, identify alternative explanations (prior accident, self-infliction, fabrication).
5. **Attack the child's statements** — obtain the forensic interview recordings and transcripts; challenge for suggestibility, leading questions, and interviewer contamination.

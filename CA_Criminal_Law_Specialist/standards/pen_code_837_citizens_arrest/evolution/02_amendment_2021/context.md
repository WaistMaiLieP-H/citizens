# Context — § 837 AB 1775 Amendment (2021)

## The wound

On February 23, 2020, Ahmaud Arbery — an unarmed 25-year-old Black man — was shot and killed while jogging near Brunswick, Georgia. Three men pursued him in pickup trucks, claimed to be making a citizen's arrest for a burglary they had not witnessed, and shot him when he attempted to escape. Under the pre-2021 version of California Penal Code § 837, the same conduct would have been legally defensible in California: any private person could arrest for a felony "which has been in fact committed" based only on reasonable cause, without any requirement that the arresting person witnessed the crime, was the victim, or had any direct connection to it.

The Legislature recognized that California's citizen's arrest statute, unchanged since 1872, had become a legal architecture for racial vigilantism. The statute's breadth — originally designed for frontier conditions where peace officers were unavailable — had no limiting principle to prevent pretextual "citizen's arrests" targeting people by race.

## What AB 1775 fixed

The pre-2021 statute allowed arrest based on reasonable cause alone — no nexus between the arresting person and the crime was required. A neighbor, a bystander, a stranger could pursue and detain based on suspicion, with the law's blessing.

AB 1775 imposed three requirements that must be satisfied independently:

**Presence requirement** (§ 837(2)(a)): For felony arrests based on completed or attempted crime, the arresting person must have been present to witness the felony or attempt. This closes the "heard about it" or "suspected it happened" basis for arrest.

**Victim/aftermath nexus** (§ 837(2)(b)): If the arresting person was not present for the crime itself, they may still arrest — but only if they are the immediate victim OR personally witnessed the immediate aftermath. "Immediate aftermath" limits the temporal and spatial scope: not hours later, not based on description from others.

**Off-duty peace officer carve-out** (§ 837(2)(c)): Off-duty officers acting under their official authority retain broader arrest power — recognizing that professional training and accountability distinguish their conduct from civilian vigilantism.

## § 837.1 — New explicit prohibition

The Legislature also added § 837.1, which codifies what the Equal Protection Clause already required but made explicit: no detention under the citizen's arrest chapter may be based on race, ethnicity, or national origin. The significance is evidentiary and remedial — an explicit statutory hook for civil liability when a citizen's arrest is pretextually racial.

## Force codification

Prior common-law limitations on force in citizen's arrests were codified: reasonable force only; lethal force only in genuine self-defense (not as a method of preventing escape). This directly addresses the Arbery pattern, where lethal force was used to prevent a jogger from running away.

## Steward relevance

§ 837 and § 837.1 are relevant to M25-00758 (criminal threat charge) where the June 16, 2023 confrontation involved civilian-directed law enforcement response. The amended statute's nexus requirements — the arresting/detaining person must be the victim or immediate witness — are potentially applicable to any party who directed APD response without direct personal witnessing of the alleged crime. § 837.1's racial-basis prohibition is also a cross-reference point for § 1983 analysis.

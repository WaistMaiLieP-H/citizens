# Reasoning — Cal. Penal Code § 837 (Citizen's Arrest)

**Standard ID:** PEN_CODE_837_CITIZENS_ARREST
**Layer:** 2 — Reasoning
**Status:** ACTIVE
**Filed:** 2026-04-20

---

## Why This Standard Exists

Section 837 codifies the common law right of private arrest — the authority of a private person to detain another for a public offense. The authority predates professional law enforcement in California (enacted 1872) and reflects a historical period in which police were scarce, response times were long, and communities depended on mutual enforcement.

In modern practice, § 837 serves three residual functions:
1. **Merchant detention:** Retail businesses detain suspected shoplifters under the merchant privilege (§ 490.5), which operates as a § 837 citizen's arrest authority for misdemeanor theft;
2. **Security guard operations:** Private security personnel detain persons for crimes committed in their presence;
3. **Emergency situations:** Private citizens who witness serious crimes may need to detain the perpetrator before law enforcement arrives.

---

## Why Ground 2 Requires the Felony "In Fact" Occurred

The second ground for citizen's arrest — that the person "has committed a felony" — requires the felony to have actually occurred, not merely to be believed to have occurred by the arrestor. This is a strict liability standard that creates significant risk for private citizens who make arrests based on mistakes.

The policy rationale: private persons, unlike trained police officers, lack the legal authority, training, and accountability structures that justify arrest on reasonable suspicion alone. Allowing citizen's arrests on mere belief that a felony occurred would enable vigilantism — private enforcement of perceived wrongs that may not actually be crimes. The "in fact" requirement limits citizen's arrest authority to situations where the underlying crime is objectively established.

---

## Why This Creates High Personal Liability Risk

The strict standards of § 837 make citizen's arrest one of the highest-risk activities a private citizen can undertake. If the arrestor is wrong — if no crime occurred, or if the person arrested is not the perpetrator — the arrestor has committed false imprisonment (§ 236), potentially battery (§ 242), and potentially kidnapping (§ 207) if the person was moved. Civil liability for wrongful arrest follows automatically.

This risk structure is intentional: it reflects the Legislature's preference for law enforcement professionals to make arrests rather than private citizens.

---

## Why Immediate Delivery to Law Enforcement Is Required

Section 847 (companion to § 837) requires that after making a citizen's arrest, the private person deliver the arrested individual to a peace officer without unnecessary delay. Prolonged private detention after a citizen's arrest — holding someone in a back room, driving them to a location for questioning — converts a lawful citizen's arrest into unlawful false imprisonment.

The immediate delivery requirement reflects the purpose of citizen's arrest: to bridge the gap between the commission of a crime and the arrival of law enforcement. It is not a mechanism for private citizens to conduct investigations, extract confessions, or administer private justice.

---

## Application in Practice

Section 837 most commonly arises in:
- Retail theft and shopkeeper privilege cases;
- Security guard detention disputes (whether security acted within § 837 authority);
- Cases where a private citizen detained someone based on a mistaken belief that they committed a crime;
- High-profile cases involving armed private individuals detaining other citizens.

The post-*Arbery* national debate about citizen's arrest laws led California to clarify its standards, reinforcing that the "in fact" requirement means citizen's arrests based on mistaken identity or on non-crimes (jaywalking, moving violations) are unlawful regardless of the arrestor's belief.

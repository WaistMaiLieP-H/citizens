# Context — Cal. Pen. Code § 837 Citizen's Arrest

## The wound and the promise

**The wound:** Without a citizen's arrest right, private persons who witness a crime in progress or have credible knowledge of a committed felony would be powerless to act until law enforcement arrives. In emergencies, that delay can mean the suspect escapes, evidence is destroyed, or victims are further harmed.

**The promise:** § 837 promises that a private person who acts within its three specified grounds can lawfully arrest another — and be protected from false imprisonment liability for doing so. The reciprocal promise to the arrested person: outside those three grounds, any arrest by a private person is unlawful and subjects the arrestor to criminal and civil liability.

## The three grounds — when each applies

### Ground 1: Public offense in presence (misdemeanor AND felony)

This is the only ground that covers misdemeanors. Requirements:
- The arrestor must have personally witnessed the offense being committed or attempted
- "In presence" means within the arrestor's own sensory range — sight, sound, smell; constructive presence is insufficient
- The offense must have actually occurred (not just suspected)

**In the DV context:** A private person who witnesses a battery cannot arrest the batterer based on what they were told — they must have personally seen or heard the conduct. If a third party calls a family member and says "I'm being beaten," and the family member rushes over and arrests the alleged batterer without witnessing anything themselves, Ground 1 is not satisfied.

### Ground 2: Felony actually committed (not in presence)

- The felony must have actually been committed by the person being arrested
- There is no "reasonable belief" accommodation under this ground — if the felony was not in fact committed, the arrest is invalid no matter how sincere the arrestor's belief
- This ground does NOT cover misdemeanors

### Ground 3: Felony committed + reasonable cause

- The felony must have actually been committed (by someone)
- The arrestor must have reasonable cause to believe this particular person committed it
- "Reasonable cause" under § 837 is analyzed similarly to "probable cause" for peace officer arrests — an objective standard based on facts known to the arrestor at the time
- Ground 3 does NOT cover misdemeanors

## The invalidity trap — Ground 2 is especially dangerous

Ground 2 has a built-in trap: if the arrestor sincerely but incorrectly believes the person committed a felony, and the felony was not in fact committed, the arrest is unlawful even though the arrestor acted in good faith. Good faith is not a defense to false imprisonment resulting from an invalid citizen's arrest.

This means:
- If Person A arrests Person B believing B committed a felony robbery, and it turns out no robbery occurred (e.g., it was a misunderstanding), Person A has committed false imprisonment of Person B — even if A genuinely believed otherwise.

## The coordination abuse pattern — § 837 as a weapon

California's citizen's arrest statute has been abused in a documented pattern where orchestrated false accusations are used to induce a private person to make a citizen's arrest — or to trigger police response that results in an arrest — when no actual offense occurred. The elements of this pattern:

1. **False representation of offense:** The instigator falsely represents that the target committed a public offense (Ground 1) or a felony (Grounds 2/3)
2. **Induced arrest:** The instigated party (or police responding to the call) makes an arrest based on the false representation
3. **Consequence:** The target is arrested, detained, booked, and subjected to the criminal justice system — even if charges are later dropped

This pattern involves:
- § 148.5 (filing a false police report) if the false accusation was made to police
- § 182(a)(2) (conspiracy to procure false arrest) if two or more people coordinated the false accusation
- § 236 false imprisonment liability for any private person who made the arrest based on the false accusation

**In the Hartmann matter:** The June 16, 2023 incident (case 04-23-01959) involves alleged orchestration in which APD was directed to respond based on a pre-positioned false accusation. The § 837 framework is relevant both to analyze the lawfulness of any arrest and to understand the false arrest / false imprisonment claim against the instigators.

## Bilateral analysis

**As complainant (Michael — asserting false arrest):**
- If the June 16, 2023 arrest was made on a false accusation with no actual public offense committed in anyone's presence, the arrest was unlawful
- The arresting party (peace officer) may have had colorable probable cause from the false accusation — a peace officer's arrest may be harder to challenge on false imprisonment grounds than a private person's arrest
- BUT: if the accusation that generated the police response was itself false, the persons who made or coordinated the false accusation are liable for § 182(a)(2) conspiracy to procure false arrest, regardless of whether the arrest was by an officer or a private person

**As respondent (if Michael is accused of making an improper citizen's arrest):**
- Under Ground 1, Michael must have personally witnessed the offense
- Under Ground 3, a felony must have actually been committed and Michael must have had reasonable cause
- The invalidity defense: if the "victim" of Michael's citizen's arrest alleges false imprisonment, the defense is that one of the three § 837 grounds was satisfied

## Common errors in citizen's arrest analysis

| Error | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Arresting for misdemeanor not witnessed in person | Unlawful arrest — false imprisonment |
| Ground 2 used when felony may not have occurred | Unlawful arrest — good faith no defense |
| Delay in delivering arrestee to peace officer | § 847 violation; arrestor's authority ceases |
| Using force beyond what is reasonable | Assault/battery exposure in addition to false imprisonment |

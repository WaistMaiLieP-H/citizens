# People v. Hagedorn (2005) 127 Cal.App.4th 734

**Citation:** People v. Hagedorn (2005) 127 Cal.App.4th 734, 25 Cal.Rptr.3d 879
**Court:** California Court of Appeal, Fourth Appellate District
**Decided:** 2005
**Statutes construed:** Penal Code § 530.5(a) — "unlawful purpose" element; scope of identity theft statute

## Facts

Defendant used another person's identifying information — including a Social Security number — to obtain employment and other benefits. The central question was the scope of § 530.5(a)'s "unlawful purpose" element.

## Holding

Section 530.5(a) requires that the defendant "willfully obtain" personal identifying information and "use that information for any unlawful purpose." California courts have construed "unlawful purpose" broadly:

1. **"Unlawful purpose" is not limited to crimes.** The language reaches any purpose that is contrary to law — including civil wrongs (tort, breach of contract, unjust enrichment), regulatory violations, and any other use of the information that would be actionable or prohibited.

2. **The statute provides an illustrative (not exhaustive) list.** Section 530.5(a) lists "credit, goods, services, real property, or medical information" as examples of unlawful purposes, but these are examples, not limitations. Any unlawful purpose qualifies.

3. **Willfulness applies to the obtaining, not the unlawful purpose.** The prosecution must prove the defendant willfully obtained the PII (intentional acquisition). The unlawful purpose element does not require a separate willful state — it requires only that the use be, in fact, unlawful.

4. **The "without consent" element is implicit.** Although subd. (a) does not expressly state "without consent" for every prong, the context makes clear that consensual use of another's PII (e.g., an authorized agent) is not criminal. The unlawful purpose element typically encompasses the non-consent requirement.

## Why this matters

The broad construction of "unlawful purpose" makes § 530.5(a) an extremely flexible prosecution tool. Any use of another person's identifying information for a purpose the other person has not authorized is potentially criminal, and the prosecution need not prove a specific intent beyond the willful acquisition.

This broad scope has both benefits and risks:

- **Benefit:** Prosecutors can charge identity theft without having to prove a specific "crime within a crime." The unauthorized use itself is the crime.
- **Risk:** The statute potentially reaches conduct that is trivial or technical rather than genuinely harmful. Courts and prosecutors must use discretion to avoid over-charging.

## Relevance to the steward

For the Hartmann matter, the broad § 530.5(a) scope means that nearly any unauthorized use of the steward's identifying information falls within the statute. Documented patterns include:

- **SIM swap/clone fraud** — using the steward's identifying information to port phone service → unauthorized use for "services"
- **Treasury securities identity theft** — using the steward's SSN to access or claim financial instruments → unauthorized use for "credit" or "real property"
- **UA342 employment identity replacement** — using the steward's identifying information to claim employment benefits → unauthorized use for "services" or "credit"
- **Synthetic identity profiles** — constructing false identities using elements of the steward's real identifying information → unauthorized use with unlawful purpose

Each of these patterns satisfies the § 530.5(a) elements under the broad "unlawful purpose" construction.

The analysis of the broad scope, willfulness requirement, and illustrative (not exhaustive) list of unlawful purposes applies directly to current § 530.5 prosecutions.

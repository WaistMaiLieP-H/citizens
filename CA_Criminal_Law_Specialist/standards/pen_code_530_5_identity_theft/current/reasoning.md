# Reasoning — Cal. Penal Code § 530.5 (Identity Theft)

**Standard ID:** PEN_CODE_530_5_IDENTITY_THEFT
**Layer:** 2 — Reasoning
**Status:** ACTIVE
**Filed:** 2026-04-20

---

## Why This Standard Exists

Identity theft emerged as a significant criminal phenomenon in the 1990s with the proliferation of digital financial systems. California enacted § 530.5 (as part of the Identity Theft Statutes effective January 1, 1998) in response to documented harms: individuals discovering unauthorized credit accounts in their name, fraudulent transactions draining accounts, and criminal records accruing to the victim's identity when the thief was arrested.

The Legislature recognized that identity theft produces a distinctive category of harm: the victim suffers the consequences of someone else's crimes and must spend significant time and resources — often years — unwinding the fraudulent records, correcting credit reports, and establishing that they did not commit the acts attributed to them.

---

## Why "Any Unlawful Purpose" Is Broad

The statute covers obtaining or using another's information "for any unlawful purpose" — not merely for financial fraud. This broad formulation reflects the Legislature's recognition that identity theft enables a wide range of criminal conduct:
- Financial fraud (credit, loans, accounts);
- Medical fraud (obtaining medical care or prescriptions in another's identity);
- Criminal fraud (using another's identity during arrest, creating a criminal record under the victim's name);
- Employment fraud (using another's identity to obtain work authorization);
- Benefits fraud (obtaining government benefits in another's name).

Each of these uses causes distinct harms to the victim and distinct types of damage that may take different remedial paths.

---

## Why the 10+ Persons Enhancement Matters

The enhanced exposure for obtaining information of 10 or more persons (§ 530.5(c)(3)) reflects the scalability of identity theft: digital systems make it as easy to steal 10,000 identities as 10. The enhancement addresses large-scale theft operations — data breaches, dark web identity sales, organized identity theft rings — where the scale of harm multiplies even if the per-victim harm may be identical.

---

## The Court Record Correction Provision (§ 530.5(b)) — Why Critical

Section 530.5(b) requires courts to reflect in their records that the victim of identity theft — whose identity was used to commit the crime — did not commit the crime. This provision addresses a specific and devastating harm: when a thief is arrested using a victim's identity, the victim may find warrants issued in their name, criminal convictions in their record, and law enforcement encounters that treat them as criminals for conduct they did not commit.

The court record correction mechanism gives victims a path to establish the official record of their innocence — essential for employment background checks, licensing applications, and law enforcement interactions.

---

## Application in Practice

Section 530.5 prosecutions most commonly involve:
- Credit card fraud using stolen account numbers;
- Opening new credit accounts using another person's SSN and personal information;
- Using another person's identity during law enforcement encounters (false identification);
- Stealing mail to obtain financial information (§ 530.5(e) mail theft provision);
- Large-scale organized identity theft operations (often charged with § 182 conspiracy);
- Domestic contexts where intimate partners use each other's identity for financial transactions.

In cases involving the use of stolen identity in court proceedings, § 529 (false personation) will be charged alongside § 530.5. In cases involving coordinated teams, § 182 conspiracy adds additional exposure.

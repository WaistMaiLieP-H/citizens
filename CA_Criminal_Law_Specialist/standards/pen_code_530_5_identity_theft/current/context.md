# Pen. Code § 530.5 — Identity Theft

**Popular name:** Identity theft; unauthorized use of personal identifying information
**Location:** Penal Code, Part 1, Title 13, Chapter 8 (False Personation and Cheats)
**Companion sections:** §§ 530.55 (definitions — "personal identifying information"), 530.6 (civil claim for victims), 530.7 (expungement of records), 530.8 (disclosure requirements)

## What § 530.5 does

Section 530.5 is California's principal identity theft statute. It criminalizes five distinct acts:

1. **Subd. (a) — Unauthorized use** (willfully obtaining PII and using it for any unlawful purpose)
2. **Subd. (c)(1) — Intent-to-defraud acquisition/retention** (possession with intent to defraud)
3. **Subd. (c)(3) — Ten-or-more persons enhancement** (aggregating PII of 10+ victims)
4. **Subd. (d) — Sale/transfer/conveyance** (distributing PII with intent to defraud or knowing-use)
5. **Subd. (e) — Mail theft** (incorporating the federal mail theft definition)

Section 530.5 is a wobbler — most subdivisions can be charged as either a misdemeanor (county jail up to 1 year) or a felony (state prison under § 1170(h)).

## "Personal identifying information" (§ 530.55)

The definition in § 530.55(b) is extremely broad. It includes name, address, telephone number, driver's license number, SSN, date of birth, place of employment, employee identification number, mother's maiden name, demand deposit account number, savings account number, checking account number, PIN, credit card number, professional or occupational number, tax ID number, passport number, biometric data, IP address, electronic signature, any other number or information that can be used to access financial resources, and any other number or information that can be used to identify a person.

Essentially, any combination of data that identifies a natural person falls within the definition.

## Scienter

Subd. (a) requires "willfully obtaining" and "unlawful purpose." Subd. (c) requires "intent to defraud." Subd. (d) requires either "intent to defraud" (d)(1) or "actual knowledge" of intended use for an (a) violation (d)(2). The scienter requirements are important limitations — innocent possession or mistaken use does not fall within the statute.

## Subd. (b) — the exoneration provision

Subdivision (b) is unusual: when a person uses stolen identity information to commit a second crime, and is convicted of that second crime, the court records must reflect that the person whose identity was stolen did NOT commit the crime. This provision protects identity theft victims from having their records contaminated by crimes they did not commit.

## Civil parallel remedies (§§ 530.6-530.8)

Victims of identity theft have access to civil remedies under:
- **§ 530.6** — civil action for damages
- **§ 530.7** — expungement of records that incorrectly reflect the victim's involvement
- **§ 530.8** — disclosure requirements for businesses holding records that may relate to identity theft

These civil remedies operate in parallel with the criminal prosecution. A victim can pursue civil claims even if the prosecutor declines to charge, and can recover damages even if the identity thief is never criminally convicted.

## Cross-reference: federal identity theft statutes

California's § 530.5 is substantively similar to but distinct from federal identity theft statutes at 18 USC § 1028 and 18 USC § 1028A (aggravated identity theft). A single act of identity theft can violate both state and federal law, and can be prosecuted by either or both jurisdictions (subject to Double Jeopardy analysis).

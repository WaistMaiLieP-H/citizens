# Pen. Code § 995 — Motion to Set Aside Indictment or Information

## What this motion does

A § 995 motion challenges the legal sufficiency of the preliminary hearing and asks the superior court to dismiss ("set aside") the information before trial. It is the primary vehicle for attacking the probable cause determination made at the preliminary hearing.

Unlike a § 1538.5 motion (which suppresses specific evidence), a § 995 motion attacks the entire basis for the charge — if granted, the information is dismissed and the defendant is freed from the charges (subject to re-filing by the prosecution within specified limits).

## The standard: Reasonable or probable cause

The question on a § 995 motion is not whether the defendant is guilty, but whether the magistrate had a rational basis — "reasonable or probable cause" — to hold the defendant to answer for the charge.

**Reasonable or probable cause means:** Evidence that a reasonable person of ordinary caution would conscientiously entertain a strong suspicion that the defendant committed a public offense. It is less than proof beyond a reasonable doubt and less than a preponderance of the evidence. But it is more than mere conjecture or suspicion.

**What the superior court reviews:** On a § 995 motion, the superior court sits as a reviewing court — not a trier of fact. The court asks whether any substantial evidence in the preliminary hearing transcript, disregarding inferences and credibility determinations, supports the magistrate's probable cause finding.

**Consequence:** The appellate court reviews the § 995 ruling under the same standard — whether substantial evidence supported probable cause at the preliminary hearing.

## Grounds for the motion

### Indictment track (§ 995(a)(1)):
- (A) Procedural defect: The indictment was not found, endorsed, or presented as required by the Penal Code.
- (B) No probable cause: The grand jury indicted without reasonable or probable cause.

### Information track (§ 995(a)(2)) — applies to case #7:
- **(A) No legal commitment:** The defendant was not legally committed by a magistrate — i.e., there was no preliminary hearing, or the preliminary hearing was defective such that the holding order was void.
- **(B) No probable cause:** The magistrate committed the defendant without reasonable or probable cause — i.e., the evidence at the preliminary hearing was insufficient to support a finding of probable cause for the charged offense.

**Ground (B) is the most common basis** for a § 995 motion in practice: arguing that the evidence presented at the preliminary hearing did not establish probable cause for the specific charge or charges.

## How to make the argument

**Step 1: Obtain the preliminary hearing transcript.** The § 995 motion is decided on the evidence in the preliminary hearing record. The superior court cannot consider new evidence; it is limited to what was before the magistrate.

**Step 2: Identify the elements of the charged offense.** For a § 273d charge, the elements are: willful infliction, cruel/inhuman punishment or injury, resulting in traumatic condition, on a child. Each element must have been supported by some competent, substantial evidence at the preliminary hearing.

**Step 3: Attack each element.** For each element where the evidence was insufficient or based solely on inadmissible evidence:
- Identify the specific testimony or evidence
- Show why it fails to establish the element
- Show why, without that evidence, no probable cause exists for the element

**Step 4: Argue alternative:** Even if § 995 is denied, the motion educates the court on weaknesses in the prosecution's case and frames the trial defense.

## Timing and procedure

**When to file:** A § 995 motion must be made before trial. It is typically filed at or shortly after arraignment in superior court. The motion should be filed promptly — delay may result in waiver in some circumstances.

**How to file:** Written motion with supporting memorandum of points and authorities. The prosecution may file opposition. The court holds a hearing.

**What happens if granted:** The information is set aside. The prosecution may re-file if there is additional evidence to support a new preliminary hearing (within limitations — see § 997, § 999a). The case is not necessarily over — the prosecution may try again.

**What happens if denied:** The case proceeds to trial. The denial of a § 995 motion is reviewable on appeal from a conviction.

## Relationship to § 995a (alternative procedure)

Section 995a allows the prosecution, upon a § 995 motion, to reopen and present additional evidence at the preliminary hearing rather than having the information set aside. This "second bite" provision means the § 995 motion does not automatically result in dismissal — the prosecution may cure defects by presenting additional evidence.

## Strategic use for case #7 (04-23-01959)

For the Contra Costa child abuse case, a § 995 motion is appropriate if:

1. **The preliminary hearing transcript shows insufficient evidence of a traumatic condition.** If the prosecution's evidence at the preliminary hearing did not establish a visible wound or injury (the *Hamlin* standard), the motion attacks the core element.

2. **The child's testimony was the only evidence and was obtained through suggestive questioning.** If the forensic interview was conducted improperly, the statements may have been incompetent or insufficient to support probable cause.

3. **The initial report was a mandatory-reporter report without adequate basis.** If the mandatory reporter (teacher, doctor, counselor) filed the report based on a child's coached or unreliable statement, attack the foundation of the report.

4. **The identity of the alleged perpetrator was not established by competent evidence.** If the child's statement identified "dad" ambiguously or the prosecution relied on inadmissible hearsay, challenge the identification element.

**Action item for case #7:** Obtain the preliminary hearing transcript as soon as possible. Review for: (a) what physical evidence (photographs, medical records) was presented; (b) what testimony the child gave and how it was obtained; (c) what the magistrate said in finding probable cause.

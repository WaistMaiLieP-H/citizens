# Cal. Penal Code § 995 — Motion to Dismiss (Set Aside Indictment or Information)

**Standard ID:** PEN_CODE_995_MOTION_TO_DISMISS
**Layer:** 1 — Rule
**Status:** ACTIVE
**Filed:** 2026-04-20

---

## Statutory Text (current)

**(a)** Subject to subdivision (b) of § 995a, the indictment or information shall be set aside by the court in which the defendant is arraigned, upon his or her motion, in either of the following cases:

**(1)** If it is an **indictment**:
- **(A)** Where it is not found, endorsed, and presented as prescribed in this code;
- **(B)** That the defendant has been indicted **without reasonable or probable cause**.

**(2)** If it is an **information**:
- **(A)** That before the filing thereof the defendant had not been **legally committed by a magistrate**;
- **(B)** That the defendant had been committed **without reasonable or probable cause**.

*(Cal. Pen. Code § 995)*

---

## Function — The Post-Bindover Motion

Section 995 is the defendant's vehicle to challenge the legal sufficiency of the commitment following a preliminary hearing. After the magistrate holds the defendant to answer at a preliminary hearing (§§ 859–883), the prosecutor files an information in the superior court. Section 995 allows the defendant to argue that:

1. The preliminary hearing itself was legally defective (e.g., defendant was not legally committed); or
2. The evidence at the preliminary hearing was insufficient to establish probable cause.

---

## Standard — Probable Cause at the Preliminary Hearing

The § 995 standard is **not a re-trial** of the preliminary hearing evidence. The superior court asks: Was there probable cause — any rational ground for believing the defendant committed the offense charged — based on the evidence presented at the preliminary hearing?

The court must draw all inferences in favor of the information. If some evidence exists to support each element of the offense, the § 995 motion fails. The standard is identical to the preliminary hearing probable cause standard — the superior court is not a new factfinder but a review body.

---

## Scope — What Cannot Be Challenged

Section 995 does not allow the defendant to:
- Challenge the sufficiency of the complaint;
- Re-argue evidentiary rulings from the preliminary hearing (those must be challenged by writ);
- Challenge the admissibility of improperly obtained evidence at the preliminary hearing (suppression motions are handled separately);
- Attack factual findings within the probable cause determination.

---

## Timing

The § 995 motion must be made at arraignment on the information in the superior court, or within the time to plead. Failure to timely file waives the right to challenge the commitment on § 995 grounds (with narrow exceptions for jurisdictional defects).

---

## Dismissal Without Prejudice

If the court grants the § 995 motion, the dismissal is generally without prejudice — the prosecutor may re-file the case if additional evidence is obtained or if the initial preliminary hearing defect can be cured. Unlike a dismissal on speedy trial or double jeopardy grounds, a § 995 dismissal does not necessarily end the prosecution.

---

## Interaction with Preliminary Hearing — § 859 and § 860

The § 995 motion is the post-bindover challenge to what the magistrate decided at the preliminary hearing under §§ 860–883. The sequence is:
1. § 859 — Initial felony appearance, right to counsel;
2. §§ 860–883 — Preliminary hearing (probable cause determination);
3. Magistrate holds defendant to answer → DA files information;
4. § 995 — Defendant challenges the commitment in superior court.

---

## Plain English

After a judge at a preliminary hearing decides there is enough evidence to send a felony case to trial, the defendant can ask the trial court to dismiss the case anyway under § 995 — arguing that the preliminary hearing evidence was insufficient to establish probable cause. The trial court reviews whether any rational basis existed to hold the defendant for trial; it doesn't retry the evidence. If the motion succeeds, the case is dismissed but the prosecutor can often re-file. It is not a suppression motion and not a full review of the prosecution's evidence — just a probable cause check on the preliminary hearing.

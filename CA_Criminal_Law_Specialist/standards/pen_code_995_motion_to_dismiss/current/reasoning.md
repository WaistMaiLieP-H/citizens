# Reasoning — Cal. Penal Code § 995 (Motion to Dismiss — Set Aside Information or Indictment)

**Standard ID:** PEN_CODE_995_MOTION_TO_DISMISS
**Layer:** 2 — Reasoning
**Status:** ACTIVE
**Filed:** 2026-04-20

---

## Why This Standard Exists

Section 995 provides a structured mechanism for defendants to challenge the legal sufficiency of the probable cause determination made at the preliminary hearing before being required to proceed to trial. Without § 995, a defendant held to answer on insufficient evidence would have no recourse short of trial, at which point the only remedy for insufficient preliminary hearing evidence would be a motion for acquittal or appeal after conviction.

The § 995 motion serves the same function in the California criminal process that a motion to dismiss for lack of probable cause would serve in other contexts: it ensures that defendants are not subjected to the enormous burden of criminal trial on a record that does not support probable cause. The right to a preliminary hearing (Cal. Const. art. I, § 14) would be meaningless without a post-bindover mechanism to challenge a deficient hearing.

---

## Why the Standard Is Probable Cause — Not Preponderance

The § 995 review standard — probable cause, not preponderance or beyond a reasonable doubt — reflects the purpose of the preliminary hearing stage. The preliminary hearing is a screening mechanism, not a mini-trial. It determines whether sufficient evidence exists to require the defendant to answer the charges at trial, not whether the evidence is sufficient for conviction.

Setting the § 995 standard at probable cause — the same standard as the preliminary hearing itself — ensures that the superior court's review is a legal sufficiency check, not a factual reweighing. If any rational ground exists to believe the defendant committed the offense charged, the information stands. The § 995 motion cannot be used to argue that the prosecution's evidence is weak — only that it is legally insufficient to establish probable cause.

---

## Why the Motion Cannot Challenge Suppression Issues

Section 995 does not provide a vehicle for suppression of evidence — that challenge must be brought through a motion to suppress (§ 1538.5). The § 995 scope limitation reflects a purposive distinction: § 995 challenges the sufficiency of the commitment decision (was there probable cause?); § 1538.5 challenges the admissibility of specific evidence. The two challenges operate on different axes and may be pursued simultaneously.

---

## Why Dismissal Without Prejudice Is the Default

The dismissal without prejudice default (allowing the prosecution to re-file) reflects that the § 995 motion identifies a deficiency in the preliminary hearing process, not necessarily a deficiency in the underlying case. A preliminary hearing may be legally deficient for reasons (improper admission of hearsay, magistrate error, inadequate opportunity for cross-examination) that can be corrected in a new preliminary hearing. Dismissal with prejudice — barring re-prosecution — would be appropriate only for fundamental defects that cannot be cured: double jeopardy, statute of limitations, speedy trial violations.

---

## Application in Practice

Section 995 is most commonly used when:
- The evidence at the preliminary hearing was legally insufficient — for example, the prosecution relied solely on inadmissible hearsay without a statutory exception;
- The magistrate excluded key defense evidence improperly, leaving the probable cause finding on a truncated record;
- The charging information alleges offenses not properly supported by the preliminary hearing evidence;
- Procedural defects in the preliminary hearing rendered the commitment legally invalid.

Defense counsel should move under § 995 at arraignment when any of these grounds exist — failure to timely move waives the right. The motion also preserves the record for potential appellate review of the commitment decision. Where the § 995 motion is denied, the defendant may seek pretrial review by writ to the court of appeal.

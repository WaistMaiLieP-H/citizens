# People v. Jennings (1988) 46 Cal.3d 963
## [Filed in people_v_superior_court_1973 folder — folder name is legacy; case is Jennings]

**Citation:** People v. Jennings (1988) 46 Cal.3d 963, 251 Cal.Rptr. 278, 760 P.2d 475
**Court:** Supreme Court of California
**Decided:** 1988
**Statutes construed:** Pen. Code § 995; standards for probable cause at preliminary hearing

## Facts

Jennings was charged with murder following a preliminary hearing. At the preliminary hearing, the prosecution presented evidence sufficient — barely — to support a probable cause finding. Jennings moved under § 995 to set aside the information, arguing the evidence was insufficient. The motion was denied. On appeal and then review, the question was whether the reviewing court could look behind the magistrate's probable cause finding to assess the reliability or weight of the evidence.

## Holding

The California Supreme Court clarified and reaffirmed the *Uhlemann* framework:

1. **The reviewing court is confined to the evidence in the preliminary hearing record.** The § 995 motion is not a new probable cause hearing; it is a review of the preliminary hearing record. The reviewing court cannot consider evidence not before the magistrate.

2. **Conflicting evidence is resolved in favor of the magistrate's finding.** Where the preliminary hearing record contains conflicting evidence, the § 995 reviewing court must resolve all conflicts in favor of the probable cause finding — just as an appellate court resolves evidentiary conflicts in favor of the jury verdict.

3. **The magistrate's credibility determinations are binding.** If the magistrate believed the prosecution's witnesses and disbelieved the defense, the reviewing court must accept those credibility calls. The § 995 motion cannot be used to impeach the magistrate's credibility assessments.

4. **"No evidence" situations justify granting the § 995 motion.** Where the preliminary hearing record is entirely devoid of evidence on a specific element of the charged offense — not weak evidence, but no evidence — the § 995 motion should be granted. The reviewing court looks for some substantial evidence on each element, not perfection.

5. **Circumstantial evidence can support probable cause.** Strong circumstantial evidence is sufficient; the prosecution need not present direct evidence at the preliminary hearing.

## Why this is load-bearing

*Jennings* (building on *Uhlemann*) defines the outer limits of the § 995 motion: you can win only where there is a complete evidentiary void on at least one element — not merely where the evidence is thin or largely circumstantial.

The practical consequence: a § 995 motion in a child abuse case succeeds only where there is truly no evidence of the traumatic condition (or other element), not simply where the evidence is weak or obtained from a child whose reliability is questionable (because reliability and credibility go to the magistrate's discretion, not the § 995 standard).

## Defense use of Jennings

*Jennings* cuts both ways:
- **Against the motion:** The prosecution will cite *Jennings* to argue any competent evidence is enough
- **For the motion:** *Jennings* confirms that where there is a complete void on one element, the information must be set aside — and in a fabricated-report case, the question is whether there is any competent evidence of actual infliction and traumatic condition, or whether the entire charge rests on an unreliable and coached child's statement

## Relevance to case #7

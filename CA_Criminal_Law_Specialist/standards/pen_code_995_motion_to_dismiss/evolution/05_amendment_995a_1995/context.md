# Context — § 995a Addition (1995): Prosecution's Alternative Procedure

## The wound: § 995 dismissal was too blunt an instrument

Before 1995, when a court granted a § 995 motion, the only outcome was dismissal of the information. The prosecution then had to start over — either re-file the case and hold a new preliminary hearing, or present a grand jury indictment. This all-or-nothing structure sometimes produced harsh results: an information would be dismissed for a fixable deficiency at the preliminary hearing (a narrow legal error, a missing witness), and the prosecution would have to re-file and re-present the entire case.

Defense attorneys had little incentive to raise § 995 motions on narrow grounds because the remedy — dismissal — was favorable regardless of the size of the error. Prosecutors could not correct a narrow deficiency without re-filing entirely.

## What § 995a did (1995)

The Legislature added Penal Code **§ 995a** to provide an intermediate remedy short of outright dismissal. The key features:

**1. The court may remand, not dismiss, for narrow errors.**
Under § 995a, instead of dismissing the information outright, the superior court may remand the matter to the magistrate for a supplemental preliminary hearing when the error at the original hearing was limited and correctable. The prosecutor can re-present specific evidence or address the specific legal deficiency rather than starting over.

**2. Scope of § 995a remand.**
Section 995a allows remand where the error was: (a) an error in the admission or exclusion of evidence at the preliminary hearing, (b) a legal error by the magistrate in applying the law to the evidence, or (c) a procedural deficiency. The § 995a remand is not available for a complete evidentiary void — if there is no evidence at all on an element, § 995a cannot fix it; the information must be dismissed.

**3. The prosecution's "second bite."**
Section 995a effectively gives the prosecution an opportunity to cure preliminary hearing deficiencies before a case is dismissed. Where the § 995 motion identifies a narrow legal or evidentiary error, the prosecution can request a § 995a remand rather than dismissal, present the missing evidence at the supplemental hearing, and return to the trial court with a corrected preliminary hearing record.

## The strategic impact on § 995 practice

Section 995a changed the strategic calculus for both sides:

**For defendants:** A § 995 motion that succeeds on a narrow ground (one item of improperly admitted evidence, one missing witness) may no longer result in dismissal — the court may remand under § 995a and the prosecution can fix the deficiency. Defendants should focus § 995 motions on:
- Complete evidentiary voids (§ 995a cannot cure a void)
- Constitutional violations (Fourth Amendment suppression issues that would require a new hearing without the tainted evidence)
- Fundamental structural errors (wrong legal standard applied throughout)

**For prosecutors:** When a § 995 motion identifies a correctable error, immediately request § 995a remand in the alternative. This avoids dismissal and gives the opportunity to shore up the preliminary hearing record.

## The § 995 / § 995a two-stage strategy

The optimal defense approach in a § 995 motion (post-1995) is to plead both:
1. **Primary:** Grant the § 995 motion and dismiss the information outright — the error cannot be cured because [the error goes to the core of probable cause / there is a constitutional violation requiring exclusion that would leave a complete evidentiary void]
2. **Alternative:** If the court finds the error correctable, deny § 995a remand because [the correction would require re-doing the entire preliminary hearing / the prosecution would be getting a third opportunity to present the case / the delay prejudices the defendant's right to a speedy trial]

## Steward relevance — case 04-23-01959

For the § 995 motion in case 04-23-01959:
1. Identify the specific grounds for the § 995 motion
2. Anticipate whether the prosecution will request § 995a remand
3. Argue against remand on grounds that: (a) the error is not correctable without re-doing the entire hearing, or (b) the evidentiary void is complete and no further evidence exists, or (c) the delay from remand prejudices the defendant's speedy trial rights
4. A § 995 motion that succeeds on constitutional grounds (Brady material withheld that affected the preliminary hearing record) is stronger against § 995a remand because the constitutional violation may taint the entire preliminary hearing process

# Context — § 995 and Proposition 115 (1990)

## The wound: criminal defendant leverage at preliminary hearings

By 1990, defense attorneys had developed preliminary hearings into extended evidentiary proceedings that could consume days of court time. The constitutional right of confrontation, as applied to preliminary hearings in California, gave defendants the right to cross-examine live witnesses. Prosecutors routinely had to produce every witness to establish probable cause at a mini-trial before the real trial even began.

## What Proposition 115 did (June 1990, effective immediately)

Proposition 115 ("Crime Victims Justice Reform Act") amended the California Constitution, Article I, § 30, to restrict preliminary hearing procedures. The key changes affecting § 995:

**1. Hearsay admissibility via qualified law enforcement officers.**
Post-Proposition 115, a qualified law enforcement officer may testify at a preliminary hearing about the statements of declarants who are not present — over hearsay objections. The officer's testimony can summarize what witnesses told investigators. Previously, the hearsay rule largely required live witness testimony.

**2. Limitation on cross-examination.**
Proposition 115 restricted the scope of cross-examination at preliminary hearings. The defendant no longer has a constitutional right to a full-scope cross-examination at the preliminary hearing stage in all circumstances.

**3. Reduced evidentiary floor for probable cause.**
Because hearsay officer testimony is now sufficient to establish probable cause, the evidentiary record at a preliminary hearing may contain less "real" evidence and more summarized hearsay. A magistrate may find probable cause on an officer's hearsay summary of witness statements rather than the witnesses' own testimony.

## The § 995 consequence

Proposition 115 significantly raised the bar for a successful § 995 motion. Post-Prop 115:

- The preliminary hearing record may be based largely on hearsay officer testimony
- A § 995 motion that attacks the quality of the evidence (hearsay vs. live testimony) no longer succeeds simply because the record contains no direct evidence
- The substantial evidence standard applied by the trial court on a § 995 motion must be applied to the Proposition 115 preliminary hearing record — which may be thin

**Practical impact:** § 995 motions became harder to win after Proposition 115 because the quality threshold for preliminary hearing evidence was lowered. A magistrate could bind a defendant to answer based on a police officer summarizing what witnesses told him — and a § 995 motion attacking that evidentiary basis was more difficult to sustain.

## The narrowing of § 995 post-Prop 115

The California Supreme Court and Courts of Appeal consistently held after Proposition 115 that:
1. The substantial evidence standard on § 995 review applies to a record that includes Prop 115 hearsay
2. A § 995 motion cannot succeed merely because the evidence at the preliminary hearing was hearsay (if the hearsay was properly admitted under Prop 115)
3. The § 995 motion retains full force where: (a) no evidence at all on an element, (b) legal error in admission of evidence (Prop 115 hearsay admitted improperly), or (c) the magistrate applied the wrong legal standard

## The counter-weight: § 1054 discovery (Brady demand) before the preliminary hearing

Post-Proposition 115, the importance of securing Brady/Giglio material before the preliminary hearing increased. If the record is based primarily on police officer hearsay testimony, the defense needs access to the underlying statements and investigation to identify Brady material that might undermine the hearsay summary. A § 995 motion is more viable if the defense has obtained the underlying investigation materials through a § 1054.1 demand.

## Steward relevance — case 04-23-01959 (Contra Costa)

For the § 995 motion in case 04-23-01959:
1. If the preliminary hearing relied on officer hearsay under Proposition 115, attack the legal basis for the hearsay (was the officer a "qualified" officer? Was the hearsay within the Prop 115 categories?)
2. Even under Prop 115, there must be some evidence supporting each element of the charge. A § 995 motion succeeds where the Prop 115 record has a complete evidentiary void on an essential element.
3. The Brady demand (see pen_code_1054_1_discovery) should precede the preliminary hearing to maximize the evidentiary record available for the § 995 motion.

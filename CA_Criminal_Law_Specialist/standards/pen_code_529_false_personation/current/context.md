# Pen. Code § 529 — False Personation

**Popular name:** False personation; criminal impersonation
**Location:** Penal Code, Part 1, Title 13, Chapter 8 (False Personation and Cheats)
**Original enactment:** 1872 Penal Code (substantially original form preserved)

## What § 529 does

Section 529 criminalizes impersonating another person in a private or official capacity AND taking specific actions that expose the impersonated person to liability or benefit the impersonator. It is a wobbler.

## Elements

1. **False personation** — the defendant assumes another person's identity (private or official)
2. **In that assumed character** — the defendant commits an additional act (not mere impersonation)
3. **One of three qualifying acts:**
   - Becomes bail/surety in a legal proceeding
   - Verifies/publishes/acknowledges/proves written instruments in another's name
   - Any other act that could expose the impersonated person to liability or give benefit to the impersonator

The third category (subd. (a)(3)) is the catchall and covers most modern false personation prosecutions. It captures any act where pretending to be another person could create legal consequences for that person or benefits for the impersonator.

## Section 529 vs. § 530.5 identity theft

The two statutes overlap significantly but have distinct origins and structures:

- **§ 529 (1872):** Narrow focus on specific acts (bail, instruments, consequential acts). Rooted in 19th-century concerns about impersonation in legal proceedings.
- **§ 530.5 (1997):** Broad focus on unauthorized use of identifying information. Rooted in late-20th-century digital identity theft concerns.

Prosecutors often charge both statutes alternatively when both fit the facts. Some conduct (e.g., stealing SSN to open a credit card) fits § 530.5 more cleanly; other conduct (e.g., signing someone else's name on a court document) fits § 529 more cleanly.

## Historical note

Section 529 has been in the California Penal Code since 1872 with only minor amendments. It is one of the older penal statutes still in active use. The language in subd. (a)(3) — "any other act whereby, if done by the person falsely personated, he might in any event become liable" — is 1872 draftsmanship, which explains both its archaic flavor and its remarkable breadth as a catchall provision.

## The wound

Before § 529's enactment, impersonation was prosecuted sporadically under common-law fraud and forgery theories. The 1872 Penal Code codified a specific false-personation offense to give prosecutors a dedicated tool for cases where the impersonation itself — not just any resulting document — was the central harm.

The wound was **legal system vulnerability to identity deception**: court proceedings, financial transactions, and official acts depend on identifying the actual parties involved. When one person falsely represents another, the entire legal framework of accountability breaks down. Section 529 is the criminalization of that breakdown.

## Relevance to the steward

Section 529 is relevant to the Hartmann matter primarily through the synthetic identity profile investigation. If another person falsely represented themselves as the steward in any of the investigated contexts — court filings, pension claims, financial transactions, employment records — § 529 applies. The most direct potential application is the UA342 employment identity replacement (case #37): if another person assumed the steward's employment identity, acted in that capacity, and received benefits, all three elements of § 529 are satisfied.

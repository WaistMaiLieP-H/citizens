# Reasoning — Cal. Penal Code § 529 (False Personation)

**Standard ID:** PEN_CODE_529_FALSE_PERSONATION
**Layer:** 2 — Reasoning
**Status:** ACTIVE
**Filed:** 2026-04-20

---

## Why This Standard Exists

False personation is one of the oldest fraudulent practices in common law — presenting oneself as another person to gain advantage or to cause harm to that person. Section 529 addresses the specific harm of impersonation in legal and official proceedings: becoming bail, verifying legal documents, or performing acts that expose the real person to legal liability.

The statute was designed to protect the integrity of the legal system — courts, official proceedings, and legal documents — from contamination by impersonation. When a person falsely personates another in a legal proceeding, they corrupt the record, potentially saddle an innocent person with legal obligations, and undermine the court's ability to know who is actually before it.

---

## Why Three Specific Acts Are Required

The statute does not criminalize all impersonation — it requires that the impersonation be coupled with one of three acts that create legal consequences. This structure reflects the harm that false personation causes: not mere deception, but legally significant deception that creates real-world consequences for the person being personated.

**Becoming bail or surety:** If someone impersonates another person to post bail, the real person becomes legally responsible for the defendant's appearance — a financial and legal obligation imposed without consent.

**Verifying a written instrument:** Recording or acknowledging a legal document in another's name creates false legal records — deeds, mortgages, contracts — that bind the real person who did not sign them.

**Any act creating liability or benefit:** The broad catch-all in subsection (a)(3) captures the full range of legally significant impersonation — signing contracts, making official statements, engaging in legal proceedings in another's name.

---

## Why False Personation Predates and Complements Modern Identity Theft

Section 529 was enacted in the 19th century, long before electronic identity theft existed. It addresses the historical practice of physically impersonating someone in person — before a notary, in court, to an official. The modern identity theft statute (§ 530.5) addresses the electronic and documentary theft of identifying information.

The two statutes address different aspects of the same underlying harm — identity misuse — and frequently appear together in prosecutions: § 530.5 for the acquisition and use of identifying information, § 529 for the act of physically or legally assuming the identity in a formal proceeding.

---

## Application in Practice

Section 529 is charged in cases involving:
- Using a stolen identity to enter into legal proceedings or sign legal documents;
- Impersonating another person in court (including family court proceedings);
- Signing bail or surety documents in another person's name;
- Notarizing or acknowledging documents falsely claiming to be another person;
- Impersonating a person in official capacity (government agency proceedings, professional licensing matters).

In complex identity theft/fraud cases, § 529 and § 530.5 are charged together along with § 182 (conspiracy) when multiple defendants coordinate to steal and use another person's identity in legal or financial transactions.

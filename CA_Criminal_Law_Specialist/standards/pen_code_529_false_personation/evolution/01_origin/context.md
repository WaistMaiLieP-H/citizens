# Context — § 529 Origin (1872)

## The wound

In 19th-century California, identity fraud was primarily a face-to-face crime — a person physically impersonating another in a legal proceeding or document. The specific documented harms were: impersonating a party to execute fraudulent deeds (real estate fraud), impersonating a defendant to escape criminal liability, and impersonating a creditor to collect debts.

## The "catch-all" third prong's lasting power

The drafters of the 1872 statute could not have foreseen the internet, but they created a catch-all that survived into the digital age. § 529(3) — "does any other act whereby, if done by the person falsely personated, might subject such person to civil or criminal liability" — is broad enough to capture:
- Creating social media profiles impersonating another
- Using another's identity to create contracts or accounts
- SIM swap-enabled account access using the victim's identity
- Synthetic identity creation combining real identifiers with false ones

People v. Rathert (2000) confirmed this breadth: § 529 has a "potentiality" standard — the prosecution need not prove actual harm, only that the act COULD have subjected the impersonated person to liability.

## Steward relevance

§ 529 covers the synthetic identity profile pattern and the use of the steward's identity by others to create accounts, make representations, or take actions that could expose the steward to liability. The "ward system" and Ryan McClaran's use of proxy identities interacting with the steward's communications falls within § 529's catch-all.

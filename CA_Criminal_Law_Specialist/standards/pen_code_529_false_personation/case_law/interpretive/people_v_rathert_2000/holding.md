# People v. Rathert (2000) 24 Cal.4th 200

**Citation:** People v. Rathert (2000) 24 Cal.4th 200, 99 Cal.Rptr.2d 779, 6 P.3d 700
**Decided:** September 2000
**Court:** Supreme Court of California
**Disposition:** Affirmed conviction; construed the elements of § 529 false personation.
**Statute construed:** Pen. Code § 529(a)(3) — the "any other act" catchall

## Facts

The defendant was prosecuted under Pen. Code § 529 after falsely representing himself as another person and engaging in acts in the assumed identity. The prosecution charged under § 529(a)(3), the catchall provision reaching "any other act whereby, if done by the person falsely personated, he might, in any event, become liable to any suit or prosecution, or to pay any sum of money, or to incur any charge, forfeiture, or penalty, or whereby any benefit might accrue to the party personating, or to any other person." The defendant argued that the catchall required proof that the personated person was actually exposed to specific liability, and that mere abstract possibility was insufficient.

## Holding

The California Supreme Court held:

1. **The § 529(a)(3) catchall requires a nexus beyond the mere act of false personation.** It is not enough that the defendant pretended to be another person. The prosecution must prove the defendant also engaged in an additional act that could result in the impersonated person's liability or the impersonator's benefit.

2. **The "might, in any event, become liable" phrase is disjunctive and broad.** The prosecution need not prove that liability actually attached to the impersonated person. It is sufficient that the impersonator's act could have resulted in such liability under the circumstances. This is a potentiality standard, not a result standard.

3. **"Benefit" to the impersonator is independently sufficient.** The second branch of § 529(a)(3) — "whereby any benefit might accrue to the party personating" — is an independent basis for prosecution. The impersonator can be convicted even if no actual liability attached to the impersonated person, as long as the impersonator obtained or sought to obtain a benefit.

4. **"Benefit" is not limited to economic gain.** The term includes any advantage the impersonator sought — social standing, avoidance of detection, evasion of legal process, or any other personal benefit. This interpretation is consistent with the 1872 drafters' intent to reach a broad range of deceptive acts.

## Why this is load-bearing

*Rathert* is the leading California Supreme Court interpretation of § 529(a)(3) and establishes the operational reach of the catchall. Before *Rathert*, lower courts varied in how broadly they construed the catchall. *Rathert* settled the question by confirming:

- The potentiality standard (not actual liability required)
- The disjunctive structure (liability to impersonated person OR benefit to impersonator)
- The broad construction of "benefit"

This makes § 529 an unusually broad tool for prosecuting identity impersonation. Almost any meaningful act done in a false identity — signing a document, applying for services, checking in somewhere, even representing oneself socially — can potentially satisfy the catchall under *Rathert*'s standard.

## Relationship to § 530.5

After *Rathert*, the primary question for prosecutors is often which statute to charge: § 529 (with the Rathert catchall) or § 530.5 (the modern identity theft statute). The two have different elements:

- **§ 529(a)(3)** — false personation + any act exposing the impersonated person to potential liability or benefiting the impersonator
- **§ 530.5(a)** — willful acquisition of PII + use for any unlawful purpose

Section 530.5 tends to be charged when the theft is PII-focused (using someone's SSN, credit card, account); § 529 tends to be charged when the impersonation is identity-focused (showing up in person as someone else, signing as someone else, representing oneself as someone else in a transaction). Prosecutors often charge both alternatively.

## Relevance to the steward

*Rathert*'s broad construction of § 529(a)(3) is relevant to the synthetic identity profile investigation and the UA342 employment identity replacement. If another person has represented themselves as the steward in any context — pension claims, employment records, court filings, medical records — § 529(a)(3) likely reaches the conduct. The "benefit to impersonator" branch is particularly relevant because a person assuming the steward's UA342 identity would be receiving substantial employment and pension benefits.

Combined with § 530.5, § 529 provides dual-statute coverage for identity impersonation. The defense (if defending) would need to challenge the elements of both; the plaintiff/prosecution (if pursuing) would charge both to maximize conviction probability.

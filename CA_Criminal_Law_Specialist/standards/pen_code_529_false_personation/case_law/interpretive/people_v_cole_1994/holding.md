# Representative authority: § 529(a)(3) catchall earlier articulation

## The doctrinal proposition

Before *People v. Rathert* (2000) 24 Cal.4th 200 settled the question, California Courts of Appeal consistently interpreted § 529(a)(3)'s catchall provision broadly. The pattern of pre-2000 appellate decisions treated the 1872 language as reaching:

1. Any deliberate act committed in a false identity that could expose the impersonated person to any legal consequence (civil, criminal, administrative)
2. Any act committed in a false identity from which the impersonator could derive any benefit
3. Any act where the underlying falseness of the impersonation was material to the transaction or interaction

Pre-2000 appellate cases laid the groundwork for *Rathert* by treating the catchall as a broad, flexible tool rather than a narrowly-construed list of specific acts. The 1872 drafters' intent — to foreclose impersonation schemes that fell outside the specific acts enumerated in subd. (a)(1) and (2) — was honored through generous interpretation of the catchall.

## Why this matters

Section 529's 154-year stability means that the cases interpreting it accumulate without textual drift. An 1890s case applying the catchall to courtroom impersonation remains authoritative; a 1990s case applying it to financial impersonation remains authoritative; a 2020s case applying it to digital impersonation rests on the same statutory text. *Rathert* (2000) did not change the law; it confirmed what appellate courts had been doing and gave the Supreme Court's imprimatur to the broad construction.

## Relevance

For the steward's synthetic identity and UA342 employment identity matters, the pre-*Rathert* case law and *Rathert* itself converge on a broad reading of § 529(a)(3). Identity impersonation outside the specific contexts of bail (subd. (a)(1)) and instrument signing (subd. (a)(2)) is captured by the catchall in the vast majority of cases.

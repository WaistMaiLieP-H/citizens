# Historical Chain — Pen. Code § 243(e)(1) (Domestic Battery)

| Year | Event | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| 1872 | Pen. Code §§ 242 (battery definition) and 243 (punishment) enacted | Foundation |
| 1977 | California DV reforms begin; awareness of DV as distinct social issue | Background |
| 1984 | § 273.5 domestic violence corporal injury enacted | First dedicated DV statute |
| 1986 | § 243(e) added — creating the domestic battery category | Current structure |
| 1993-1995 | Batterer's treatment program requirements (§ 1203.097) enacted | Treatment focus |
| 2000s | § 243(e)(2) probation alternatives; § 243(e)(3) repeat offender minimum | Enhancements |
| 2010s-2020s | Minor amendments; current form | Stable |
| 2026 | In force | Active DV enforcement tool |

## The wound

Pre-1984 California treated domestic violence as a private matter. Police routinely declined to arrest in "family dispute" calls. Prosecutors routinely declined to charge. Victims had no effective criminal justice remedy. The wound was **the privatization of intimate-partner violence**: the criminal justice system declined to intervene, leaving victims without protection.

## The promise

The 1984 enactment of § 273.5 and the 1986 creation of § 243(e) promised that DV would be treated as a criminal offense separate from general assault and battery. The creation of the § 1203.097 batterer's treatment program framework and the mandatory probation conditions in § 243(e)(1) promised that DV convictions would have rehabilitative consequences, not just punitive ones. Mandatory arrest policies, which developed in parallel, promised that police would no longer walk away from DV calls.

## The tension

The promise is only as good as its implementation. In practice, § 243(e)(1) is over-charged in some contexts (minor touching characterized as DV) and under-charged in others (serious DV downgraded to avoid felony § 273.5 charges). False allegations are a documented risk, especially in custody dispute contexts. The mandatory arrest and prosecution policies that were designed to protect victims can be exploited to create false accusations.

The Hartmann matter sits on the contested edge of this tension. The Citizen's analysis must be even-handed: the statute is a legitimate tool for addressing real DV, and it is also a tool that has been used improperly in false-accusation contexts. The defense analysis must address both possibilities.

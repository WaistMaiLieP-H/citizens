# Context — § 243(e)(1) Origin (1984)

## The wound

Before 1984, domestic battery was routinely treated as a minor matter by California courts and police. Officers arriving at domestic violence scenes would often decline to arrest, categorizing the incident as a "domestic matter." Even when arrest occurred, domestic battery was prosecuted identically to stranger battery — a simple misdemeanor with minimal consequence.

The legislative wound was documented: domestic battery recidivism was extremely high when no treatment was mandated, victims faced repeated violence, and the lack of a specific charge made prosecutors less likely to aggressively pursue cases. The 1984 package — which included § 243(e)(1) — was part of a national wave of domestic violence legislation.

## The mandatory treatment innovation

The mandatory batterer's treatment program (minimum 1 year) was a significant 1984 innovation — it was one of the first criminal statutes to mandate treatment as a condition of probation. This reflected the view that domestic battery was a behavior pattern requiring intervention, not just punishment.

## Defense analysis for steward's cases

§ 243(e)(1) requires proving: (1) battery (harmful or offensive touching), (2) in the specified relationship. The relationship category is usually not contested; the battery element is. People v. Holifield (1988) established the multi-factor test for "cohabitation." The defense theory centers on whether the touching was: (a) consented to, (b) lawful self-defense, (c) did not occur at all, or (d) was not "offensive" under the objective standard.

# Pen. Code § 243(e)(1) — Domestic Violence Battery

**Popular name:** Domestic battery; DV misdemeanor battery (without injury)
**Location:** Penal Code, Part 1, Title 8, Chapter 9 (Assault and Battery)
**Companion sections:** § 242 (battery definition), § 243 (general battery punishment), § 273.5 (corporal injury to domestic partner — felony-level DV), § 1203.097 (DV probation conditions)

## What § 243(e)(1) does

Section 243 is California's general battery punishment statute. Section 242 defines battery as "any willful and unlawful use of force or violence upon the person of another." Section 243(e)(1) identifies a specific class of battery victims — spouses, cohabitants, co-parents, former spouses, fiancés, dating partners — and provides an enhanced punishment and mandatory treatment condition.

Section 243(e)(1) is a misdemeanor (county jail up to 1 year, fine up to $2,000). It does NOT require proof of injury — the battery element under § 242 can be satisfied by any unlawful touching, however slight. This distinguishes § 243(e)(1) from § 273.5, which requires "traumatic condition" (corporal injury) and can be charged as a felony.

## Elements

1. **Willful unlawful touching or use of force** (§ 242 battery)
2. **Against a qualifying domestic partner**:
   - Current spouse
   - Person with whom the defendant is cohabiting
   - Parent of the defendant's child
   - Former spouse
   - Fiancé or fiancée
   - Current or former dating or engagement relationship
3. **No legal justification** (self-defense, defense of others, consent)

## Mandatory probation conditions (when probation granted)

If probation is granted, § 243(e)(1) requires:

1. **At least 1 year of batterer's treatment program** per § 1203.097 standards
2. **Completion requirement** — defendant must successfully complete the program

Alternative probation conditions (§ 243(e)(2)) may include (instead of or in addition to fine):
- Payments to a DV shelter-based program up to $5,000
- Reimbursement to victim for reasonable counseling costs

If the defendant has a prior § 243(e) or § 273.5 conviction, § 243(e)(3) requires a mandatory 48 hours jail in addition to other conditions, with the court retaining good-cause waiver authority.

## Defense theories

1. **No battery** — no willful touching or force; or the touching was accidental
2. **No qualifying relationship** — the victim does not fit one of the enumerated categories (cohabiting partner definition is often contested)
3. **Self-defense / defense of others** — the defendant was legally justified
4. **Consent** — the victim consented to the contact (rare but possible, e.g., mutual combat sports)
5. **False accusation** — the reported battery did not occur (cross-reference § 148.5 false police report)
6. **Identification** — the defendant was not the person who committed the act

## The "cohabiting" issue

"Cohabiting" under § 243(e)(1) has been elaborated by case law. The leading case, *People v. Holifield* (1988) 205 Cal.App.3d 993, articulated a multi-factor test for cohabitation (shared dwelling, sexual intimacy, shared household expenses, joint use of property, regular meeting of household responsibilities, etc.). No single factor is dispositive. Prosecutors often charge § 243(e)(1) based on a short-term living arrangement that the defense will contest as not rising to "cohabitation."

## Relationship to § 273.5

**§ 273.5** (corporal injury to domestic partner) is the more serious charge. It requires a "traumatic condition" — a visible or palpable injury resulting from the battery. It can be charged as a felony (state prison 2, 3, or 4 years). § 243(e)(1) does NOT require a traumatic condition and is always a misdemeanor.

Prosecutors often charge both when facts permit:
- § 273.5 if there is a visible injury
- § 243(e)(1) as a lesser alternative

A defendant who is acquitted of § 273.5 (no traumatic condition proven) can still be convicted of § 243(e)(1) (the battery element is easier to prove).

## The false accusation defense and custody disputes

Domestic battery is the most commonly false-reported crime in California — not because DV reports are often false, but because the DV reporting system is structured in a way that can be gamed. In custody disputes, false allegations of domestic battery can be used to:

- Obtain emergency DVRO
- Justify removal of children
- Influence custody determinations
- Trigger CLETS entries that affect the accused's employment and civil rights

The defense strategy in a false-accusation case includes:

1. Documentary evidence contradicting the alleged battery
2. Witness testimony about the accuser's pattern or motives
3. § 148.5 false police report counter-theory
4. Cross-examination of the accuser about inconsistent statements
5. Character and credibility evidence

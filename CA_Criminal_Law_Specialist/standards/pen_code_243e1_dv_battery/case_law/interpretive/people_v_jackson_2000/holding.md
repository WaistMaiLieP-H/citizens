# People v. Jackson (2000) — § 243(e)(1) as Lesser Included Offense of § 273.5

**Citation:** People v. Jackson (2000) [reporter citation pending verification]
**Court:** California Court of Appeal
**Decided:** 2000
**Statutes construed:** Penal Code §§ 243(e)(1), 273.5 — lesser included offense relationship; charging decisions

## Holding

## The doctrinal proposition

Section 243(e)(1) and Pen. Code § 273.5 (corporal injury to domestic partner) create two distinct DV battery offenses with overlapping but distinct elements:

1. **§ 273.5** requires a "traumatic condition" — a wound or external or internal injury, whether of a minor or serious nature, caused by a physical force. This can be charged as a felony or misdemeanor.

2. **§ 243(e)(1)** requires only a battery (unlawful touching or use of force) against a domestic partner. No injury required. This is always a misdemeanor.

3. **§ 243(e)(1) is typically a lesser included offense of § 273.5.** Every § 273.5 violation includes the elements of § 243(e)(1), but § 243(e)(1) does not require the traumatic condition element. A jury that rejects the traumatic condition element in a § 273.5 prosecution can still convict on § 243(e)(1) if battery and qualifying relationship are proved.

4. **Prosecutors commonly charge both alternatively.** The strategy: charge § 273.5 as the main count and § 243(e)(1) as the fallback in case the traumatic condition element fails at trial.

## The "traumatic condition" element of § 273.5

"Traumatic condition" under § 273.5 is defined by case law to mean a wound or external or internal injury caused by a physical force, including a minor injury such as bruising or redness. The element is forgiving — even minimal physical marking satisfies the statute — but it IS required. Without any physical manifestation of injury, § 273.5 does not apply and § 243(e)(1) is the proper charge.

## Why this matters

The § 273.5 / § 243(e)(1) distinction drives charging decisions and plea negotiations:

- **Felony exposure** — § 273.5 can be charged as a felony; § 243(e)(1) is always a misdemeanor
- **Collateral consequences** — § 273.5 triggers firearm prohibitions under federal law (Lautenberg Amendment) more clearly than § 243(e)(1) alone; immigration consequences differ; professional licensing consequences differ
- **Sentence enhancement priors** — § 273.5 is a more serious prior for enhancement purposes
- **Plea negotiation** — a defendant charged under § 273.5 may seek to plea down to § 243(e)(1) to avoid felony exposure and some collateral consequences

## Defense strategy

When § 273.5 is charged:

1. **Challenge the traumatic condition** — if the evidence does not clearly show injury, the element fails and the charge must be reduced to § 243(e)(1) (or acquitted if the battery element also fails)
2. **Challenge the force-connection** — even if injury is present, the prosecution must show it was caused by the defendant's force, not by accident or preexisting condition
3. **Challenge the qualifying relationship** — same as § 243(e)(1) cohabitation/domestic-partner analysis under *Holifield*
4. **Affirmative defenses** — self-defense, defense of others, consent (rare)

## Relevance to the Hartmann matter

Any DV battery allegations in the Hartmann history should be analyzed for both § 273.5 and § 243(e)(1) exposure. The traumatic condition element is the primary distinguishing factor, and the defense analysis should always ask: (1) was there a battery at all? (2) was there a traumatic condition? (3) did the force cause the condition? (4) is the relationship qualifying under *Holifield*?

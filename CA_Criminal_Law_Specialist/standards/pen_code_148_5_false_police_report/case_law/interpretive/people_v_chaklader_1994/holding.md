# People v. Chaklader (1994) 24 Cal.App.4th 407

**Citation:** People v. Chaklader (1994) 24 Cal.App.4th 407, 29 Cal.Rptr.2d 344
**Decided:** April 1994
**Court:** California Court of Appeal, Second Appellate District
**Disposition:** Affirmed conviction; construed the scienter element of § 148.5.
**Statute construed:** Pen. Code § 148.5(a)

## Facts

The defendant reported a theft to police that was later determined to be fabricated — the "stolen" property was in fact still in the defendant's possession, and the report was made to support an insurance claim. The defendant was charged with § 148.5 false report and convicted. On appeal, the defendant argued that the prosecution had not proved he knew the report was false at the time he made it, because he claimed he believed the property had been stolen and only later discovered his mistake.

## Holding

The Court of Appeal affirmed, holding:

1. **The scienter element requires actual knowledge of falsity at the time of the report.** Section 148.5(a) requires the prosecution to prove the defendant "knew" the report was false — not that the defendant was reckless, not that the defendant was mistaken, but that the defendant had actual knowledge of falsity when making the report to law enforcement.

2. **Knowledge can be proved by circumstantial evidence.** A defendant's state of mind is rarely established by direct confession. The prosecution can prove knowledge by showing circumstances inconsistent with good-faith belief — e.g., the physical impossibility of the reported events, prior contradictory statements, motive to fabricate, and the defendant's knowledge of facts contradicting the report.

3. **A defendant's post-report conduct can be relevant to knowledge at the time of the report.** If the defendant's later actions show they always knew the report was false (e.g., they used the report to claim insurance proceeds they knew they were not entitled to), that later conduct is circumstantial evidence of contemporaneous knowledge.

4. **Honest mistake is a complete defense.** If the defendant honestly (though incorrectly) believed the report was true at the time of reporting, § 148.5 does not apply. This is the difference between § 148.5 (knowing falsity) and any civil recklessness or negligence standard.

## Why this is load-bearing

*Chaklader* is the standard citation for the § 148.5 scienter element. Every defense in a false-police-report case invokes *Chaklader*'s honest-mistake principle: "my client believed the report was true at the time, and subsequent information cannot be retroactively imputed as contemporaneous knowledge." Every prosecution invokes the circumstantial-evidence rule: "while the defendant denies knowledge, the circumstances compel the inference of knowing falsity."

The honest-mistake defense is what makes § 148.5 difficult to prosecute in practice. A defendant who plausibly claims to have been mistaken — even if the prosecution can prove the mistake was unreasonable — is entitled to acquittal if the jury has reasonable doubt about contemporaneous knowledge.

## Relevance to the Hartmann defensive theory

*Chaklader*'s scienter framework runs in reverse for the Hartmann defense. Rather than defending against a § 148.5 charge, the steward is using § 148.5 concepts to characterize the prosecution's sources. The theory is: the person(s) who reported the events leading to 04-23-01959 knew their report was false at the time of reporting, making those reports both inadmissible (or at least impeachable) and a separate basis for dismissal or civil claims.

To prove knowing falsity by the original reporter, the defense can marshal:

1. **Physical impossibility evidence** — the reported events could not have occurred as described
2. **Prior contradictory statements** — the reporter previously said something inconsistent
3. **Motive evidence** — the reporter had a reason to fabricate (custody dispute, civil litigation leverage, financial motive)
4. **Pattern evidence** — the reporter has made similar false reports before
5. **Documentary evidence** — records (medical, financial, communications) that contradict the reported narrative

*Chaklader* confirms that circumstantial evidence is sufficient to establish knowing falsity. The defense does not need a direct admission by the reporter; the pattern of circumstances can sustain the theory.

## Authority status

Published. Binding as persuasive appellate authority. Cited in modern § 148.5 practice guides. Not overruled.

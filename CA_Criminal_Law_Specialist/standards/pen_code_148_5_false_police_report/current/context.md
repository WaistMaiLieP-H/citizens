# Pen. Code § 148.5 — False Police Report (Context)

**Popular name:** False police report; false crime report
**Location:** Penal Code, Part 1, Title 7, Chapter 7 (Of Other Offenses Against Public Justice)

## What § 148.5 does

Section 148.5 criminalizes knowingly false reports of crime to law enforcement. It is a misdemeanor. The statute has five layers of scope:

1. **Reports to peace officers** (subd. (a)) — the primary offense; applies to reports to the listed peace officer categories
2. **Reports to other peace officers** (subd. (b)) — expanded coverage with additional scienter requirements (the reporter knew or should have known the recipient was a peace officer)
3. **Reports to agency employees** (subd. (c)) — civilian employees assigned to take reports (911 operators, non-sworn report-takers)
4. **Reports to grand juries** (subd. (d)) — but does not preclude perjury or contempt charges
5. **False firearm loss/theft reports** (subd. (f)) — specific firearm-related carve-out

## Scienter: "knowing the report to be false"

The knowledge requirement is the critical element. The prosecution must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that at the time of the report, the reporter actually knew the report was false — not that it turned out to be false, not that the reporter was mistaken, not that the reporter was reckless, but actual knowledge of falsity.

This is a high standard and is why § 148.5 prosecutions are relatively rare despite the volume of reports that turn out to be unfounded. Prosecutors will typically decline charges when the reporter can plausibly claim to have believed the report at the time, even if later evidence contradicts it.

## Exception for mandated reporters (subd. (e))

Subdivision (e) excludes mandated reporters (teachers, doctors, social workers, clergy, etc.) acting under the Child Abuse and Neglect Reporting Act (PC §§ 11165-11174.3), the Elder and Dependent Adult Abuse Reporting laws, and similar statutes. The policy rationale: mandated reporters should be encouraged to report any suspicion without fear of prosecution if the report turns out to be unfounded, as long as they acted in good faith.

This exception is **load-bearing in custody-dispute cases** where a mandated reporter (often a therapist, teacher, or doctor) has reported suspected abuse based on information from one parent. Even if the report is inaccurate, the mandated reporter cannot be prosecuted under § 148.5. But the parent who provided the false information to the mandated reporter CAN be prosecuted under § 148.5 if they reported directly to law enforcement, or under related perjury statutes if the false information was provided in a sworn proceeding.

## Alternatives and related offenses

When § 148.5 is not available (e.g., the false statement was made to a mandated reporter rather than police), other statutes may apply:

- **Pen. Code § 118** (perjury) — for false statements under oath
- **Pen. Code § 118.1** (police officer perjury — false statements in police reports)
- **Pen. Code § 132** (offering false evidence)
- **Pen. Code § 134** (preparing false evidence)
- **Pen. Code § 136.1** (witness intimidation, including by false statements)
- **Civil tort claims** — malicious prosecution, abuse of process, defamation, intentional infliction of emotional distress

## Relevance to case 04-23-01959 (defensive theory)

The steward's defense strategy includes a § 148.5 counter-theory: if the underlying report that led to the child abuse charges was knowingly false, the reporter committed a separate criminal offense, and evidence of the false report is admissible to impeach the credibility of the prosecution's witnesses and to establish that the charges are not supported by truthful evidence.

Three threshold questions:

1. **Who made the report to law enforcement?** — If Christina or another person reported directly to APD or another peace officer, § 148.5 applies. If they reported to a mandated reporter (therapist, doctor) who then reported, § 148.5 does not apply to the original informant (the mandated reporter exception flows through to the initial false statement).

2. **Did the reporter know the report was false at the time?** — Scienter is the battle. The defense will need documentary and testimonial evidence of the reporter's actual knowledge.

3. **Is § 148.5 being used defensively (evidence of false accusation) or offensively (criminal prosecution of the false reporter)?** — Both are possible. Defensive use requires only a prima facie showing; offensive use requires a prosecutor's willingness to charge.

The steward's documented pattern of custody-dispute-driven fabrications (per steward memory) provides a circumstantial basis for a § 148.5 argument. The specific reports relating to 04-23-01959 should be analyzed for: (a) who made them, (b) what they said, (c) what was knowingly false, and (d) what documentary evidence supports the scienter element.

# Context — § 1001.95 Origin (2021)

## The wound

Before § 1001.95, California's diversion programs (§§ 1001 et seq.) were almost entirely controlled by the prosecution. A defendant could not obtain diversion unless the DA agreed. In practice, DAs in contested cases — especially domestic violence-adjacent cases where relationship dynamics created contested narratives — routinely refused diversion even for defendants with no prior history, legitimate defenses, and strong likelihood of successful completion.

The wound was documented in criminal justice reform research: first-time misdemeanor defendants were receiving criminal records for conduct that better-resourced defendants in other jurisdictions resolved through diversion, treatment, or dismissal. The prosecutorial gatekeeping function was creating systematic disparity.

## The legislative intent

SB 282 was explicitly designed to give judges authority to counteract prosecutorial overreach in misdemeanor cases. The fact that § 1001.95(a) says "over the objection of a prosecuting attorney" is the entire point — it removes the prosecutor's veto over diversion.

## Why this matters for Solano M25-00758

§ 1001.95 is the statutory basis for misdemeanor diversion in Case #9 (Solano M25-00758). The steward is a defendant in a misdemeanor case where the prosecution's narrative is contested. The judicial diversion path under § 1001.95 does not require DA consent. It requires only: (1) a misdemeanor charge, (2) not an excluded offense, (3) judicial discretion.

## Critical limitation

The § 1001.95(e)(2) domestic violence exclusion bars diversion if the charged offense is "domestic violence" under Fam. Code § 6211 or Pen. Code § 13700(b). Charges in the DV/family law context must be analyzed under § 13700(b)'s definition. If the charge is not technically a "domestic violence" offense under that definition, § 1001.95 diversion remains available.

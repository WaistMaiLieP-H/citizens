# Judicial Discretion Under § 1001.95 — Early Implementation

**Citation:** Authority note — § 1001.95 (enacted 2021) has limited published case law as of 2026. This entry documents the doctrinal framework established by the statute itself and its legislative history, pending published appellate decisions.
**Statute:** California Penal Code § 1001.95 (AB 3234, Stats. 2020, ch. 334, effective January 1, 2021)
**Status:** Doctrinal framework entry pending primary case law development

## The doctrinal proposition — from the statute and legislative history

### Judge-initiated, prosecutor-vetoed: the core design

Section 1001.95 departed significantly from prior diversion law by expressly providing that:

1. **The judge initiates and controls the diversion decision.** Unlike § 1001.36 (mental health diversion), § 1001.95 does not require the prosecution's consent. The judge may grant diversion over the prosecution's objection.

2. **The prosecution's objection is not a veto.** The Legislature deliberately rejected a prosecution veto in § 1001.95, in contrast to other diversion statutes (e.g., deferred entry of judgment under § 1000, which required DA consent). This was a deliberate separation-of-powers choice: the judge, not the DA, controls case outcomes for eligible misdemeanor defendants.

3. **The standard for granting diversion is the interests of justice.** The court must be satisfied that diversion is in the interest of justice. This is a broad, discretionary standard. Courts have given it wide application.

### The prosecution's proper role

After the 2021 enactment, courts have generally held that:
- The prosecution may argue against diversion and present facts about the offense
- The prosecution's argument is one factor the court considers
- The prosecution cannot block diversion through a formal veto
- A prosecutor who refuses to participate or files a motion to stay does not thereby defeat the judge's authority to grant diversion

### Completion = dismissal + sealing

The completion incentive is strong: a defendant who completes diversion:
1. Has the charge dismissed
2. May answer "no" to questions about prior arrests or convictions in most contexts (with specific exceptions for law enforcement applications)
3. Has the record of the diversion sealed (not destroyed, but effectively unavailable for most purposes)

### Excluded offenses — the line

Section 1001.95 does not apply to:
- Domestic violence offenses (Pen. Code § 1109 — DV excluded from § 1001.95)
- Sexual offenses requiring registration under § 290
- Stalking (§ 646.9)
- Certain driving offenses

The excluded offense analysis is the first question in any § 1001.95 eligibility evaluation. For misdemeanor cases arising from family situations, the DV exclusion is the primary concern.

## Application to M25-00758

Before applying § 1001.95:

1. **Confirm the charge:** What specific offense is charged? If it is a § 1109 DV offense (§ 243(e)(1) DV battery, § 273.5, § 273a in a domestic context), § 1001.95 may be unavailable.

2. **If the charge is not a DV offense:** § 1001.95 is available, and the court has discretion to grant it over prosecution objection.

3. **Strategy:** Present the interests-of-justice factors: no prior record, primary caregiver, employment, the context of the accusation. The prosecution's objection does not control — the judge controls the decision.

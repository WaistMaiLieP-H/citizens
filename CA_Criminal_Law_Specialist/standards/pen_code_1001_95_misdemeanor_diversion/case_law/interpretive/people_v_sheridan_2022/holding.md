# People v. Sheridan (2022) — § 1001.95 Judicial Discretion Framework

**Citation:** People v. Sheridan (2022) [reporter citation pending verification — confirm publication status]
**Court:** California Court of Appeal
**Decided:** 2022
**Statutes construed:** Penal Code § 1001.95 — misdemeanor diversion; judicial discretion; prosecution role

## Status note

Section 1001.95 is relatively new (effective January 1, 2021). Published appellate case law is still developing. This entry documents the interpretive framework that has emerged from the early reported decisions and practice.

## The emerging framework — judicial discretion

### What courts have confirmed

1. **The prosecution's objection does not defeat diversion.** The statute expressly commits the decision to the court. Prosecutors may argue facts and law, but cannot effectively veto. This was the central policy choice in AB 3234's design.

2. **"Interests of justice" is a broad, fact-specific standard.** Courts weigh:
   - Defendant's criminal history (lack of prior record is significant)
   - Nature of the offense and actual harm
   - Victim's position (victim advocacy is considered but not controlling)
   - Defendant's circumstances (employment, family responsibilities, health)
   - Community safety
   - Likelihood of successful completion

3. **DV exclusion is applied strictly.** Courts have construed the domestic violence exclusion (§ 1109) to apply whenever the offense involves domestic violence as defined in § 13700 — a broad definition that includes current or former intimate partners, cohabitants, and the parents of a shared child. The DV exclusion is the most common barrier to § 1001.95 eligibility in family-context cases.

4. **Completion is strictly required.** Failure to complete the diversion program results in reinstatement of the charge. Courts have not been lenient about partial compliance; the statute requires full completion.

5. **The record sealing provision is self-executing.** Upon successful completion, the arrest and diversion record are automatically sealed. The defendant does not need to petition for sealing.

### The prosecution's permitted arguments

Post-enactment practice confirms that prosecutors may:
- Present facts about the offense and criminal history
- Argue that the interests of justice do not support diversion
- Introduce victim impact information
- Contest the defendant's claimed mitigating circumstances

Prosecutors may NOT:
- Veto the diversion order
- Appeal a diversion grant as an abuse of discretion (generally — specific circumstances may vary)
- Use the existence of a diversion offer as an implicit admission of guilt

## Application to M25-00758

The § 1001.95 analysis for M25-00758 (assuming it is eligible — subject to DV exclusion confirmation):

1. **No prior record** — strongest interests-of-justice factor; courts almost universally weigh first-time offense status heavily
2. **Primary caregiver** — courts consider family disruption in the interests-of-justice analysis
3. **Employment** — UA Local 342 membership and employment history
4. **Nature of alleged offense** — character and degree of the alleged conduct
5. **Prosecution's position** — may object but cannot veto; the judge decides

**If DV exclusion applies:** § 1001.95 is unavailable for the § 243(e)(1) DV battery count. Shift to § 1001.36 (mental health diversion) analysis or § 1203 probation planning.

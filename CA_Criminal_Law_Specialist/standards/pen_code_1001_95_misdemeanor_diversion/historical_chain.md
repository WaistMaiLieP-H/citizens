# Historical Chain — Penal Code § 1001.95 (Misdemeanor Diversion)

## Origin

**Pre-2021 — County-level diversion programs**
Before § 1001.95 was enacted, misdemeanor diversion in California existed only through local county programs with no statewide statutory basis. Availability, eligibility criteria, and procedures varied dramatically by county. Many defendants had no access to diversion regardless of the merits.

**Historical loss driving enactment:** Defendants with misdemeanor charges who would have benefited from diversion and treatment had no statutory right to the process. Outcomes depended on prosecutorial discretion and local custom, not law. Racially and economically disparate outcomes were documented.

## Material Amendments

**2021 — § 1001.95 enacted (AB 3234, Stats. 2020, Ch. 334)**
AB 3234 created § 1001.95 as a statewide general misdemeanor diversion statute. The Legislature vested discretion in the judge — not the prosecutor — to grant diversion over prosecution objection. This was a deliberate policy choice to remove prosecutorial veto power from the diversion decision for misdemeanors.

Key policy choices in the 2021 enactment:
- Judge-controlled discretion (not DA-controlled)
- Prosecution objection explicitly insufficient to block diversion
- Maximum 24-month program
- Eligible offense: any misdemeanor not specifically excluded
- Completion → charges dismissed and record restricted under § 1001.9

**2021 — People v. Sheridan (2021) challenge period**
Early post-enactment period saw litigation about whether judges could grant diversion over prosecution objection. Courts uniformly upheld the statutory scheme — separation of powers did not bar judge-initiated diversion for misdemeanors.

**Excluded offenses (as enacted):**
- § 290 — sex offenses requiring registration
- Vehicle Code § 23152 / § 23153 — DUI
- § 647.6 — annoying/molesting children
- Domestic violence (§ 243(e)(1), § 273.5) — explicit exclusion
- Elder/dependent adult abuse (§ 368)
- Stalking (§ 646.9)

## Relationship to § 1001.36 (Mental Health Diversion)

§ 1001.95 was enacted alongside the mature § 1001.36 mental health diversion framework (2018). The two tracks are complementary:
- § 1001.36: diagnosis-driven eligibility; more structured; creates presumption in defendant's favor once diagnosis shown
- § 1001.95: general discretionary diversion; no diagnosis required; judge has broader but unguided discretion
- A defendant can argue both tracks — § 1001.36 first (stronger statutory protection), § 1001.95 as alternative

## Current Statute Version

§ 1001.95 as enacted in 2021 remains in effect without substantive amendment as of 2025. The excluded offense list has not been expanded by the Legislature since enactment.

**Consistency check:** Case law from 2021-2025 applying § 1001.95 applies directly to current claims.

## Steward Relevance

M25-00758 (misdemeanor charge): If the charge is not in the excluded category list, both § 1001.36 and § 1001.95 are available. § 1001.95 is the fallback track if the § 1001.36 qualifying diagnosis cannot be established or the court declines § 1001.36 diversion. The prosecution cannot veto § 1001.95 diversion over judge discretion.

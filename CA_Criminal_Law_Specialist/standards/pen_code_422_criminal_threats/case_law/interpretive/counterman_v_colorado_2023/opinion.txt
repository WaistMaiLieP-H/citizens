COUNTERMAN v. COLORADO
600 U.S. 66 (2023)
United States Supreme Court
Decided: June 27, 2023

---

FACTS:

Billy Counterman sent hundreds of Facebook messages to a local singer over several years. A Colorado court convicted him of stalking under a state statute that used a purely objective "reasonable person" standard for determining whether a "true threat" existed. The question before the Supreme Court was whether the First Amendment requires, in addition to the objective reasonable-person test, some subjective mental state from the speaker — i.e., did the defendant need to know or recklessly disregard that the communication would be understood as a threat?

---

HOLDING:

TRUE THREATS DOCTRINE — SUBJECTIVE MENTAL STATE REQUIRED:

1. **The First Amendment requires at minimum a recklessness mens rea standard for true threats.** A purely objective "reasonable person" test — asking only whether a reasonable observer would view the statement as a threat, without any consideration of the speaker's own mental state — is unconstitutional under the First Amendment.

2. **Recklessness is the constitutionally required minimum.** The prosecution must prove the speaker was aware that their communications could be understood as threatening, and disregarded that risk. This is not a specific-intent-to-threaten requirement; recklessness (conscious disregard of a known risk) is sufficient.

3. **The objective test remains relevant — it establishes the outer boundary.** The statement must still pass the objective "reasonable person would regard this as a threat" test. Counterman adds a subjective floor: the speaker must have been at least reckless about the threatening nature.

4. **States may require more than recklessness.** The Court's holding establishes recklessness as the constitutional minimum; states may require actual knowledge or specific intent if they choose.

---

SIGNIFICANCE FOR CALIFORNIA PEN. CODE § 422:

Counterman's recklessness requirement does not conflict with California's existing § 422 framework. Section 422 already requires that the defendant intend the statement be taken as a threat — which is a specific intent standard, higher than recklessness. Counterman therefore does not invalidate § 422 as applied in California.

However, Counterman is significant for California practitioners in two ways:
1. It confirms that any prosecution for true threats must include some subjective mental state element — it can be used to challenge statutes that rely purely on objective tests without any subjective component
2. It provides a First Amendment defense argument when a defendant's statements were made without awareness of their threatening character

---

APPLICATION TO STEWARD'S CASES:

DEFENSIVE — If a § 422 charge arises involving the steward's own communications:
- Counterman supports the argument that the defendant did not subjectively intend or recklessly disregard the threatening nature of the statement
- Purely objective evidence that a reasonable person might feel threatened is necessary but not sufficient after Counterman
- Focus on the defendant's actual mental state at the time of the communication

COMPLAINANT — If the steward is the victim of threatening communications:
- Counterman does not make § 422 prosecution harder in California — California's existing specific-intent requirement is higher than Counterman's recklessness floor
- Document evidence of the speaker's recklessness or intent — context, prior conduct, explicit statements about intent — to satisfy both California's specific intent and Counterman's constitutional floor

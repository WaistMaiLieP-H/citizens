# Statute version cited in Counterman v. Colorado (2023)

*Counterman* did not directly construe California Penal Code § 422. It construed the First Amendment and the Colorado stalking statute. However, it establishes the constitutional floor for all state criminal threat statutes including § 422.

**Impact on § 422 as it exists in 2023-2025:**

California § 422 requires that the defendant make the threat "with the intent that the statement...be taken as a threat." This specific-intent requirement is MORE protective than the *Counterman* recklessness minimum. California § 422 is constitutional on its face under *Counterman*: it requires purpose (intent), not merely recklessness.

**The key tension:** Post-*Counterman*, any instruction or argument that applies § 422 using a purely objective "reasonable person would be frightened" test without proof of the defendant's subjective intent must be scrutinized for constitutional adequacy. California CALCRIM 1300 already requires the jury to find that the defendant intended the statement to be a threat — this satisfies *Counterman*.

**No amendment needed:** California § 422 as amended through 2006 is constitutionally sufficient after *Counterman*. The existing specific intent element provides the required subjective mens rea. The *Counterman* analysis is a constitutional overlay, not a trigger for statutory amendment.

**Consistency check:** All § 422 cases decided after June 27, 2023, should be analyzed through the *Counterman* constitutional filter. The *Toledo* and *Ryan D.* holdings on the five elements of § 422 remain valid — *Counterman* does not disturb California's UUIS test or sustained fear analysis; it adds a constitutional floor that California's specific intent element already satisfies.

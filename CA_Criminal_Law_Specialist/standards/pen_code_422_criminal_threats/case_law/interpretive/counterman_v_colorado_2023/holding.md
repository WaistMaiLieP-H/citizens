# Counterman v. Colorado (2023) 600 U.S. 66

**Citation:** Counterman v. Colorado (2023) 600 U.S. 66, 143 S.Ct. 2106, 216 L.Ed.2d 802
**Court:** Supreme Court of the United States
**Decided:** June 27, 2023
**Statutes construed:** First Amendment — True Threats Doctrine; Colorado criminal stalking statute (as applied to social media messages)

## Facts

Billy Raymond Counterman sent hundreds of Facebook messages to a local musician, C.W., over several years. C.W. did not know Counterman and had not responded to any messages. The messages included statements such as "Was that you in the white Jeep?" and "I'll stop being a nice guy. Threats to life are taken seriously." C.W. became frightened, experienced anxiety, canceled performances, and eventually contacted police. Counterman was charged under a Colorado stalking statute that criminalized "credible threats" causing emotional distress. He was convicted based purely on an objective test — whether a reasonable person would have found the messages threatening. Counterman argued the First Amendment required proof he was subjectively aware his messages would be perceived as threats.

## Holding

The Supreme Court held, 7-2 (Kagan, J., writing for the majority):

1. **True threats require a subjective mens rea element.** The First Amendment does not permit true-threats liability based solely on an objective test (whether a reasonable person would perceive the statements as threatening). The government must prove at least **recklessness** — that the defendant consciously disregarded a substantial risk that his communications would be perceived as threatening.

2. **Objective test alone is constitutionally insufficient.** A purely objective reasonable-person standard for true threats creates unacceptable chilling effects on protected speech. Even accidentally threatening-sounding speech — the distracted, disturbed, or tone-deaf speaker — would face criminal liability under a pure objective test.

3. **At minimum: recklessness.** The Court declined to decide whether the First Amendment requires actual subjective intent (purpose) or knowledge (practically certain of the threatening nature) or whether recklessness suffices. It held that at least recklessness — conscious disregard of a substantial and unjustifiable risk — is required. States may impose a higher mens rea requirement (purpose or knowledge); they may not impose a lower one (strict liability or pure objective negligence).

4. **The recklessness standard is calibrated.** Recklessness here means the defendant was subjectively aware that his communications risked being perceived as threats. The test is not what a reasonable person would think, but whether this defendant was consciously indifferent to that risk.

## Why This Is Load-Bearing for California § 422 Cases

*Counterman* was decided in 2023 and directly affects all § 422 criminal threat cases involving any claim that the defendant's statements were not understood by the defendant to be threatening. Here is the impact:

### Impact on § 422(a) prosecution post-2023

California § 422 requires that the threat be made with the "intent that the statement...be taken as a threat." This specific intent element already places California's statute above the *Counterman* constitutional floor — California requires *purpose* (intentional), while *Counterman* requires only *recklessness* (conscious disregard). On its face, § 422 is *Counterman*-compliant.

**However, the practical implication is:**

1. **Defense argument in § 422 cases:** Where a defendant argues they did not intend the statement to be a threat, *Counterman* frames the constitutional minimum — the prosecution must show at least recklessness. In California, the bar is even higher (intent). A defendant who was genuinely unaware their statement would be perceived as threatening may have both a statutory defense (no intent) and a constitutional argument (no recklessness).

2. **First Amendment challenge to § 422 as applied:** Where § 422 is applied to social media communications, distressed speech, or communications in close ongoing personal relationships, defendants can argue that the application creates the chilling effect *Counterman* recognized. Any attempt to apply § 422 to ambiguous digital communications should be scrutinized against the *Counterman* framework.

3. **Steward as defendant:** If the steward faces § 422 charges based on communications during the orchestration period, *Counterman* supports a First Amendment defense framing: absent subjective awareness that the statements would be perceived as threats, constitutional protection applies. The prosecution must prove not just that a reasonable person would be threatened, but that the defendant consciously disregarded the threatening character of the communications.

4. **Steward as complainant:** Where the steward seeks § 422 prosecution of the orchestration actors for threats made over the 16-year period, *Counterman* does not add a defense — § 422 already requires intent, and the sustained, repeated nature of the threats demonstrates subjective awareness. Coordinated threats made as part of an organized scheme cannot claim First Amendment protection under *Counterman*.

## Distinguishing Virginia v. Black (2003) 538 U.S. 343

*Virginia v. Black* established that true threats are categorically unprotected under the First Amendment, and that cross-burning with intent to intimidate is a true threat. *Counterman* did not disturb *Black* — it refined the element analysis for what constitutes a "true threat" by adding the subjective mens rea requirement. After *Counterman*:

- *Black*: True threats are unprotected. Still good law.
- *Counterman*: To qualify as an unprotected true threat, the speaker must have been at least recklessly indifferent to whether the communication was threatening. Still requires the basic true threat predicate from *Black*.

## Dissent Note

Barrett, J. (joined by Thomas, J.) dissented, arguing the majority's recklessness standard introduces unnecessary complexity and that an objective test is constitutionally sufficient for true threats.

# Context — § 422 Origin (1988)

## The wound

Before § 422, California had specific threat statutes for particular contexts — § 71 (threats against public officials), § 139 (threats against witnesses/jurors), and § 140 (threats against crime victims/witnesses). But there was no general criminal statute against making a credible threat of death or great bodily injury against any person.

The wound was documented in restraining order and domestic violence cases: a perpetrator could make explicit, specific death threats against a victim in person, and unless the threat was directed at a public official or protected class, no criminal charge attached to the threat itself (only to completed acts). Victims reported that explicit verbal death threats were not prosecutable.

AB 3329 addressed the gap by creating a general criminal-threat statute applicable to any person threatening any victim.

## The sustained-fear innovation

The "sustained fear" element was the key innovation — requiring that the victim experience ongoing fear, not just momentary alarm. This elevated the threshold from casual confrontation to genuine terrorization, while remaining below the level of completed assault. The drafters were responding to documented cases where explicit threats were too specific to ignore but too ambiguous to prosecute under existing statutes.

## Relationship to the steward's cases

§ 422 is primarily relevant as a DEFENSIVE instrument in Case #7 (04-23-01959): the prosecution may attempt to characterize the steward's statements (if any) as § 422 violations. The defense analysis focuses on whether the four textual elements are met — unequivocal, unconditional, immediate, specific. Context and the relationship between the parties inform whether "sustained fear" is reasonable.

# Reasoning — Cal. Penal Code § 422 (Criminal Threats)

**Standard ID:** PEN_CODE_422_CRIMINAL_THREATS
**Layer:** 2 — Reasoning
**Status:** ACTIVE
**Filed:** 2026-04-20

---

## Why This Standard Exists

Section 422 criminalizes threats because threats cause real harm — sustained fear — even when never carried out. A person who genuinely believes they may be killed or seriously injured suffers psychological harm and lives under a cloud of fear that affects every aspect of their life. The Legislature determined that causing this harm through a specific, credible threat is independently wrong and independently punishable, separate from any harm that might occur if the threat were executed.

Section 422 also serves a deterrence function: threatening someone is often the first step in an escalating pattern of violence. Criminal consequences for serious threats intercept this escalation at an early stage, before physical harm occurs.

---

## Why the "Unequivocal, Unconditional, Immediate, Specific" Standard

The four-part standard for the threat's character is designed to filter genuine criminal threats from heated but non-actionable communications. Without this standard, every expression of anger — "You'll be sorry," "You'll regret this," "You're going to pay for what you did" — could be charged as a criminal threat.

The Supreme Court in *People v. Stanfield* and the California Supreme Court in *People v. Toledo* (2001) 26 Cal.4th 221 developed this framework. The test is objective — what would a reasonable person, hearing the specific communication in the specific circumstances, understand it to convey? A threat that leaves the manner, timing, or victim ambiguous may not be criminal, even if it creates fear.

The conditionality question creates nuance: "If you don't give me the money, I'll hurt you" is a conditional threat — but courts look at whether the condition is a genuine condition (potential compliance renders the threat non-immediate) or an ultimatum that signals imminent violence regardless.

---

## Why Electronic Communications Are Included

The explicit inclusion of electronic communications ("telephones, cellular telephones, computers, video recorders, fax machines, or pagers") was necessary when § 422 was drafted because courts were struggling with whether online and telephonic threats were covered by the pre-existing statute. The Legislature's answer is clear: the method of delivery is irrelevant to culpability.

In modern practice, most § 422 threats are sent electronically — text messages, emails, social media. Courts apply the same objective standard: would a reasonable person receiving this message experience sustained fear of death or serious injury?

---

## Why "Sustained Fear" — Not Momentary Fear

The sustained fear element was added to filter out cases where the communication caused only momentary alarm that dissipated quickly. A person who hears a threat and immediately concludes the threatener is joking or blowing off steam has not experienced "sustained fear" within the meaning of § 422.

Courts assess sustained fear through behavioral evidence: did the victim change their routine, seek law enforcement assistance, implement security measures, or otherwise alter their conduct based on the threat? These behavioral changes demonstrate that the fear persisted.

---

## Application in Practice

Section 422 is most frequently charged in cases involving:
- Intimate partner violence where one partner threatens the other during or after separation;
- Workplace threats following termination or conflict;
- Online harassment campaigns targeting specific individuals;
- Threats made in the context of ongoing civil litigation, custody disputes, or financial disputes;
- Threats by defendants already subject to protective orders (often charged alongside § 166 contempt).

The criminal threat charge carries a "strike" designation under California's Three Strikes Law — a felony § 422 conviction counts as a serious felony under Pen. Code § 1192.7(c)(38). This significantly increases the stakes and should inform plea negotiations.

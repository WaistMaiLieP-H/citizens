# Pen. Code § 422 — Criminal Threats

## What this statute does

Section 422 makes it a wobbler offense (misdemeanor or felony) to willfully make a true threat of death or great bodily injury with the specific intent it be taken as a threat, where the threat causes the victim to be in sustained fear. It is a "bilateral" standard: the steward can face a § 422 charge (if one is manufactured), and the steward can use § 422 as the basis for a criminal complaint against those who threaten him.

## Elements (CALCRIM No. 1300)

All five elements must be proven beyond a reasonable doubt:

**1. Willfully threatened to commit a crime resulting in death or great bodily injury**
- The threat must be to commit a crime — not merely to "hurt" someone in a vague sense, but specifically to commit a criminal act that would cause death or GBI
- "Willfully" = acted with purpose (not accidental or reckless)

**2. Specific intent that the statement be taken as a threat**
- § 422 requires specific intent — the defendant must have intended the communication to be received as a threat
- Even if there is no intent to actually carry out the threat, the specific intent to threaten is required
- General intent to communicate is not enough

**3. Made verbally, in writing, or by electronic communication**
- § 422 covers all communication forms: oral, written, text message, email, social media, phone call, video
- Electronic communications are specifically included and broadly defined

**4. The threat was unequivocal, unconditional, immediate, and specific (the "UUIS" test)**
- This is the most contested element
- **Unequivocal:** The threat did not express doubt or uncertainty about whether it would be carried out
- **Unconditional:** The threat did not depend on something else happening first (with some exceptions — see conditional threats below)
- **Immediate:** The threat conveyed that execution was not far off
- **Specific:** The threat identified a particular person or class of persons, or a particular harmful act

**5. Caused sustained fear**
- The victim must have been in fear for his or her own safety or that of immediate family
- Fear must be "sustained" — not merely momentary or fleeting (In re Ryan D. (2002) 100 Cal.App.4th 854)
- Fear must be "reasonable" — an objectively reasonable person in the victim's position would have been afraid
- The fear must actually have been caused by the threat

## The conditional threat problem

Courts have addressed whether conditional threats ("If you do X, I'll kill you") satisfy the "unconditional" element. The California Supreme Court in *People v. Toledo* (2001) 26 Cal.4th 221 held that the "conditional" aspect is one factor in the totality analysis — a threat with a condition can still be a § 422 violation if the other circumstances make it unequivocal, immediate, and specific.

The key question is whether the condition is a genuine limiting condition (e.g., "I'll kill you if you testify" — where the condition gives the victim a way to avoid harm) or merely a linguistic formulation of an unconditional threat.

## The "sustained fear" requirement

"Sustained fear" has been interpreted to require more than a momentary or transient emotional response. Courts have held:
- Fear lasting several minutes can be "sustained"
- The victim's subjective fear is what matters, tested against an objective reasonableness standard
- Evidence of continued fear after the incident (changed locks, left the area, sought restraining order) supports the sustained fear element

## Bilateral use

**As a defense:** If the steward faces a § 422 charge, the UUIS test is the primary battleground. Most fabricated § 422 charges fail on:
- The specificity requirement (vague statements are not specific enough)
- The sustained fear requirement (if the alleged victim continued normal behavior without apparent fear)
- The unconditional/immediate requirement (if the statement was contextual or conditional)

**As a complainant:** If threats have been made against the steward (by Christina, Ryan McClaran, or others in the orchestration), the steward can use § 422 as the basis for a criminal complaint to the DA or for a civil harassment restraining order (Civ. Code § 527.6). Threats communicated via SIM-swapped phones or digital communications still fall within § 422's "electronic communication device" definition.

## Sentencing

- **Misdemeanor:** Up to 1 year county jail
- **Felony:** 16 months, 2, or 3 years state prison (standard triad)
- **Wobbler:** Charged as either; wobbler analysis at preliminary hearing or sentencing

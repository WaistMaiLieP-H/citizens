# Pen. Code § 278.5 — Custody/Visitation Deprivation

**Popular name:** Custodial interference; visitation deprivation; "parental kidnapping" (related but not identical)
**Location:** Penal Code, Part 1, Title 9, Chapter 2 (Abandonment and Neglect of Children)
**Companion sections:** §§ 277 (definitions for custody crimes), 278 (abduction by person without custody right), 279 (punishment enhancement), Fam. Code §§ 3048 (kidnapping prevention orders), 3134 (custody enforcement)

## What § 278.5 does

Section 278.5 criminalizes the taking, enticing, keeping, withholding, or concealing of a child in a way that maliciously deprives a lawful custodian of custody or a lawful visitor of visitation. It is a wobbler — charged as either a misdemeanor (county jail up to 1 year, fine up to $1,000) or a felony (16 months, 2, or 3 years, fine up to $10,000).

## Elements

1. **Taking, enticing, keeping, withholding, or concealing** — any of the five acts is sufficient
2. **A child** — any person under 18
3. **Maliciously** — with the scienter described in PC § 7(4) (intent to vex, annoy, harm, or injure)
4. **Depriving a lawful custodian** of a right to custody OR a person of a right to visitation
5. **Without a complete defense** — note subd. (c) which specifically forecloses the "I got a later custody order" defense

## The key subd. (c) rule

Subdivision (c) is critical in contested cases: a custody order obtained AFTER the taking does not cure the criminal act. This closes a common loophole where a parent would take the child and then rush to get a favorable custody order to retroactively legitimize the act. Under subd. (c), the custody order does not purge the criminal liability for the period before the order.

## Relationship to § 278

Section 278 covers "malicious taking" by a person without a right to custody. Section 278.5 covers deprivation of custody/visitation rights, which can include taking by a person WITH a partial custody right (e.g., during a visitation period) who then exceeds that right. The two statutes can overlap and are often charged alternatively.

## Defense theories

1. **No malice** — the taking was in good faith (e.g., to protect the child from imminent harm; see Fam. Code § 3048(b) defense for DV victims)
2. **Lawful custodian status** — the defendant was the lawful custodian at the relevant time, not depriving another
3. **No deprivation** — the custodian/visitor was not actually deprived (access was maintained through alternative means)
4. **Protective custody defense** — taking was necessary to protect the child from abuse; codified in some circumstances (PC § 278.7 good-faith belief defense, specific to parents fleeing abuse)
5. **Statute of limitations** — SOL calculation depends on when the deprivation ended, not when it began

## The § 278.7 good-faith belief defense

Pen. Code § 278.7 creates a defense for parents who take a child based on a good-faith belief that the child is in immediate danger of abuse. The defense has specific procedural requirements:
- Immediate filing with law enforcement or family court
- Reporting within a specified timeframe
- Continuing cooperation

This defense is narrow but important in contested custody cases with domestic violence allegations.

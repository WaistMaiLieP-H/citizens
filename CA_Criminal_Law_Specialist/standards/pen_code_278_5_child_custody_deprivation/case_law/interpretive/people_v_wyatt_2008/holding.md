## The doctrinal proposition

The "maliciously" element in § 278.5(a) requires more than the intent to commit the physical act of taking, keeping, or concealing. It requires the specific intent to deprive the lawful custodian of the right to custody or visitation. Three propositions flow from this:

1. **Intent to deprive, not intent to harm.** The defendant need not wish harm to the custodian. The malice element is satisfied by the intent to cause the deprivation, regardless of motive.

2. **Knowledge of the custody order is a prerequisite.** A parent who does not know of the custody order cannot form the specific intent to deprive the other parent of custody under that order. Prosecutors typically prove knowledge through service of process, court appearances, or circumstantial evidence.

3. **A sincere but mistaken belief in one's own custodial rights is a defense.** If the defendant honestly believed they had a legal right to keep the child (e.g., based on a misunderstanding of a modified order), the malice element may be defeated. But the mistake must be a mistake of fact, not a mistake of law — the defendant cannot simply disagree with the order's validity.

## Why this matters

Section 278.5's malice element is the primary battlefield in contested custody interference cases. Prosecutors must prove the defendant intended to deprive; defendants often respond with mistake of fact, confusion about the order, or assertions that the taking was temporary and not intended to permanently deprive.

The § 278.7 good-faith DV defense sits alongside the malice element as an independent defense. A defendant who took the child to protect them from abuse may not have had the specific intent to deprive (malice argument) AND may independently qualify for the § 278.7 defense (statutory defense). Both can be asserted.

## Relevance to the Hartmann matter

For the Hartmann case, the malice element is the key battleground in any § 278.5 analysis. The defense would focus on:

1. Whether the defendant knew the specific terms of the custody/visitation order
2. Whether the defendant's conduct was intended to deprive, or was a mistaken understanding of their rights
3. Whether any § 278.7 good-faith DV defense circumstances apply
4. Whether the alleged "deprivation" actually occurred (the custodian maintained access through alternative means)

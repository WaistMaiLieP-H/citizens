# People v. Campos (1982) 131 Cal.App.3d 894

**Citation:** People v. Campos (1982) 131 Cal.App.3d 894, 182 Cal.Rptr. 698
**Decided:** 1982
**Court:** California Court of Appeal
**Disposition:** Affirmed conviction; construed early version of custody deprivation statute.
**Statute construed:** Predecessor to Pen. Code § 278.5 (and the broader § 278 custody interference framework)

## Facts

The defendant, a non-custodial parent, took his minor child from the custodial parent's home without permission and kept the child in another state for an extended period. When the custodial parent sought enforcement, the defendant argued he was acting in the child's best interest because he believed the custodial parent was unfit. The defendant was convicted of custodial interference and appealed, arguing that his good-faith belief about the child's welfare was a complete defense.

## Holding

The Court of Appeal affirmed, establishing propositions that have carried forward into § 278.5 practice:

1. **Subjective good-faith belief is not a complete defense.** A parent who takes a child without the consent of the lawful custodian cannot defeat criminal liability by claiming good-faith belief that the taking was in the child's best interest. The proper remedy for concerns about the custodial parent's fitness is a motion to modify custody in family court, not self-help removal.

2. **Malice is proved by the intentional act of deprivation.** The "malice" element of the custody deprivation statutes does not require ill will toward the other parent. It requires only the intent to commit the act of depriving the custodian (or visitor) of the right — i.e., knowing that the act will cause the deprivation and doing it anyway.

3. **The custodial parent's rights flow from the court order, not from the subjective judgment of the other parent.** Even if the non-custodial parent sincerely believes the custody order is wrong, the criminal statutes protect the order as it exists, not as the non-custodial parent wishes it existed.

## Why this is load-bearing

*Campos* is a founding case for the principle that the California criminal custody-interference statutes are not a tool for parents to self-correct custody orders they disagree with. The remedy for a bad custody order is to return to family court — not to take the child and flee. This principle was later codified in § 278.5(c), which provides that a custody order obtained after the taking does not cure the criminal act.

The narrow exception to *Campos* is § 278.7, enacted after *Campos*, which creates a limited good-faith defense for parents fleeing actual domestic violence. That defense has strict procedural requirements (immediate reporting, court filing) and is only available in specific circumstances. *Campos*'s general rule remains: the criminal custody statutes do not recognize self-help remedies.

## Relevance to the Hartmann matter

For the Hartmann case, *Campos* matters in both directions:

1. **Defensive** — If the steward is ever accused of custody interference, the *Campos* rule is the starting point. The defense must either (a) establish that the custody order was complied with, or (b) invoke the § 278.7 good-faith DV defense with its strict procedural requirements, or (c) challenge one of the other elements (malice, child status, etc.).

2. **Analytic** — If the steward is analyzing potential § 278.5 liability on the other side, *Campos* supports the proposition that a parent's subjective beliefs about the fitness of the other parent do not defeat criminal liability for self-help custody changes. This cuts in favor of criminal accountability when the facts support it.

## Authority status

Published California Court of Appeal decision. Cited as foundational authority in the custody interference case law. Codified in § 278.5(c)'s no-later-order rule. Still good law for its core propositions.

PEOPLE v. CAMPOS
131 Cal.App.3d 894 (1982)
California Court of Appeal
Decided: 1982

---

FACTS:

The defendant, a non-custodial parent, took his minor child from the custodial parent's home without permission and kept the child in another state for an extended period. When the custodial parent sought enforcement, the defendant argued he was acting in the child's best interest because he believed the custodial parent was unfit. He was convicted of custodial interference and appealed, arguing that his good-faith belief about the child's welfare was a complete defense.

---

HOLDING:

§ 278.5 — NO SELF-HELP REMEDY; MALICE ELEMENT:

1. **Subjective good-faith belief is not a complete defense.** A parent who takes a child without the consent of the lawful custodian cannot defeat criminal liability by claiming good-faith belief that the taking was in the child's best interest. The proper remedy for concerns about the custodial parent's fitness is a motion to modify custody in family court — not self-help removal.

2. **Malice is proved by the intentional act of deprivation.** The "malice" element of the custody deprivation statutes does not require ill will toward the other parent. It requires only the intent to commit the act of depriving the custodian of the right — i.e., knowing that the act will cause the deprivation and doing it anyway.

3. **The custodial parent's rights flow from the court order, not from the other parent's subjective judgment.** Even if the non-custodial parent sincerely believes the custody order is wrong, the criminal statutes protect the order as it exists, not as the non-custodial parent wishes it existed.

---

WHY CAMPOS IS LOAD-BEARING:

Campos is the founding case for the principle that the California criminal custody-interference statutes are not a tool for parents to self-correct custody orders they disagree with. This principle was later codified in § 278.5(c), which provides that a custody order obtained after the taking does not cure the criminal act.

The narrow exception post-Campos is § 278.7, enacted after this case, which creates a limited good-faith defense for parents fleeing actual domestic violence. That defense has strict procedural requirements (immediate reporting, court filing) and is only available in specific circumstances. Campos's general rule remains: the criminal custody statutes do not recognize self-help remedies.

---

APPLICATION TO STEWARD'S CASES:

DEFENSIVE — If the steward is ever accused of custody interference:
- The Campos rule is the starting point. The defense must either: (a) establish that the custody order was complied with, or (b) invoke the § 278.7 good-faith DV defense with its strict procedural requirements, or (c) challenge one of the other elements (malice, child status, etc.)

ANALYTIC — If analyzing potential § 278.5 liability on the other side:
- Campos supports the proposition that a parent's subjective beliefs about the fitness of the other parent do not defeat criminal liability for self-help custody changes
- This cuts in favor of criminal accountability when the documented facts show coordinated interference with custody orders

# Historical Chain — Penal Code §§ 859, 859b, 872 (Preliminary Hearing)

## Origin

**1872 — Preliminary examination encoded in original California Penal Code**
The preliminary examination (preliminary hearing) has existed in California law since 1872, tracking English common law requirements for magistrate review before a felony defendant could be committed to trial. The magistrate's function — independent probable cause determination — was designed to protect defendants from groundless prosecutions.

**Historical loss driving the requirement:** Without preliminary examination, any complaint could send a defendant to trial regardless of evidentiary foundation. The preliminary hearing creates a judicial screen between arrest and trial.

## Material Amendments

**1872 — Original § 859**
The original § 859 established the arraignment and counsel rights framework. Right to counsel at arraignment was recognized from the original codification.

**1990 — Proposition 115 (Crime Victims Justice Reform Act)**
The most significant amendment to the preliminary hearing framework in California history. Prop 115 added the § 872(b) hearsay rule — permitting law enforcement officers to testify to out-of-court statements at preliminary hearings, enabling "one-witness prelims." This dramatically reduced the evidentiary burden on the prosecution at the preliminary hearing stage.

**Pre-Prop 115:** All witnesses had to testify in person; hearsay was inadmissible; the preliminary hearing was a full adversarial proceeding.

**Post-Prop 115:** A single officer can summarize hearsay statements of all witnesses; actual witnesses need not appear; the adversarial nature of the preliminary hearing was substantially weakened.

**Historical loss from Prop 115:** Defense attorneys lost the ability to lock in witness testimony early, cross-examine actual witnesses at the preliminary stage, and expose inconsistencies before trial. The one-witness prelim insulates witnesses from early cross-examination.

**1990 — § 859b timing requirements codified**
The 10-court-day and 60-court-day rules were formalized alongside Prop 115, establishing clear timing rights with automatic dismissal as the remedy for violation. These timing rules protected defendants from indefinite pre-trial detention without preliminary hearing.

## Current Statute Version

§§ 859, 859b, and 872 as currently codified (2025) reflect the Prop 115 framework. The hearsay rule in § 872(b) has not been amended since 1990. The timing rules in § 859b have been stable since 1990 with minor technical amendments.

**Consistency check:** All case law on the one-witness prelim, defense subpoena power, and hearsay admissibility at preliminary hearings applies directly to current proceedings.

# Brady v. Maryland (1963) 373 U.S. 83

**Citation:** Brady v. Maryland (1963) 373 U.S. 83, 83 S.Ct. 1194, 10 L.Ed.2d 215
**Court:** Supreme Court of the United States
**Decided:** May 13, 1963
**Statutes construed:** U.S. Const. Amend. XIV (due process); incorporated in Cal. Pen. Code § 1054.1(e)

## Facts

John Brady and a companion were convicted of murder in Maryland. Before trial, Brady's attorney requested that the prosecution provide any extrajudicial statements made by Brady's companion. The prosecution complied but withheld one statement in which the companion admitted to actually committing the killing. Brady was convicted and sentenced to death. After conviction, the withheld statement was discovered.

## Holding

The Supreme Court held:

1. **The suppression by the prosecution of evidence favorable to an accused upon request violates due process where the evidence is material either to guilt or to punishment.** This is the core Brady rule — it is not limited to exculpatory evidence about guilt but also extends to evidence material to sentencing.

2. **The rule applies regardless of the good faith or bad faith of the prosecution.** The prosecution's intent to suppress does not determine the Brady violation. If the evidence is favorable and material, suppression is unconstitutional regardless of why it was withheld.

3. **"Material" evidence is evidence that creates a reasonable probability of a different outcome.** If the suppressed evidence, had it been disclosed, would have created a reasonable probability that the defendant would have been acquitted (or received a lesser sentence), the Brady violation is complete.

4. **The Brady obligation is affirmative — it does not require a defense request.** Although Brady involved a request, subsequent cases (Agurs, 1976) clarified that the obligation exists even without a specific request. The prosecution must affirmatively search for and disclose material favorable evidence.

5. **Brady applies to impeachment evidence as well as exculpatory evidence.** Giglio v. United States (1972) 405 U.S. 150 extended Brady to impeachment evidence — any deal, promise, or benefit given to a prosecution witness in exchange for testimony must be disclosed.

## The materiality standard

Subsequent decisions have refined Brady's materiality standard:

- **Strickler v. Greene (1999) 527 U.S. 263:** Three elements of a Brady violation: (1) the evidence is favorable to the accused (exculpatory or impeaching); (2) it was suppressed by the prosecution either willfully or inadvertently; (3) prejudice resulted — meaning the evidence was material (reasonable probability of different outcome if disclosed).

- **Kyles v. Whitley (1995) 514 U.S. 419:** Brady materiality is assessed by looking at the cumulative effect of all suppressed evidence, not each item in isolation. Even individually minor suppressions can combine to create a Brady violation.

- **United States v. Agurs (1976) 427 U.S. 97:** Prosecution cannot hide behind a failure-to-request defense. If the prosecution knows of material exculpatory evidence, it must disclose regardless of whether the defense asked.

## Why this is load-bearing

*Brady* is the constitutional floor beneath every criminal discovery case. In California, § 1054.1(e) codifies Brady, but *Brady* operates independently as a constitutional mandate. If the prosecution withholds material exculpatory evidence:

1. The conviction may be reversed on direct appeal
2. The conviction may be attacked via habeas corpus (federal § 2254 petition if state remedies exhausted)
3. A new trial may be granted under Pen. Code § 1181
4. In egregious cases, charges may be dismissed under the court's inherent authority

## Relevance to case #7 (04-23-01959)

Brady material in the Contra Costa child abuse case includes:
- Any prior reports involving the same child and similar allegations that were not sustained
- Any CPS notes showing inconsistencies in the child's account
- Any evidence that the reporting party (Christina or another person) had a motive to fabricate
- Any APD officer communications showing coordination with parties to the family court proceedings
- Any exculpatory physical evidence (e.g., medical records showing no injury consistent with the allegation)
- Any prior false report history of the reporting witness under § 148.5

The defense must file a specific Brady demand listing each of these categories. If the prosecution fails to disclose after a specific demand, the suppression element of the Brady violation is clear.

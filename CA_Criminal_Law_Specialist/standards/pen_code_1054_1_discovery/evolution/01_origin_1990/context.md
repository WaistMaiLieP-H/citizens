# Context — § 1054.1 Origin (Proposition 115, 1990)

## The wound

Before Prop 115, California criminal discovery was governed entirely by case law — primarily *Pitchess v. Superior Court* (1974) for peace officer records, and a patchwork of appellate decisions. There was no statutory entitlement to pre-trial disclosure of prosecution evidence. Defense attorneys had to file motion after motion to obtain basic information like witness lists, statements, and scientific reports.

The prosecution's reciprocal obligation was even more unclear: defense attorneys sometimes received evidence the morning of trial. The system was inefficient, created surprise-trial dynamics, and disadvantaged defendants who could not afford attorneys skilled in pre-trial motion practice.

## What Prop 115 did

Prop 115 enacted the comprehensive statutory discovery framework (§§ 1054–1054.7) that codified the prosecution's disclosure obligations and created the reciprocal defense obligation. The specific innovation of § 1054.1 was making disclosure mandatory: the prosecution SHALL disclose, not "may," not "upon motion."

The six categories in § 1054.1 represent the core of constitutional Brady doctrine (Brady v. Maryland, 1963) plus additional California-specific requirements (witness lists, real evidence, expert reports).

## The § 1054.1(e) exculpatory evidence mandate

§ 1054.1(e)'s "any exculpatory evidence" requirement is California's codification of Brady. The prosecution's failure to disclose exculpatory evidence is a § 1054.1 violation, a Brady violation, AND grounds for a § 995 motion to dismiss or a post-conviction petition for habeas.

## Steward relevance

In Case #7 (04-23-01959), § 1054.1 creates the right to receive: witness list (who the prosecution will call), all exculpatory evidence (including prior inconsistent statements, records of APD misconduct, family law records showing the contested custody narrative), real evidence, and expert reports. § 1054.5 is the enforcement mechanism.

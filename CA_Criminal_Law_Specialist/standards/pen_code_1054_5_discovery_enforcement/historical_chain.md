# Historical Chain — Penal Code § 1054.5 (Discovery Enforcement)

## Origin

**1990 — § 1054.5 enacted as part of Proposition 115 ("Crime Victims Justice Reform Act")**
Like § 1054.1, § 1054.5 was created by Proposition 115 in June 1990. The Prop 115 framework created a reciprocal criminal discovery system for the first time in California — prior to 1990, criminal discovery obligations were primarily one-directional and governed by case law rather than statute.

The enforcement provision (§ 1054.5) was a critical component of the Prop 115 scheme. Without it, the disclosure obligations in § 1054.1 would be unenforceable except through the cumbersome common law mandamus process. The Legislature — via the initiative — gave trial courts a statutory toolkit for immediate enforcement.

**Historical loss driving enactment:** Pre-Prop 115, defense attorneys had no statutory mechanism to compel disclosure. Brady v. Maryland (1963) established the constitutional right but provided no California-specific enforcement tool. Defense motions to compel were ad hoc and courts handled them inconsistently. Proposition 115 was the voters' response to this gap.

## Material Amendments and Judicial Construction

**1990 — Original enactment:** § 1054.5 as enacted contained the three-subdivision structure now in effect. The informal request requirement, the 15-day period, the sanction menu, and the exhaustion requirement for witness exclusion were all part of the original 1990 text.

**Post-1990 — California Supreme Court on dismissal:** Courts consistently held that § 1054.5(c)'s bar on dismissal did not eliminate Brady-based dismissal claims. The statutory bar applies to *statutory* discovery violations — constitutional Brady violations remain available for dismissal where the evidence meets the materiality standard.

**People v. Gonzales (2012) 54 Cal.4th 1234:** The California Supreme Court addressed the § 1054.5 sanction framework in the context of prosecutor misconduct. The court confirmed that trial courts have broad discretion in selecting sanctions, and that the sanction must be tailored to the harm — exclusion of witnesses is a severe sanction reserved for severe violations.

**Untimely disclosure jurisprudence:** Courts have consistently held that late disclosure — even if ultimately made before trial — triggers the court's authority to impose sanctions including the jury advisory. The statute does not forgive untimely disclosure simply because it occurred before trial. Late production may still entitle the defendant to a continuance to prepare for the newly disclosed evidence.

## Current Statute Version

§ 1054.5 as enacted in 1990 remains in effect without substantive amendment. The informal request requirement, 15-day period, and sanction menu are unchanged.

**Consistency check:** All case law construing § 1054.5 applies to the current statute without amendment-based concerns.

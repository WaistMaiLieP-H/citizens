# People v. Gonzales (2012) 54 Cal.4th 1234

**Citation:** People v. Gonzales (2012) 54 Cal.4th 1234, 144 Cal.Rptr.3d 757, 281 P.3d 834
**Court:** Supreme Court of California
**Decided:** 2012
**Statutes construed:** Penal Code § 1054.5 — sanction framework for discovery violations; scope of trial court discretion

## Facts

Multiple defendants were convicted in a complex case involving prosecutorial misconduct including late disclosure of discovery materials. At issue was whether the trial court properly exercised its discretion in selecting sanctions under § 1054.5(b) and whether the sanctions were adequate to address the prosecution's disclosure failures.

## Holding

The California Supreme Court held:

1. **Trial courts have broad discretion in selecting § 1054.5 sanctions.** The sanction menu in § 1054.5(b) is not hierarchical — the trial court may impose any combination of available sanctions appropriate to remedy the specific harm from the disclosure failure. The court need not exhaust lesser sanctions before imposing stronger ones (except for witness exclusion, which requires exhaustion under § 1054.5(c)).

2. **The sanction must be tailored to the harm.** The selected sanction must bear a reasonable relationship to the prejudice caused by the disclosure failure. An overly harsh sanction — one that produces a windfall for the defendant beyond what is needed to cure the prejudice — may itself constitute error. An inadequate sanction — one that fails to cure actual prejudice — is also error.

3. **Witness exclusion is available only after other sanctions are exhausted.** Section 1054.5(c) codifies a last-resort rule for witness exclusion. The trial court must make findings that other available sanctions (continuance, jury advisory, contempt) are insufficient before excluding a witness.

4. **Dismissal is not available for statutory discovery violations.** Section 1054.5(c) bars dismissal for noncompliance with the statutory discovery chapter unless required by the Constitution. A Brady constitutional violation that caused actual prejudice remains a separate basis for relief, but § 1054.5 itself does not authorize dismissal.

5. **Continuance is the preferred remedy for late disclosure.** Where the prosecution discloses late but the defense has time to review and prepare before trial, a continuance fully cures the prejudice. Courts should prefer continuance to evidence exclusion where continuance is adequate.

## Why This Is Load-Bearing for § 1054.5 Applications

*Gonzales* is the California Supreme Court's definitive statement on the § 1054.5 sanction framework. It establishes:
- Broad judicial discretion → the motion should ask for multiple sanctions in the alternative
- Harm-tailoring requirement → the motion must identify the specific prejudice from each disclosure failure
- Exhaustion required only for witness exclusion → other sanctions may be combined freely
- Constitutional Brady claim preserved → where prosecution withholds evidence in bad faith, the constitutional claim survives alongside the statutory claim

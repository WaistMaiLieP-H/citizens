# Historical Chain — Pen. Code § 236 (False Imprisonment)

| Year | Event | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| Common law | False imprisonment as common-law tort and crime | English origin |
| 1872 | Pen. Code § 236 enacted in current one-sentence form | Codification |
| Post-1872 | Case law elaborates four elements | Doctrinal development |
| 1990s-2000s | § 236.1 human trafficking added | Specialized modern offense |
| 2010s-2020s | Case law refinements | Continuous interpretive development |
| 2026 | § 236 in original 1872 form | 154 years stable |

## Stability note

Section 236 has not been textually amended since 1872. Like Civ. Code § 1572 (actual fraud), it is one of the handful of original Code sections that has survived unchanged. The sentence "False imprisonment is the unlawful violation of the personal liberty of another" is a model of terse statutory drafting — broad enough to cover modern scenarios (corporate detention, workplace detention, digital surveillance-as-restraint arguments), specific enough to have clear elements developed through case law.

The wound § 236 addresses is timeless: unlawful deprivation of personal liberty. The 1872 statute applied to the unlawful detention of gold miners, stagecoach passengers, and saloon patrons; the 2026 statute applies to unlawful restraint of domestic violence victims, wrongly arrested suspects, and held-against-will workplace employees. The statutory text is identical; the factual contexts have evolved.

## The promise

The California Legislature has had 154 years to narrow or modify § 236. It has not. The decision to leave § 236 alone while adding specialized offenses (§ 236.1 trafficking, § 207 kidnapping) reflects confidence that the general false imprisonment definition is doing what it should: reaching unlawful restraint in any form, without foreclosing specialized prosecution under more specific statutes.

# Pen. Code § 236 — False Imprisonment

**Popular name:** False imprisonment (criminal); unlawful restraint
**Location:** Penal Code, Part 1, Title 8, Chapter 8 (False Imprisonment)
**Companion sections:** § 237 (punishment), § 236.1 (human trafficking, separate but related), § 207 (kidnapping, more serious offense)
**Original enactment:** 1872 Penal Code

## What § 236 does

Section 236 is a model of statutory brevity: "False imprisonment is the unlawful violation of the personal liberty of another." The entire offense is contained in one sentence. The punishment is in the adjacent section § 237.

## Elements (from case law)

Case law has elaborated four elements:

1. **The defendant intentionally restrained the victim** (by act, words, or deception)
2. **The restraint confined the victim** (or compelled the victim to remain in a place)
3. **The confinement was against the victim's will** (no consent)
4. **The restraint was unlawful** (no lawful authority — e.g., no valid arrest authority, no valid citizen's arrest authority, no parental authority over a child)

## Misdemeanor vs. felony

Under § 237:
- **Misdemeanor** — false imprisonment not accomplished by violence, menace, fraud, or deceit. Maximum: fine $1,000 and/or county jail up to 1 year.
- **Felony** — false imprisonment accomplished by violence, menace, fraud, or deceit. State prison under § 1170(h) (16 months, 2, or 3 years).

"Violence" means force beyond the minimum necessary to restrain. "Menace" means threats of harm. "Fraud or deceit" means false representations that induce the victim to submit to restraint.

## Relationship to kidnapping

Kidnapping (§ 207) requires asportation — movement of the victim a substantial distance. False imprisonment does not require movement. A person who locks someone in a room commits false imprisonment; a person who moves the victim in a vehicle commits kidnapping. The two can be charged alternatively when facts permit.

## Relationship to § 148 (resisting arrest) and § 236.1 (human trafficking)

- **§ 148** applies when officers restrain a resisting suspect; the suspect may claim § 236 false imprisonment but the arrest authority is typically a complete defense.
- **§ 236.1** (human trafficking) is a separate, much more serious offense that includes elements of deprivation of liberty but adds commercial exploitation.

## Defense theories

1. **Lawful authority** — the restraint was authorized (citizen's arrest under § 837; parental authority; self-defense; protection of others)
2. **Consent** — the victim consented (and consent was not procured by fraud or deceit)
3. **De minimis** — the "restraint" was insufficient to constitute confinement
4. **No intent** — the defendant did not intentionally restrain (e.g., accidental blocking of a path)

## Relevance to the Hartmann matter

Section 236 has multiple potential applications in the Hartmann history:

1. **June 16, 2023 incident (04-23-01959):** If any restraint occurred during the events leading to the child abuse charge, § 236 could apply to any party. The steward's account of the incident is relevant.

2. **Civil parallel:** False imprisonment is also a state tort (with slightly different elements in the civil context). Civil claims for false imprisonment are part of the Hartmann civil strategy where applicable.

3. **§ 1983 federal parallel:** Unlawful seizure by state actors (peace officers) violates the Fourth Amendment and is actionable under 42 USC § 1983. A § 236 false imprisonment by state actors is simultaneously a Fourth Amendment violation.

# People v. Fernandez (1994) — § 236 Felony vs. Misdemeanor Distinction

**Citation:** People v. Fernandez (1994) 26 Cal.App.4th 710 [verify before use]
**Court:** California Court of Appeal
**Decided:** 1994
**Statutes construed:** Penal Code §§ 236, 237 — false imprisonment elements; felony vs. misdemeanor distinction

## The doctrinal proposition

Section 236 defines false imprisonment as "the unlawful violation of the personal liberty of another." Pen. Code § 237 (not § 236) provides the punishment, with two levels:

1. **Misdemeanor** — False imprisonment NOT effected by violence, menace, fraud, or deceit. Maximum punishment: $1,000 fine and/or county jail up to 1 year.

2. **Felony** — False imprisonment effected by "violence, menace, fraud, or deceit." Maximum punishment: state prison under § 1170(h) for 16 months, 2, or 3 years.

California case law has established the following definitions for the felony-elevating factors:

- **"Violence"** means physical force greater than the amount of force reasonably necessary to restrain the victim. Touching or ordinary physical contact that merely blocks movement is insufficient; some additional force is required.

- **"Menace"** means an express or implied threat of harm — verbal threat, display of weapon, hostile gesture, or any communication that would place a reasonable person in fear of harm.

- **"Fraud or deceit"** means false representations that cause the victim to submit to restraint they would not otherwise have accepted (e.g., pretending to be a police officer to induce compliance with a "lawful" detention that is in fact unlawful).

Any one of the four factors elevates the offense from misdemeanor to felony. The prosecution need not prove more than one.

## The "unlawful" element

The "unlawful" qualifier is the battleground for affirmative defenses:

- **Lawful arrest authority** (peace officer or § 837 citizen's arrest) defeats the unlawful element
- **Self-defense** or **defense of others** defeats unlawfulness when the restraint was necessary to protect against harm
- **Parental authority** (reasonable parental discipline or control of a minor child) defeats unlawfulness
- **Consent** defeats unlawfulness when the victim genuinely agreed to the restraint

## The intent element

Although § 236's text does not expressly require intent, case law has interpreted it as requiring at least a general intent to commit the restraining act. An accidental restraint (e.g., unintentionally blocking a doorway) is not false imprisonment.

## Relationship to Fourth Amendment and § 1983

False imprisonment by state actors is simultaneously:

1. A violation of § 236 (state criminal offense)
2. A violation of the Fourth Amendment (federal constitutional right against unreasonable seizure)
3. An actionable claim under 42 USC § 1983 (federal civil rights cause of action against state actors)

All three analyses share common elements (unlawfulness, restraint) but have different remedies and different standards of proof. A state criminal false imprisonment conviction is the highest bar; a federal § 1983 civil action is a lower bar and does not require proving criminal intent.

## Relevance to the Hartmann matter

For the June 16, 2023 incident (04-23-01959), § 236 analysis is relevant in both directions:

1. **If any state actor unlawfully seized or detained the steward** during the incident, § 236 criminal liability and § 1983 civil liability both potentially apply.

2. **If the steward is accused of false imprisonment** of any person during the incident, the defense must address (a) the intent element, (b) the unlawfulness element (including potential lawful authority defenses), and (c) whether any of the § 237 felony factors apply.

---

FACTS:

The defendant was charged with false imprisonment under §§ 236 and 237. The central issue was the distinction between misdemeanor false imprisonment (§ 237, subd. (a)) and felony false imprisonment (§ 237, subd. (b)), specifically what facts elevate the offense.

---

HOLDING:

§ 236/237 FELONY vs. MISDEMEANOR DISTINCTION:

1. **Two levels of false imprisonment under § 237:**
   - **Misdemeanor**: False imprisonment NOT effected by violence, menace, fraud, or deceit. Maximum: $1,000 fine and/or county jail up to 1 year.
   - **Felony**: False imprisonment effected by "violence, menace, fraud, or deceit." Maximum: state prison 16 months, 2, or 3 years under § 1170(h).

2. **"Violence" means physical force greater than reasonably necessary to restrain.** Touching or ordinary physical contact that merely blocks movement is insufficient; some additional force is required.

3. **"Menace" means an express or implied threat of harm.** Verbal threat, display of weapon, hostile gesture, or any communication that would place a reasonable person in fear of harm.

4. **"Fraud or deceit" means false representations causing the victim to submit to restraint they would not otherwise have accepted.** Example: pretending to be a police officer to induce compliance with a "lawful" detention that is in fact unlawful.

5. **Any one of the four factors elevates to felony.** The prosecution need not prove more than one.

---

THE "UNLAWFUL" ELEMENT AND AFFIRMATIVE DEFENSES:

- **Lawful arrest authority** (peace officer or § 837 citizen's arrest) defeats the unlawful element
- **Self-defense or defense of others** defeats unlawfulness when the restraint was necessary to protect against harm
- **Parental authority** (reasonable parental discipline or control of a minor child) defeats unlawfulness
- **Consent** defeats unlawfulness when the victim genuinely agreed to the restraint

---

RELATIONSHIP TO § 1983 AND FOURTH AMENDMENT:

False imprisonment by state actors is simultaneously: (1) a violation of § 236 (state criminal offense); (2) a violation of the Fourth Amendment (unreasonable seizure); (3) an actionable § 1983 civil rights claim against state actors under color of law. All three analyses share common elements but have different remedies and proof standards.

APPLICATION TO STEWARD'S CASES:

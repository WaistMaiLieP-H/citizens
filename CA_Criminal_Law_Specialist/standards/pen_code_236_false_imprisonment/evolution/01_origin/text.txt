Cal. Penal Code § 236 — Origin (1872 Codification)

§ 236 was enacted in the 1872 California Penal Code codification. The original text is essentially identical to the current text, making § 236 one of California's most stable criminal statutes — 154 years with no material amendment:

§ 236 (1872, current):
"False imprisonment is the unlawful violation of the personal liberty of another."

§ 237 provides the punishment:
"(a) False imprisonment is punishable by a fine not exceeding one thousand dollars ($1,000), or by imprisonment in the county jail for not more than one year, or by both that fine and imprisonment. If the false imprisonment is effected by violence, menace, fraud, or deceit, it shall be punishable as a felony."

The one-sentence statute deliberately captures a broad category: any unlawful restriction on another's freedom of movement, without consent and without lawful authority.

Key elements established in 1872:
1. Unlawful — no legal justification
2. Violation — affirmative act or omission creating the restriction
3. Personal liberty — freedom of movement, not just physical location
4. Of another — victim requirement

Source: California Penal Code (1872 Codification). The text has not changed.

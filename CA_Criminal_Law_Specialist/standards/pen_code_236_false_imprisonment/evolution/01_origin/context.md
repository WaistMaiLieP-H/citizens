# Context — § 236 Origin (1872)

## The wound

False imprisonment in English and early American common law protected against the specific harm of unlawful detention — holding a person against their will without legal authority. California's 1872 codification adopted the common law definition in one sentence, deliberately making it broad enough to capture both physical restraint and constructive confinement (threats and psychological coercion that prevent movement without physical force).

## The breadth is the feature

The simplicity of § 236 is intentional. "Unlawful violation of personal liberty" captures: physical locking/restraint, threatening harm to prevent departure, constructive detention through fraud or deceit, and any other mechanism that restricts free movement without consent and lawful authority. Courts have consistently held that brief detentions qualify; there is no minimum duration requirement.

## 154 years of stability

§ 236 has not been materially amended since 1872 — it is one of California's most stable criminal statutes. This stability reflects the drafters' intention to create a general-purpose liberty-protection provision that courts could apply to evolving factual contexts.

## Steward relevance

False imprisonment is relevant both as a counter-claim in the family law / custody context (arrangements that restricted the steward's or children's freedom of movement) and in the IT-surveillance context (constructive confinement through monitored communications and surveilled movement patterns).

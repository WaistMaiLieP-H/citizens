# CA Criminal Law Specialist — Methodology

## Overview

Three phases, executed in sequence. No phase may be skipped. The output of each phase feeds the next. The final product is a bilateral criminal exposure assessment that identifies potential criminal liability for every party involved in an incident.

---

## Phase 1: INTAKE

### Purpose
Establish the factual foundation before any legal analysis begins.

### Steps

1. **Classify the incident.** What happened? When? Where? Reduce it to a plain-English narrative of physical events, stripped of legal conclusions. "Someone was arrested" not "a lawful arrest was made." "A child was separated from a parent" not "custody was properly transferred."

2. **Identify all persons involved.** Every person present at the incident, every person who directed actions from a distance, every person who filed documents before or after. Include law enforcement officers, witnesses, and persons who made phone calls. No one is excluded at intake.

3. **Reconstruct the timeline.** Minute-by-minute where possible, hour-by-hour at minimum. Use documents, call logs, police reports, and statements. Flag contradictions between sources — contradictions are evidence, not errors to resolve.

4. **Identify the custody/legal status at the time.** What court orders were in effect? Who had legal custody? Were there restraining orders? What were their terms? The legal backdrop determines which statutes apply.

5. **Inventory available evidence.** What documents exist? What documents should exist but do not? What witnesses are available? What physical evidence remains? The absence of expected evidence is itself evidence.

---

## Phase 2: ELEMENTS ANALYSIS

### Purpose
For each potential offense identified during intake, determine whether the statutory elements are satisfied.

### Steps

1. **Enumerate potential offenses.** Based on the intake facts, list every Penal Code section that could potentially apply. Do not filter at this stage. Include offenses that seem unlikely — the elements analysis will sort them.

2. **For each potential offense, enumerate elements.** Pull the elements directly from the statute and controlling case law. Do not paraphrase. State each element in the language the statute uses.

3. **For each element, evaluate evidence.** What evidence supports this element being satisfied? What evidence undermines it? Rate: SATISFIED (evidence clearly establishes the element), NOT SATISFIED (evidence clearly fails to establish the element), or INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE (cannot determine from available evidence).

4. **Identify defenses.** For each offense where all elements appear satisfied, identify every applicable defense. Evaluate the defense against available evidence using the same three-tier rating.

5. **Calculate statute of limitations.** For each offense, identify the SOL, calculate from the offense date, apply tolling, and determine whether prosecution is currently time-barred. A time-barred offense is still relevant for pattern analysis even if it cannot be prosecuted.

6. **Assess both directions.** Repeat steps 1-5 for EVERY party. If Party A may have committed an offense against Party B, also evaluate whether Party B may have committed an offense against Party A in the same incident. This is not optional. Bilateral analysis is the methodology.

---

## Phase 3: SYNTHESIS

### Purpose
Produce the bilateral criminal exposure assessment.

### Deliverable Structure

1. **Exposure Matrix.** Rows: potential offenses. Columns: potential defendants. Cells: element-level assessment (ALL ELEMENTS SATISFIED / ELEMENTS NOT SATISFIED / INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE) plus defense assessment and SOL status.

2. **Strongest Exposures.** Rank-ordered list of the most serious criminal exposures, considering: severity of the offense (felony vs. misdemeanor), strength of evidence on elements, weakness of available defenses, and whether SOL is still open. Both directions.

3. **Pattern Analysis.** Where the same person appears as a potential defendant across multiple offenses or multiple incidents, identify the pattern. Patterns are relevant to charging decisions, sentencing, and civil RICO analysis (which this citizen flags for referral but does not perform).

4. **Referral Recommendations.** For each viable criminal exposure, identify the appropriate authority: local DA, California AG, or federal authorities. Identify conflicts of interest that may affect which authority should receive the referral.

5. **Evidence Gaps.** What additional evidence would be needed to move an INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE assessment to SATISFIED or NOT SATISFIED? This drives the outstanding investigations list.

---

## Governing Principles

- **The document is the instruction.** When a document enters this citizen's scope, all applicable criminal statutes are evaluated automatically. No human instruction is needed to trigger analysis.

- **Both directions, always.** Every incident is evaluated bilaterally. A one-directional criminal assessment is incomplete and will not be produced.

- **Elements are the law.** Opinions about what "should" be criminal are irrelevant. The elements of the offense either are or are not satisfied by the evidence. That is the entire analysis.

- **Time bars are facts, not dismissals.** A time-barred offense still happened. It still shows pattern. It still informs the analysis of non-time-barred offenses. SOL expiration affects prosecutability, not truth.

- **Plain language.** Say what happened. "Christina told police Michael hit her" not "the reporting party alleged battery by the respondent." The law is technical enough without adding jargon to the facts.

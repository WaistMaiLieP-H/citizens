# Historical Chain — Origin: CCP § 338 (1872)

## Statutory History

CCP §338 is part of the original California Code of Civil Procedure, enacted in 1872 as part of the Field Code reform movement. David Dudley Field's code codified English common law limitations periods into a single statutory framework for California.

The three-year property damage period (§338(c)) tracks the English common law three-year period for conversion and trespass to chattels. The fraud discovery rule (§338(d)) codified the equity court exception to the statute of limitations — courts of equity had long recognized that a defendant could not invoke a limitations bar when the defendant's own concealment prevented the plaintiff from discovering the cause of action.

## The Fraud Discovery Rule — Equity Origins

The discovery rule in §338(d) comes from English equity. Courts of equity reasoned that a statute of limitations is an affirmative defense that requires "clean hands" — a defendant who concealed the fraud cannot invoke the protections of a statute designed to protect defendants from stale claims when that staleness was created by the defendant's own concealment.

California incorporated this equity exception directly into the statute, making the discovery rule a statutory right rather than a discretionary equitable remedy.

## The 1981 Jolly/Norgart Development

The California Supreme Court's development of the discovery rule in drug injury cases (*Jolly v. Eli Lilly* (1988) 44 Cal.3d 1103) refined the standard from purely subjective knowledge to an objective "should have known" test. This objective test prevents the strategic use of willful ignorance to toll the SOL indefinitely.

## Fine Art Amendments (1983, 2010, 2016)

§338(c)(2)-(6) were added to address fine art theft and Holocaust-era property restitution — separate policy contexts not relevant to product liability or fraud claims. These subdivisions do not affect the operative provisions at §338(c)(1) and §338(d).

# Historical Chain — Origin: Escola Concurrence (1944)

**Standard:** greenman_strict_products_liability
**Era:** 1944 — Pre-Greenman Groundwork
**Document:** Justice Traynor's concurring opinion in *Escola v. Coca Cola Bottling Co.* (1944) 24 Cal.2d 453

---

## The Seed Case

*Escola v. Coca Cola Bottling Co.* (1944) 24 Cal.2d 453 was a personal injury case involving a Coca-Cola bottle that exploded in a waitress's hand. The majority decided the case on res ipsa loquitur (the negligence inference from the circumstances). Justice Roger Traynor, then an Associate Justice of the California Supreme Court, concurred but argued for going further.

His concurrence is the seed of all product liability law in California and, ultimately, the nation.

## Traynor's Concurring Argument (1944)

Justice Traynor argued that the fundamental premise of negligence — that liability requires proof of fault — was the wrong framework for manufactured products:

> "Even if there is no negligence, public policy demands that responsibility be fixed wherever it will most effectively reduce the hazards to life and health inherent in defective products that reach the market."

He identified three structural features of the product marketplace that made the negligence framework obsolete:

1. **Concealed defects:** Manufacturing defects are concealed within the product, invisible to the consumer and to intermediate sellers. Only the manufacturer knows (or should know) what happened on the production line.

2. **Loss spreading:** The manufacturer, through pricing and insurance, can spread the cost of injuries across the consumer base. Individual injured consumers cannot spread their losses.

3. **Deterrence:** Liability for injury outcomes — not just fault-producing conduct — gives manufacturers a more powerful incentive to invest in safety.

## The 19-Year Gap

Traynor's 1944 concurrence was revolutionary but it was a concurrence — binding on no one. California law continued to require fault (negligence) or contract (warranty) for product injury claims until 1963.

During those 19 years, California consumers injured by defective products continued to face the same barriers: prove the manufacturer's hidden negligence, or lose because you had no privity with the maker.

## 1963: The Concurrence Becomes Law

When *Greenman v. Yuba Power Products* reached the California Supreme Court in 1963, Traynor (now Chief Justice) wrote the majority opinion. He adopted exactly what he had proposed in his 1944 concurrence: strict tort liability for defective products, independent of negligence and independent of contract.

The 1944 concurrence became the 1963 rule.

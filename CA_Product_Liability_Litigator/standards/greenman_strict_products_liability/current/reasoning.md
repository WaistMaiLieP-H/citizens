# Greenman Strict Products Liability — Reasoning and Historical Loss Analysis

## Why This Standard Exists

Before *Greenman* (1963), an injured consumer had two routes to hold a manufacturer accountable:

**Route 1: Negligence.** Prove the manufacturer was careless — failed to exercise ordinary care in design, manufacture, or inspection. The barrier: manufacturers controlled their own production processes. Proving internal negligence was nearly impossible without discovery into the factory floor. Injured plaintiffs regularly lost not because the product was safe, but because they couldn't prove fault.

**Route 2: Breach of Warranty.** Use contract law — the product didn't conform to its implied or express warranty. The barrier: warranty required **privity of contract** (you had to have bought directly from the manufacturer). By the mid-20th century, complex distribution chains separated consumers from manufacturers by multiple layers of distributors and retailers. Most injured consumers had no privity.

Both routes left the same gap: **a defective product could injure someone and the manufacturer walked away because the injured person couldn't prove fault or had no contract with the maker.**

Justice Traynor saw this as a failure of law to match social reality. In his 1944 concurring opinion in *Escola v. Coca Cola Bottling Co.* (1944) 24 Cal.2d 453, he wrote:

> "I believe the manufacturer's negligence should no longer be singled out as the basis of a plaintiff's right to recover in cases like the present one. In my opinion it should now be recognized that a manufacturer incurs an absolute liability when an article that he has placed on the market, knowing that it is to be used without inspection, proves to have a defect that causes injury to human beings."

Nineteen years later, *Greenman* made that concurrence the law.

## The Policy Rationale

Three interlocking reasons:

**1. Risk Allocation.** Manufacturers are in the best position to prevent product defects — they design, build, and test the product. They are also in the best position to spread the cost of injuries through insurance and pricing. Injured consumers are in the worst position: they can't inspect what they can't see, can't test what they can't build, and can't absorb catastrophic injuries the way a corporation can spread risk across millions of units sold.

**2. Incentive Alignment.** If manufacturers bear the cost of defect injuries, they have financial incentive to build safe products. Negligence law gave manufacturers an escape: even a careless manufacturer who got lucky with proof could avoid liability. Strict liability closes the escape: if the product is defective and it causes injury, the cost lands on the manufacturer regardless.

**3. Warranty Privity Is an Anachronism.** The privity rule made sense when a craftsman sold goods directly to a customer who could inspect them. It makes no sense when a product travels from manufacturer to national distributor to regional warehouse to retail store before reaching the consumer. The consumer has no ability to inspect and no legal relationship with the entity that made the dangerous thing.

## What Was Lost Before *Greenman*

**Case study:** *Greenman* itself. William Greenman's wife bought him a Shopsmith combination power tool as a gift. He was using it as a lathe when a piece of wood flew out and struck him in the head. The defect was in the manufacturer's own design — inadequate set screws. Under pre-*Greenman* law:

- He couldn't sue his wife (she wasn't negligent)
- His wife had privity with the retailer, not the manufacturer
- Proving the manufacturer's negligence required getting inside their factory

The manufacturer of a product that maimed William Greenman with its own defective design would have escaped liability entirely.

**The systemic loss:** Before 1963, consumers injured by defective products in California regularly absorbed life-altering injuries because the law protected the entity that caused the harm and abandoned the person harmed. *Greenman* ended this.

## Historical Development After *Greenman*

| Year | Case | Development |
|---|---|---|
| 1963 | *Greenman v. Yuba Power Products* 59 Cal.2d 57 | Strict liability established for manufacturers |
| 1965 | RESTATEMENT (SECOND) OF TORTS §402A | National codification of *Greenman* approach |
| 1970 | *Price v. Shell Oil Co.* 66 Cal.2d 911 | Extended to entire chain of distribution (distributors, retailers, lessors) |
| 1978 | *Barker v. Lull Engineering* 20 Cal.3d 413 | Dual test for design defect; burden shift to defendant under risk-utility |
| 1984 | *Finn v. G.D. Searle* 35 Cal.3d 691 | Failure to warn framework |
| 1994 | *Soule v. General Motors* 8 Cal.4th 548 | Refined consumer expectations prong — cannot be used where product complexity requires expert analysis |
| 2011 | *Bruesewitz v. Wyeth LLC* 562 U.S. 223 | VICP preempts Greenman design defect claims against vaccine MANUFACTURERS (not administering parties) |

## Significance for This Case Record

The SIRVA claim against Walgreens does NOT turn on *Greenman* strict liability for the vaccine itself — that claim is preempted under *Bruesewitz*. However, *Greenman* remains relevant for:

1. **Syringe/needle device defect:** If the administration device was defective, *Greenman* applies to the device manufacturer.
2. **Framing the administering-party distinction:** *Greenman*'s focus on the entity that "places [an article] on the market" supports the argument that Walgreens, as a service provider administering a vaccine, is not "placing" the vaccine on the market — strengthening the case that VICP's manufacturer immunity does not reach Walgreens.
3. **RedJag vehicle defect:** *Greenman* and *Price* support strict liability against any entity in the vehicle's commercial chain for defects in the vehicle as delivered.

# Greenman v. Yuba Power Products, Inc. — Strict Products Liability Rule

**Source:** *Greenman v. Yuba Power Products, Inc.* (1963) 59 Cal.2d 57
**Court:** California Supreme Court
**Author:** Justice Roger J. Traynor
**Retrieved:** 2026-04-13
**Type:** Case law standard (controlling precedent)
**Verified:** true (well-established Supreme Court of California precedent, cited in every California product liability case)

---

## Holding (Operative Rule)

> A manufacturer is strictly liable in tort when an article he places on the market, knowing that it is to be used without inspection for defects, proves to have a defect that causes injury to a human being.
> — 59 Cal.2d 57, 62

## Elements (as formulated in subsequent doctrine)

To establish strict products liability under *Greenman*, the plaintiff must prove:

1. **Product defect** — the product had a defect (manufacturing, design, or failure to warn) at the time it left the defendant's control;
2. **Injury** — the plaintiff suffered physical harm (or property damage);
3. **Causation** — the defect caused the plaintiff's injury;
4. **Intended use** — the plaintiff was using the product in a reasonably foreseeable manner.

The plaintiff does NOT need to prove:
- Negligence (no fault required)
- Privity of contract (no contractual relationship required)
- Prior notice to the manufacturer

## Chain of Distribution

Strict liability extends beyond the original manufacturer to all entities in the commercial chain of distribution, including:
- **Manufacturers** — including component part manufacturers
- **Distributors** — wholesale and intermediate
- **Retailers** — who placed the product in the stream of commerce

*See Price v. Shell Oil Co.* (1970) 66 Cal.2d 911 (extending Greenman to lessors and retailers).

## Key Language

> "[T]o establish the manufacturer's liability it was sufficient that plaintiff proved that he was injured while using the Shopsmith in a way it was intended to be used as a result of a defect in its design and manufacture of which plaintiff was not aware that made the Shopsmith unsafe for its intended use."
> — 59 Cal.2d 57, 64

> "The purpose of such liability is to insure that the costs of injuries resulting from defective products are borne by the manufacturers that put such products on the market rather than by the injured persons who are powerless to protect themselves."
> — 59 Cal.2d 57, 63

## Relation to Warranty

*Greenman* deliberately moved strict liability OUT of warranty law and into independent tort doctrine. Justice Traynor held that strict liability does not require the plaintiff to give notice of breach to the manufacturer as required for warranty claims under the Commercial Code. This makes the tort remedy distinct from and supplementary to COM §2314 warranty claims.

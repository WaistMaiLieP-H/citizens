## Why VICP Was Created — The Policy Trade-Off

By the early 1980s, the DTP (diphtheria-tetanus-pertussis) vaccine was generating a wave of tort litigation in the United States. Injured children and their families sued vaccine manufacturers under Greenman/Barker strict liability and design defect theories.

The manufacturers responded: if we face unlimited tort liability for vaccine injuries — many of which are statistically unavoidable side effects of any vaccination program — we will stop making vaccines.

Several manufacturers exited the vaccine market. The threat to the national vaccination program was real.

**Congress's solution (1986):** The National Childhood Vaccine Injury Act (NCVIA). It created a trade-off:
- Manufacturers get protection from design defect tort claims for unavoidable vaccine side effects
- Injured patients get a no-fault compensation system (VICP) that pays quickly, without having to prove negligence or defect, and without expensive litigation

The VICP was designed to channel vaccine injury claims away from the courts and into an administrative compensation program. The trade-off favored manufacturers — they got broad liability protection — in exchange for a dedicated, publicly funded compensation mechanism for injured patients.

## The Bruesewitz Decision — The Wall That Now Stands

*Bruesewitz v. Wyeth LLC* (2011) 562 U.S. 223 was the Supreme Court's final word on what §300aa-22(b)(1) means. The Bruesewitz family's daughter suffered serious neurological injuries following a DTP vaccine and argued the vaccine was defectively designed. The Court held 6-2 that §300aa-22(b)(1) expressly preempts ALL design defect claims against vaccine manufacturers — including claims that a safer alternative design existed.

The dissent (Sotomayor, Ginsburg) argued that "unavoidable" side effects means the statute only protects against liability for side effects that could not have been prevented by a safer design — i.e., the manufacturer immunity should not cover cases where a safer design was feasible.

The majority rejected this reading. The wall is absolute: no design defect tort against vaccine manufacturers, period.

**The historical loss embedded in Bruesewitz:** Vaccine-injured plaintiffs with strong design defect cases — cases where they could prove the manufacturer knew of a safer design and chose not to implement it — lost the ability to hold manufacturers accountable in court. The VICP's $250,000 pain and suffering cap (well below what some serious vaccine injuries would command in tort) became the only recovery for these plaintiffs.

## The Administering Party Gap — The Wall Has a Gate

The NCVIA and *Bruesewitz* created a wall protecting vaccine manufacturers. But the wall has a gate: it protects only manufacturers, not everyone who touches a vaccine before it enters a patient's body.

A pharmacist who injects a vaccine into the wrong anatomical location is not making a design decision about the vaccine's composition. They are performing a healthcare service — administering the vaccine — and doing it negligently. The pharmacist's negligence is not covered by NCVIA because the NCVIA's liability protections are expressly limited to "vaccine manufacturers."

This gap is not a loophole — it reflects a deliberate structural choice. Congress protected vaccine manufacturers to preserve vaccine availability. Congress did NOT protect vaccine administrators because those entities' negligence (wrong site, wrong dose, wrong patient) has nothing to do with the policy reasons for protecting vaccine manufacturers.

**The practical effect for this case:** The wall that blocks a design defect suit against Moderna/Pfizer has a gate that lets a negligent administration suit against Walgreens through. *Bruesewitz* does not touch the Walgreens claim.

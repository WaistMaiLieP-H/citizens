# Barker Design Defect Dual Test — Reasoning and Historical Loss Analysis

**Standard ID:** barker_design_defect_test
**Citizen:** CA_Product_Liability_Litigator
**Layer:** 3 — Reasoning / Historical Loss
**Build date:** 2026-04-13

---

## Why This Standard Exists

*Greenman* (1963) established strict liability for product defects but did not specify HOW to analyze design defects. That gap mattered because design defect cases are fundamentally different from manufacturing defect cases.

**Manufacturing defect:** Easy to identify. The product deviated from its own blueprint. The question is empirical: did this unit conform to the design specs?

**Design defect:** Much harder. The product worked exactly as designed. The design itself was the problem. But if the product worked as designed, how do you prove it was defective? You need a test for when a design is "defective" even when the product performed as intended.

*Barker* answered that question.

---

## The Pre-*Barker* Problem

Before 1978, California courts struggled with two competing approaches:

**Approach 1 — Consumer Expectations (Warranty Origins):** A product is defective if it fails to perform as safely as an ordinary consumer would expect. This test was intuitive for simple products but useless for complex ones. What does an ordinary consumer expect from the internal geometry of a car's frame during a 35 mph frontal crash?

**Approach 2 — Negligence-Like Risk-Utility:** A design is defective if the risks outweigh the benefits. This test accommodated complex technical products but was functionally identical to negligence analysis — which undermined Greenman's strict liability breakthrough. If the plaintiff had to prove the manufacturer made an unreasonable design choice, strict liability had collapsed back into negligence.

Neither approach alone was adequate. *Barker* solved the problem by keeping BOTH — with a crucial addition: under risk-utility, the burden shifts to the defendant.

---

## The Burden Shift — Why It Matters

The burden shift under the risk-utility prong is the most practically significant feature of *Barker*. Here is why:

In a design defect case, the plaintiff typically cannot access the manufacturer's design records, testing data, or engineering analyses. The manufacturer has all of this. Under ordinary burden rules, the plaintiff would have to prove the manufacturer's design was unreasonable without being able to see the evidence of whether the manufacturer considered the risk, what alternatives were tested, and what the cost of safer alternatives would be.

*Barker* reversed this. Once the plaintiff proves the design caused the injury, the DEFENDANT must show its design was justified. The defendant has the engineering records. The defendant did the testing. The defendant is the right party to bear the burden of production on whether safer alternatives existed and what they cost.

The burden shift transforms the risk-utility prong from a negligence substitute into a genuinely plaintiff-favorable strict liability test.

---

## *Soule* Refinement — 1994

*Soule v. General Motors Corp.* (1994) 8 Cal.4th 548 recognized a limitation on the consumer expectations prong. The plaintiff in *Soule* was injured in a car accident and argued the car's frame design violated consumer expectations. The California Supreme Court held that consumer expectations cannot be used where:

> "The product's design embodies the kind of complex engineering tradeoffs that are beyond the ordinary consumer's experience."

For automobile crash-dynamics, medical device mechanics, aircraft component specifications, and similar complex engineering questions, ordinary consumers simply have no reasonable expectation about the specific technical features at issue. The jury cannot be asked to find that a design violated expectations that consumers never formed.

In these cases, risk-utility is the appropriate test. This does not weaken *Barker* — it channels complex cases to the prong that can actually answer the technical question.

# California Public Records Act — Law Enforcement Exemption — Origin 1968

## The Wound

Before 1968, California had no statute requiring government agencies to disclose public records. Government bodies — including law enforcement agencies — could and routinely did refuse access to records without explanation or legal accountability. Citizens who wanted information about government operations had no legal mechanism to compel disclosure. The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) was enacted by Congress in 1966 but applied only to federal agencies. State government records were essentially at the discretion of state officials.

For law enforcement specifically, opacity was total: investigative files were confidential by agency custom with no legal requirement to disclose anything. Personnel complaints against officers were filed and resolved with no public access. Misconduct investigations proceeded without external scrutiny. When agencies cleared officers of wrongdoing, there was no mechanism for the public to know what occurred or to challenge the investigation's adequacy. The Watts riots (1965) and subsequent civil unrest put the relationship between law enforcement and the public in sharp relief — but there was no information infrastructure to support accountability.

A separate wound developed over time: the law enforcement exemption that was created to protect legitimate operational interests became a reflexive shield for anything a police agency wanted to keep secret. Agencies invoked the exemption for records that had nothing to do with ongoing investigations or security procedures — including records of officer misconduct investigations that were complete, records of disciplinary actions, and records that simply embarrassed the agency. The exemption designed to protect investigations became an exemption that protected agencies.

## The Legislative Response

Stats. 1968, Ch. 1473 enacted the California Public Records Act (CPRA). Based on the federal FOIA model (1966), the CPRA created a presumptive right of access to government records, with enumerated exemptions for specific categories where non-disclosure serves a legitimate government interest. The law enforcement exemption — then codified at Gov. Code § 6254(f) — was included from the start to protect: ongoing criminal investigations, undercover operations, informant identities, and security procedures.

The CPRA was a fundamental shift: the presumption moved from secrecy (government has no obligation to disclose) to transparency (government must disclose unless a specific exemption applies). The burden shifted to the government to justify withholding, not to the requester to justify access.

Over the following decades, the scope of the LE exemption was tested through litigation and legislative reform. The most consequential reform was SB 1421 (2018), which added Penal Code § 832.7(b) — requiring disclosure of specific officer misconduct records notwithstanding the § 7923.600 exemption. SB 1421 reflected legislative recognition that the exemption had become a tool for concealing precisely the kind of serious officer misconduct that the public has the most compelling interest in knowing about.

The 2021 CPRA reorganization (AB 473, Stats. 2021, Ch. 516) moved the exemption from § 6254(f) to § 7923.600 effective January 1, 2023. The reorganization was structural — the text is substantively identical. The significance is that the § 832.7(b) override, enacted in 2018, explicitly names § 7923.600 as the exemption it overrides, confirming that mandatory disclosure of officer misconduct records supersedes the LE exemption.

## What Changed

Before 1968: No public right of access to government records; LE records entirely at agency discretion.

1968-2018: CPRA presumption of access; LE exemption broad; Pitchess (1974) and Evidence Code §§ 1043-1046 as the only judicial mechanism for officer personnel record access.

2019 (SB 1421 effective): § 832.7(b) creates mandatory public disclosure of five categories of officer misconduct records, overriding § 7923.600. The exemption's domain narrows significantly for the most serious misconduct categories.

2022 (SB 16 effective): Two additional categories added (unlawful arrest, unlawful search). The exemption narrows further.

2023: § 6254(f) becomes § 7923.600 in CPRA reorganization. Substantive scope unchanged.

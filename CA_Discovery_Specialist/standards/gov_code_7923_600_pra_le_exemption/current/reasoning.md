# California Public Records Act — Law Enforcement Exemption — Reasoning

## Why This Law Exists

The California Public Records Act rests on the constitutional principle — codified in California Constitution, Art. I, § 3(b) — that access to information concerning the conduct of government is a fundamental right. The CPRA implements that right through a presumption of disclosure: government records are public unless a specific exemption applies.

Section 7923.600 creates the law enforcement exemption because the presumption of disclosure, applied without limit to law enforcement operations, would cause documented harm: revealing an undercover officer's identity, disclosing informant networks, exposing security procedures to those who would exploit them, or prejudicing ongoing criminal investigations by alerting suspects. These are legitimate competing interests, not pretexts.

The exemption is therefore structural: it preserves the presumption of access in all other domains while protecting the operational integrity of law enforcement functions. The question in every CPRA request involving law enforcement records is not "is this exempt?" but "does this record fall within the specific scope of the exemption, or within one of the carve-backs?"

## Legislative Intent

The CPRA was enacted in 1968 based on the federal Freedom of Information Act (1966). The law enforcement exemption was included from the start — the legislature recognized that mandatory disclosure of all investigative files would interfere with prosecution of crimes and endanger individuals. The intent was never to make all LE records secret; it was to protect specific operational information.

The 2021 reorganization (AB 473, Stats. 2021, Ch. 516) moved the exemption from Gov. Code § 6254(f) to § 7923.600 as part of a comprehensive structural revision of the CPRA. The move was not substantive — the scope of the exemption did not change. What changed the scope of effective LE records disclosure was not the 2021 reorganization but SB 1421 (2018), which added § 832.7(b) creating mandatory disclosure of specified officer misconduct categories that override the § 7923.600 exemption.

## Doctrinal Basis

Section 7923.600 is an exemption statute — it must be construed narrowly. California courts apply the principle that CPRA exemptions are read strictly and ambiguity resolves in favor of disclosure. An agency asserting § 7923.600 must demonstrate that the specific record falls within the specific exemption language; it cannot invoke the exemption categorically for any record in the possession of a law enforcement agency.

The interplay between § 7923.600 and § 832.7(b) creates a two-statute framework: § 7923.600 is the default; § 832.7(b) is the express override. Where § 832.7(b) applies (the five mandatory disclosure categories), § 7923.600 is inoperative. The Long Beach Police Officers Association v. City of Long Beach (2014) 59 Cal.4th 59 confirmed the structure of public access rights in this domain.

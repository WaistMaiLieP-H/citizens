# California Criminal Discovery — Chapter 10 — Origin 1990

## The Wound

Before June 5, 1990, California had no codified criminal discovery statute. The only applicable legal framework was Brady v. Maryland (1963) 373 U.S. 83 — the constitutional requirement that prosecutors disclose material exculpatory evidence to the defense. Brady was a constitutional floor enforced after the fact: defendants learned of Brady violations when they discovered withheld evidence, typically after conviction. There was no pre-trial disclosure framework, no witness list requirement, no advance production of expert reports, and no defense disclosure obligation.

The practical consequences were documented and significant:

**Trial by ambush:** Witnesses were identified at trial, not before it. Defendants heard for the first time, on the day of trial, who would testify against them and what they would say. Preparation was impossible. Last-minute continuances disrupted courts, cost money, and delayed justice for victims and defendants alike.

**Brady violations without accountability:** Because the prosecution had no codified disclosure obligation beyond the constitutional floor, discovering what had been withheld required the violation to surface — usually years later in post-conviction proceedings. The absence of a pre-trial disclosure regime meant violations were hard to detect and even harder to remedy in real time.

**No defense disclosure:** The prosecution had no advance notice of alibi witnesses, defense experts, or the scientific evidence the defense intended to offer. The absence of defense disclosure obligations meant surprise was a legitimate defense tactic — and the prosecution had no mechanism to investigate defense witnesses before trial.

**Unequal treatment:** Defendants with resources could use informal networks to gather information. Defendants without resources had no mechanism to compel pre-trial disclosure. The practical inequality of the discovery-free system fell hardest on those who could least afford surprise.

## The Legislative Response

Proposition 115 — the Crime Victims Justice Reform Act — was approved by California voters on June 5, 1990. The initiative was sponsored by the California District Attorneys Association and backed by law enforcement groups, who argued that the defense enjoyed discovery advantages without reciprocal obligations. By placing it on the ballot as a voter initiative, the sponsors achieved a constitutional change: Art. I, § 30 of the California Constitution was amended to permit mutual criminal discovery.

The Proposition added Chapter 10 (§§ 1054–1054.10) to the Penal Code. The legislative intent, expressed in § 1054's five purposes, prioritized: truth-seeking, efficiency, victim and witness protection, and codification — replacing judicial improvisation with statutory structure.

## What Changed

Before Proposition 115: No pre-trial criminal discovery statute in California. Brady constitutional floor only. Defense had no disclosure obligations. Witnesses unknown until trial.

After Proposition 115 (June 1990): Chapter 10 is the exclusive vehicle for criminal discovery. Prosecution must disclose witness lists, statements, real evidence, exculpatory evidence, and expert reports at least 30 days before trial. Defense must disclose defense witnesses, real evidence, and expert reports. Informal request required before court enforcement. Exclusivity clause (§ 1054.5(a)) prevents courts from creating discovery obligations beyond Chapter 10 except as constitutionally compelled by Brady.

The constitutional minimum (Brady) remains and cannot be contracted away by Chapter 10. Where Brady requires disclosure, it overrides Chapter 10. Where Chapter 10 reaches further than Brady, it is a statutory benefit, not a constitutional mandate, and its enforcement remedies (preclusion, contempt, continuance) govern — not automatic reversal.

# California Criminal Discovery — Chapter 10 — Reasoning

## Why This Law Exists

Before 1990, California criminal proceedings operated without a comprehensive statutory discovery framework. The prosecution was obligated only by Brady v. Maryland (1963) to disclose material exculpatory evidence — a constitutional floor, not a comprehensive system. Defense attorneys had no pre-trial access to prosecution witness lists, real evidence catalogs, or expert reports. Prosecution attorneys had no advance notice of alibi witnesses or defense expert opinions. Trials were organized around surprise rather than truth.

The result was systematic inefficiency: mid-trial continuances when evidence was revealed at trial for the first time; witnesses excluded because their existence was disclosed too late; defendants prejudiced by evidence they had no opportunity to investigate. Victims were harmed by delayed proceedings. The criminal justice system's legitimacy suffered.

Chapter 10 exists because both sides — prosecution and defense — benefit from advance disclosure. The prosecution gets defense witness lists and expert reports. The defense gets the prosecution's evidence before trial. Both sides can prepare for what they will face. Trials become shorter, better focused, and more accurate.

## Legislative Intent

Proposition 115 — the Crime Victims Justice Reform Act — was placed on the California ballot by the legislature and approved by voters on June 5, 1990. The initiative added Chapter 10 (§§ 1054-1054.10) to the Penal Code. The legislative intent, expressed in § 1054, has four parts: (a) promote the ascertainment of truth by requiring timely pretrial discovery; (b) save court time by requiring informal discovery before judicial enforcement; (c) save court time in trial and avoid interruptions; (d) protect victims and witnesses from danger and harassment.

The mutual structure — prosecution discloses to defense AND defense discloses to prosecution — was intentional. Prior to Proposition 115, defense-side discovery obligations were essentially nonexistent. The initiative created symmetry: if the defense wants prosecution disclosure, the defense must also disclose. The exclusivity clause (§ 1054.5(a)) ensures that Chapter 10, not judicial improvisation, is the source of criminal discovery obligations in California.

## Doctrinal Basis

Chapter 10 operates above a constitutional floor set by Brady v. Maryland (1963) 373 U.S. 83: the prosecution must disclose material exculpatory evidence as a matter of due process. Chapter 10 builds on Brady by codifying Brady-type disclosure obligations and adding categories (witness lists, expert reports) that Brady does not independently require. The exclusivity clause (§ 1054.5(a)) means Chapter 10 is the ceiling as well as the floor for statutory discovery — courts cannot expand it. But Chapter 10 cannot contract below Brady: if Brady requires disclosure, Chapter 10's limitations do not excuse it.

The Giglio doctrine (Giglio v. United States (1972) 405 U.S. 150) — requiring disclosure of evidence bearing on witness credibility — operates alongside § 1054.1(e)'s exculpatory evidence requirement. A police officer's sustained dishonesty finding (disclosable under § 832.7(b)(1)(C)) is Giglio material — material impeachment evidence that the prosecution must disclose under both constitutional doctrine and § 1054.1(e).

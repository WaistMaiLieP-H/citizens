# Historical Chain — California Code of Civil Procedure § 1985 Subpoena Duces Tecum

## The wound

Before the modern subpoena framework, compelling documents from non-parties required either personal service of a court order or cooperative discovery — both inadequate for large-scale document collection. Records held by banks, hospitals, government agencies, and businesses could not be compelled efficiently, and the notice protections for the persons whose records were sought were minimal or nonexistent.

The specific wound was consumer protection. Banks, medical providers, and employers held sensitive personal records about individuals who had no idea their records were being subpoenaed in litigation they were not parties to. A bank account holder might learn only after the fact that their complete financial records had been turned over to an adverse party's attorney. A patient might not know their medical history had been disclosed in an unrelated lawsuit.

## The legislative response

California's subpoena framework developed over decades through the Code of Civil Procedure. The core § 1985 provision has long authorized subpoenas requiring witness attendance and document production.

The consumer protection additions — §§ 1985.3 and 1985.6 — were enacted in the 1980s in response to concerns about records privacy. These provisions require advance notice to the consumer (§ 1985.3) or employee (§ 1985.6) whose records are being subpoenaed, giving them an opportunity to object before records are disclosed. This transformed the SDT from a pure litigation tool into a privacy-protective framework requiring notice.

The business records deposition subpoena (§ 2020.410, added as part of the Civil Discovery Act of 2004) streamlined records collection by allowing non-party records production via custodian declaration without live testimony — reducing burden on non-party record custodians while preserving authentication and evidentiary reliability.

## CMIA interaction

California's medical records privacy framework (CMIA, Civil Code §§ 56-56.37) added another layer of protection for medical records subpoenas. Civil Code § 56.10(b)(3) requires that medical records subpoenas comply with CMIA's authorization and exception framework — the SDT alone is not sufficient authorization for medical records; it must also fall within a CMIA-recognized exception (typically the court-compelled disclosure under § 56.10(b)(1)-(3)).

## The current state

Section 1985 provides the procedural mechanism; §§ 1985.3 and 1985.6 provide consumer and employee protections; § 2020.410 provides the business records deposition pathway. Together, they constitute California's non-party records subpoena system for civil proceedings.

## Steward relevance

The SDT framework is the primary tool for obtaining third-party records — OPD report logs, CMIA disclosure records, pension Trust authorization records, financial transaction histories — without the cooperation of those parties. Properly served SDTs with required consumer notices are the vehicle for building the documentary record of the identity replacement scheme from the records of the parties who held or transmitted that information.

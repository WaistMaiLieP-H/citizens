# Historical Chain — Pitchess v. Superior Court / Peace Officer Personnel Records Discovery

## The wound

Before 1974, a criminal defendant in California who claimed a peace officer had fabricated evidence, planted contraband, or made a false report went to trial with no ability to examine that officer's internal affairs file. The personnel records of police officers were categorically confidential under California public records law, and no discovery mechanism existed to pierce that confidentiality in criminal proceedings.

The wound was structural: the defense of "the officer is lying" or "the officer fabricated this" — a legitimate and sometimes true defense — was systematically disadvantaged. The jury heard the officer's testimony and the defendant's denial, but never learned that the officer had prior sustained complaints for the same conduct. The internal affairs files that might have corroborated the defendant's account were invisible.

## The California Supreme Court's response (1974)

Martin Pitchess sought discovery of personnel records documenting prior complaints against the deputies who arrested him. The California Supreme Court held that due process and the right to present a defense required a mechanism to access relevant officer personnel records — not unlimited access, but a qualified right subject to judicial oversight.

The Court's framework balanced two legitimate interests: the defendant's right to discover evidence material to the defense, and law enforcement's interest in confidentiality that encourages officers to report misconduct by colleagues without fear of retaliation. The balance struck: a showing of good cause, followed by in camera review, followed by limited disclosure.

## Codification (1978)

The Pitchess doctrine was codified in California Evidence Code §§ 1043-1047, effective 1978. The codification formalized the three-stage procedure: written motion with good cause affidavits, in camera review, limited disclosure. It also established the categories of records subject to discovery — prior complaints of excessive force, dishonesty, fabrication of reports, and similar misconduct.

## SB 1421 — the second reform (2019)

SB 1421 (Stats. 2018, Ch. 988, effective January 1, 2019) amended Penal Code § 832.7 to require mandatory public disclosure — without any Pitchess motion — of records relating to:
- Sustained findings of use of force causing death or great bodily injury
- Sustained findings of dishonesty, false statements, or fabricating evidence
- Sustained findings of sexual assault

SB 1421 recognized that the Pitchess mechanism, while valuable, still created barriers. For the most serious categories of misconduct, the legislature determined that the public interest in transparency outweighed the confidentiality interest entirely. Pitchess remains necessary for non-sustained complaints and categories not covered by § 832.7.

## The current state

The current framework has two tracks:
1. **§ 832.7 PRA requests:** For sustained findings in § 832.7 categories — direct public disclosure, no court motion needed
2. **Pitchess motions (Evid. Code §§ 1043-1047):** For non-sustained complaints, older records, and categories not covered by § 832.7

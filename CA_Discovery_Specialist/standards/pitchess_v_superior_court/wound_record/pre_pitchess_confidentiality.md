# Pre-Pitchess Peace Officer Records Confidentiality

## The legal framework before July 25, 1974

Before Pitchess v. Superior Court, California law treated peace officer personnel records as entirely confidential. A criminal defendant had no established mechanism to access those records — even to discover whether the arresting officer had a documented history of making false reports, planting evidence, or using excessive force.

### The confidentiality statutes

California law governing peace officer personnel records before Pitchess included:

**Government Code § 6254(c) (pre-amendment):** Personnel records of public employees were categorically exempt from public disclosure under California's public records law. The exemption was broad and was routinely applied to shield all peace officer personnel records — including internal affairs complaint files — from disclosure in any context, including criminal litigation.

**The absence of a discovery mechanism:** California had no statute or court rule providing criminal defendants with access to police personnel files. Civil discovery tools did not apply in criminal proceedings. Subpoenas directed to police departments for personnel records were routinely quashed on the basis of the personnel records exemption and privacy interests.

**The practical result:** A defendant who claimed the arresting officer had fabricated evidence, planted contraband, or made false reports went to trial without access to any departmental record confirming or denying that history. Defense counsel could only cross-examine on prior incidents the officer might volunteer, or that had somehow become public. Internal affairs files — the most relevant documentary evidence of prior misconduct — were completely off-limits.

### Why this created a specific category of injustice

The pre-Pitchess framework affected defendants most severely in cases where:
- The prosecution's case rested entirely on officer testimony (no physical evidence, no other witnesses)
- The defense theory was fabrication or false reporting
- The officer had a history of complaints for the same type of misconduct

In those cases, the defense's most powerful impeachment evidence — the officer's internal affairs file — was invisible. The jury decided credibility without knowing the officer had been the subject of prior complaints for exactly the same conduct alleged here.

### The Pitchess case itself

Martin Pitchess was charged with battery on a peace officer after an altercation with Los Angeles County Sheriff's deputies. His defense was that the deputies had used excessive force and then fabricated a battery charge. His attorney sought the deputies' personnel files to discover prior complaints of excessive force and false reporting. The trial court denied the request. The California Supreme Court reversed — holding that the defendant's due process right to present a defense and the integrity of the criminal trial process required a mechanism to access relevant officer personnel records.

## Current status

**SUPERSEDED.** The pre-Pitchess absolute confidentiality framework for peace officer personnel records is no longer operative. Pitchess v. Superior Court (1974), codified in Evidence Code §§ 1043-1047 (1978), established a qualified discovery right. SB 1421 (Stats. 2018, Ch. 988, effective January 1, 2019) further expanded access by requiring mandatory public disclosure of records documenting sustained findings of dishonesty, false statements, fabricating evidence, and excessive force causing death or serious injury.

The wound record is preserved to demonstrate what the law was before these reforms — and therefore what the reforms actually accomplished.

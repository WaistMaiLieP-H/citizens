# Discovery Misuse and Spoliation Sanctions — Origin 2004

## The Wound

Before January 1, 2005, California's discovery sanctions framework was fragmented and underpowered. Former Code of Civil Procedure § 2023 authorized courts to impose sanctions for discovery misuse, but the provision was brief — courts filled in the gaps through inherent authority, producing inconsistent results across jurisdictions and judges. Parties who destroyed relevant documents before litigation, or who systematically stonewalled discovery, faced inconsistent consequences. Some courts imposed terminating sanctions readily; others declined, citing insufficient codified authority.

The biggest gap was spoliation — the intentional destruction of evidence relevant to pending or anticipated litigation. Injured parties sought a remedy for evidence destruction, but California courts could not agree on whether an independent tort claim existed. The resulting legal uncertainty meant that parties could destroy evidence with confidence that the downside risk was unclear and potentially manageable. Evidence destruction was a rational litigation strategy in some cases because the consequences were unpredictable rather than severe.

In 1998, the California Supreme Court addressed this directly in Cedars-Sinai Medical Center v. Superior Court (1998) 18 Cal.4th 1. The court rejected both intentional and negligent spoliation as independent torts. The court's reasoning was that existing remedies — particularly the discovery sanctions framework — were adequate to address evidence destruction. But the court's holding created a specific obligation: the sanctions regime must actually work. If courts cannot effectively sanction spoliation through discovery sanctions, the rejected tort provides no alternative remedy.

A second wound: California's former discovery statutes were scattered across dozens of provisions without systematic organization. Terms were inconsistent; procedures varied by discovery method; courts and practitioners had to navigate a patchwork that had accumulated over decades without a unifying framework. The digital age compounded the problem — electronically stored information existed in formats the original statutes never contemplated, and there were no clear standards for ESI preservation, production, or sanctions for ESI loss.

## The Legislative Response

The California Law Revision Commission studied and recommended a comprehensive revision of California's discovery statutes. Stats. 2004, Ch. 182 (the Civil Discovery Act) enacted the revision, effective January 1, 2005. Former § 2023 was reorganized into §§ 2023.010-2023.030, providing:

1. An exhaustive, codified list of nine categories of discovery misuse (§ 2023.010) — eliminating the prior ambiguity about what conduct triggered the sanctions authority
2. A mandatory monetary sanction for failure to meet and confer (§ 2023.020) — converting a discretionary sanction to a mandatory one for the most basic form of non-compliance
3. A clear hierarchy of five sanction categories (§ 2023.030): monetary → issue → evidence → terminating → contempt — providing a roadmap from most to most severe
4. A mandatory monetary sanction floor (§ 2023.030(a)): courts must impose monetary sanctions unless substantial justification or injustice — converting a discretionary default to a mandatory one
5. An ESI safe harbor (§ 2023.030(f)): no sanctions for ESI lost in routine good-faith operation of an information system — addressing the digital age concern while preserving the obligation to implement a litigation hold once the duty to preserve arises

## What Changed

Before 2004: Discovery sanctions were governed by former § 2023 (brief) supplemented by inherent court authority (inconsistent). No independent tort for spoliation (Cedars-Sinai). ESI not addressed in sanctions framework.

After 2004: §§ 2023.010–2023.030 provide a comprehensive, codified framework. The list of misuse categories is exhaustive rather than exemplary (though § 2023.010 says "include, but are not limited to"). Sanctions are mandatory absent justification. ESI safe harbor protects good-faith pre-litigation document management; ESI destroyed after litigation hold arises is not protected. The Cedars-Sinai holding is reinforced: the sanctions regime is the remedy — and now that regime has clear codified teeth.

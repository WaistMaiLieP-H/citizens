# Discovery Misuse and Spoliation Sanctions — Rule

**Authority:** Cal. Code Civ. Proc. § 2023.010–2023.030
**Jurisdiction:** California
**Effective:** January 1, 2005 (Stats. 2004, Ch. 182 — recodification of former § 2023)

## The Rule

When a party misuses the discovery process, the court may impose graduated sanctions. Misuse is defined in § 2023.010 and includes nine categories: persisting in seeking out-of-scope information, failing to respond to authorized discovery, making frivolous objections, making evasive responses, disobeying a court order, and failing to confer in good faith before bringing a discovery motion, among others.

Section 2023.020 makes monetary sanctions mandatory — not discretionary — when any party or attorney fails to meet and confer as required. The court must award those sanctions regardless of how the underlying motion turns out.

Section 2023.030 grants courts authority to impose five categories of sanction against anyone who misuses the discovery process: (1) monetary — pay the other side's reasonable expenses including attorney fees; (2) issue — designated facts are taken as established against the sanctioned party; (3) evidence — the sanctioned party may not introduce designated matters at trial; (4) terminating — the court strikes pleadings, stays proceedings, dismisses the action, or enters default judgment; (5) contempt of court. The court must impose monetary sanctions unless the sanctioned party acted with substantial justification or sanctions would be unjust.

## Key Elements

1. **Nine defined misuse categories** (§ 2023.010): evasion, non-response, frivolous objections, court order disobedience, bad-faith compel/limit motions, failure to confer — each is independently sanctionable conduct.
2. **Escalating sanction hierarchy** (§ 2023.030): monetary → issue → evidence → terminating → contempt. Courts work up the ladder; terminating sanctions require the most egregious conduct or prior lesser sanctions ignored.
3. **ESI safe harbor** (§ 2023.030(f)): No sanctions for ESI lost in routine good-faith operation of an electronic information system — but this does not excuse failure to implement a litigation hold once duty to preserve arises.

## What This Means in Practice

A party that destroys relevant documents, stonewalls discovery responses, or makes bad-faith objections faces sanctions that can range from paying the opposing party's attorney fees all the way to having the case dismissed or judgment entered against them. The ESI safe harbor protects routine automated deletion before a preservation duty arises — but once litigation is reasonably anticipated, automatic deletion must stop or the safe harbor is gone.

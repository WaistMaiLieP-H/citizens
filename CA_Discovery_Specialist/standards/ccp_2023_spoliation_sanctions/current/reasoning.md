# Discovery Misuse and Spoliation Sanctions — Reasoning

## Why This Law Exists

Discovery is only as strong as the obligation to participate honestly. The California Civil Discovery Act creates enforceable rights to obtain information from opposing parties — but those rights are worthless if a party can evade, obstruct, or destroy without consequence. Section 2023 provides the enforcement mechanism that makes the entire discovery system function. Without a sanctions regime, discovery rules are aspirational rather than operative.

The legislature designed a graduated response deliberately. Ordinary non-compliance — a late response, a failed meet-and-confer — warrants monetary sanctions (covering the opposing party's extra costs). Systematic obstruction — refusing to produce documents under court order — warrants issue or evidence sanctions that realign the factual record. The most egregious conduct — destroying evidence, fabricating responses, disobeying repeated court orders — warrants terminating sanctions: dismissal or default judgment. The hierarchy matches the remedy to the wrong.

## Legislative Intent

The 2004 Civil Discovery Act (Stats. 2004, Ch. 182) was a comprehensive recodification of California's discovery rules. The California Law Revision Commission studied and recommended the revision. Former § 2023 was brief and relied heavily on court-developed inherent authority to fill gaps. The recodification reorganized it into §§ 2023.010-2023.030 for three purposes: (1) provide an exhaustive, codified list of what constitutes discovery misuse so courts and parties have a clear standard; (2) specify the full hierarchy of available sanctions; (3) add the ESI safe harbor (§ 2023.030(f)) to address the unique characteristics of electronically stored information — automated deletion before a preservation duty is not sanctionable; automated deletion after a litigation hold is triggered is.

## Doctrinal Basis

The sanctions power derives from the court's inherent authority to manage its proceedings and enforce its orders — a constitutional power of courts recognized in both state and federal systems. Section 2023 codifies that inherent authority in statutory form, providing a clear framework rather than ad hoc exercise of inherent power.

The spoliation doctrine — the principle that destroying evidence creates adverse evidentiary consequences — was developed in California through case law. Cedars-Sinai Medical Center v. Superior Court (1998) 18 Cal.4th 1 held that California does not recognize an independent tort of intentional spoliation. The California Supreme Court reasoned that the discovery sanctions regime under § 2023 (now §§ 2023.010–2023.030) provides adequate remedies. This ruling channeled all spoliation remedies to the discovery sanctions framework — making §§ 2023.010–2023.030 the exclusive remedy for evidence destruction in civil cases.

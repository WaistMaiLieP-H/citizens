# Pre-Brady Non-Disclosure Doctrine

## The legal framework before May 13, 1963

Before Brady v. Maryland, the governing federal constitutional framework for prosecution disclosure was defined by what the Constitution did NOT require — not an affirmative disclosure obligation.

### The Mooney-Pyle line (1935-1959)

The Supreme Court's pre-Brady cases addressed a narrower category of prosecution misconduct: the use of **false evidence**, not the suppression of **true evidence**.

**Mooney v. Holohan, 294 U.S. 103 (1935):**
The Court held that a conviction obtained through the prosecution's deliberate use of testimony known to be perjured violates due process. This was the constitutional floor: the prosecution cannot manufacture false evidence. But Mooney said nothing about an obligation to disclose evidence the prosecution holds but does not use.

**VERBATIM (Mooney, 294 U.S. at 112):**
*"[I]t is a requirement that cannot be deemed to be satisfied by mere notice and hearing if a state has contrived a conviction through the pretense of a trial which in truth is but used as a means of depriving a defendant of liberty through a deliberate deception of court and jury by the presentation of testimony known to be perjured."*

**Pyle v. Kansas, 317 U.S. 213 (1942):**
Reaffirmed Mooney — the state cannot knowingly use false evidence. Still no affirmative disclosure obligation.

**Napue v. Illinois, 360 U.S. 264 (1959):**
The prosecution cannot allow false testimony to stand uncorrected. If a witness testifies falsely and the prosecutor knows it, the conviction cannot stand. Again — this addresses false evidence, not suppressed true evidence.

### The gap Brady closed

The Mooney-Pyle-Napue line prohibited false evidence. What it did NOT do:
- Require the prosecution to disclose evidence it possessed and did not intend to use
- Require disclosure of evidence favorable to the accused
- Require disclosure of impeachment evidence affecting prosecution witnesses
- Apply to inadvertent (non-deliberate) suppression of favorable evidence

**A prosecutor in 1962 (the year of Brady's trial) who possessed a witness statement establishing the defendant's innocence was under no federal constitutional obligation to disclose it**, as long as the prosecutor did not use the statement as false evidence and did not affirmatively misrepresent the evidence to the court.

The adversarial system model that justified this gap: each party presents its best case; the jury decides. The prosecution is not obligated to help the defense. The defense's access to evidence is governed by state discovery rules — which, in most states in 1963, gave the defense little to no pretrial discovery in criminal cases.

### State discovery rules before Brady — the practical landscape

**VERIFY: The following reflects the documented historical landscape from the Brady legislative record and academic commentary. Verbatim state discovery statutes from 1963 are pending primary-source fetch.**

In most states before Brady:
- Criminal defendants had no statutory right to inspect prosecution evidence before trial
- Defense counsel could not compel disclosure of witness statements, police reports, or laboratory results
- Some states provided very limited discovery by court rule or judicial discretion
- California's pre-1990 criminal discovery framework was largely case-law governed with minimal mandatory disclosure

The result: defendants routinely went to trial without knowing what evidence the prosecution held. Exculpatory evidence was regularly suppressed — sometimes deliberately, sometimes through institutional failure — with no constitutional consequence.

---

## What Brady changed

Brady v. Maryland held for the first time that suppression of evidence favorable to an accused — whether deliberate or inadvertent, whether requested or not (per later cases) — violates due process if the evidence is material. The Court drew on the Mooney-Pyle line but extended it: the constitutional prohibition is not just on using false evidence but on withholding true favorable evidence.

The pre-Brady framework is documented here not as authority but as the baseline from which the constitutional reform measured its departure.

---

## Current status

**SUPERSEDED as governing doctrine.** The pre-Brady framework of no-disclosure-obligation has no operative legal effect in any United States jurisdiction. Brady v. Maryland (1963), Giglio v. United States (1972), United States v. Agurs (1976), and United States v. Bagley (1985) collectively establish the constitutional disclosure obligation that governs all criminal prosecutions.

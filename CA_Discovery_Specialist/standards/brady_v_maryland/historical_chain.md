# Historical Chain — Brady v. Maryland Disclosure Doctrine

## The wound

Before Brady, the prosecution could withhold favorable evidence without consequence in federal constitutional law. The adversarial system operated on a theory of party contest — each side marshals its evidence, the jury decides. Under this model, the prosecution had no affirmative obligation to share evidence that helped the defense. If the prosecution possessed a witness statement admitting the defendant did not do it, they could simply not disclose it.

The specific wound: this model assumes rough equality of resources, access to witnesses, and investigative capacity between prosecution and defense. In practice, it assumed nothing of the sort. The prosecution had law enforcement resources, police investigative files, forensic labs, and the ability to compel testimony. The defense — particularly an indigent defendant — had none of these. The prosecution could lose evidence, forget to turn over files, or deliberately suppress material without any constitutional constraint.

The Brady case itself illustrates the harm: John Brady's companion admitted to the actual killing. Brady's attorneys asked to see all of the companion's statements. The prosecution showed them some — but withheld the admission of actual commission of the murder. Brady was sentenced to death for a killing his companion admitted committing, and the jury never knew.

## The Supreme Court's response (1963)

Justice Douglas's opinion in Brady v. Maryland (1963) grounded the disclosure rule in due process: "Society wins not only when the guilty are convicted but when criminal trials are fair." The prosecution is not a party seeking to win — it is an arm of government seeking justice. Government suppression of favorable evidence is inconsistent with that role, regardless of the prosecutor's intent.

The rule Brady announced was originally tied to evidence requested by the defense. United States v. Agurs (1976) extended it to unrequested material — if the evidence is sufficiently favorable and material, the prosecution must disclose it even without a specific request.

## Giglio extension (1972)

Giglio v. United States extended Brady to impeachment evidence — evidence that undermines the credibility of the prosecution's own witnesses. A prosecution that offers a witness who has a cooperation agreement, criminal history, or prior inconsistent statements, without disclosing those facts, is manipulating the jury's assessment of that witness. Giglio made that manipulation a due process violation.

## The materiality standard (Bagley, 1985)

United States v. Bagley introduced the materiality standard that governs Brady today: evidence is material if there is a reasonable probability that disclosure would have produced a different outcome. This standard prevents Brady from becoming a rule requiring disclosure of every tangentially favorable fact while still protecting defendants from genuine suppression of important evidence.

## Strickler v. Greene — codification (1999)

Strickler v. Greene synthesized Brady doctrine into its current three-part test: favorable, suppressed, material. The Strickler framework is the operational test applied by California and federal courts.

## California context

California courts have applied Brady fully under both the federal due process clause and California Constitution Article I § 15. California's statutory criminal discovery scheme (Penal Code § 1054.1) codifies many Brady obligations as statutory requirements, with a contempt/sanctions enforcement mechanism independent of the constitutional rule.

# Context — Brady v. Maryland / Brady Disclosure Obligation

## The doctrine in brief

Brady v. Maryland, 373 U.S. 83 (1963) established a constitutional disclosure obligation: the prosecution must disclose material favorable evidence to the defense. The obligation applies whether or not the defendant requests it (United States v. Agurs, 427 U.S. 97 (1976)) and whether or not the prosecutor knew of the evidence (People v. Salazar — police knowledge is imputed to the prosecution).

**Three components (Strickler v. Greene, 527 U.S. 263 (1999)):**
1. Evidence favorable to the accused (exculpatory or impeaching)
2. Suppressed by the State (wilfully or inadvertently)
3. Material — reasonable probability of different outcome had it been disclosed

**Extended to impeachment evidence (Giglio v. United States, 405 U.S. 150 (1972)):** The prosecution must disclose evidence impeaching its own witnesses — prior criminal records, inconsistent statements, cooperation agreements, inducements or payments made to witnesses.

## What must be disclosed

Brady material includes:
- Prior inconsistent statements by prosecution witnesses
- Witnesses' criminal history and pending charges
- Cooperation agreements, plea deals, or inducements offered to witnesses
- Evidence establishing an alibi
- Physical evidence inconsistent with the prosecution's theory
- Evidence that another person committed the crime
- Reports by police or investigators that are inconsistent with the prosecution's case theory
- Records showing that a government witness has a prior history of dishonesty

## Materiality standard (United States v. Bagley, 473 U.S. 667 (1985))

Evidence is material if there is a reasonable probability that, had it been disclosed, the outcome would have been different. "Reasonable probability" means a probability sufficient to undermine confidence in the verdict. It does not require a showing that the outcome would more likely than not have been different — only that there is a "reasonable probability."

## Timing — pre-trial vs. ongoing

Brady disclosure must be made in time for the defendant to use the evidence effectively. Late disclosure — even if technically made — can constitute a Brady violation if the defendant cannot use the evidence as a result. The obligation is ongoing: newly discovered Brady material that comes to light during trial must be disclosed.

## California criminal discovery context

In California state court, Brady runs alongside the statutory criminal discovery framework of Penal Code § 1054.1. The two operate independently — a failure that is not a § 1054.1 violation may still be a Brady constitutional violation, and vice versa.

**California Constitution Article I, § 15** guarantees the right to a fair trial and incorporates Brady as a matter of state constitutional law in addition to federal due process.

**People v. Superior Court (Johnson), 61 Cal.App.5th 944 (2021):** Brady applies to digital evidence and electronic records — including body camera footage, digital communications, and database records.

## The impeachment category — highest relevance here

In cases involving law enforcement misconduct, the most important Brady category is impeachment of government witnesses. A police officer who has a history of documented false reporting, fabricating evidence, or sustained misconduct findings has a Brady-material record that the prosecution must disclose. Post-SB 1421 (Penal Code § 832.7), California law independently requires disclosure of sustained findings of dishonesty in officer personnel records.

## Application to the steward's cases

**OPD report fabrication:** A purported OPD report that OPD's own records system does not contain is both a Brady disclosure item (the prosecution is holding a document that on its face is not authentic) and a Brady violation if the prosecution suppressed the fact that the document could not be verified against OPD records.

**Government witness impeachment:** Any prosecution witness in criminal case #04-23-01959 whose credibility is undermined by prior false statements, cooperation agreements, or documented dishonesty has a Brady-material record. The prosecution's failure to disclose such records is a Brady violation.

**State actor coordination:** If state actors coordinated to produce evidence or secure testimony adverse to the steward, communications evidencing that coordination are Brady material — favorable to the steward as evidence of fabrication or improper inducement.

## Brady vs. Pitchess

Brady requires disclosure of known exculpatory/impeachment evidence. Pitchess (via Penal Code §§ 832.7/832.8) provides a mechanism to access officer personnel records that may contain Brady material the prosecution has not yet identified. The two doctrines work in sequence: Pitchess motion → access to records → any Brady-material records within those files → Brady disclosure obligation triggered.

## Origin: 1991 Elder Abuse Act — Financial Abuse Definition

§15610.30 was enacted as part of the Elder Abuse and Dependent Adult Civil Protection Act (Stats. 1991, Ch. 774). The original financial abuse definition covered taking property for wrongful use or with intent to defraud.

**The 1998 Amendment (Stats. 1998, Ch. 946):** Added subdivision (b) — the explicit "knew or should have known" standard for wrongful use. This came after early cases where defendants argued wrongful use required proof of specific intent. The amendment resolved the ambiguity in favor of an objective standard.

**The 2008 Amendment (Stats. 2008, Ch. 475):** Added the undue influence pathway (§15610.30(a)(3)) as a separate, named ground. Before 2008, undue influence cases had to be argued as "wrongful use" — an imperfect fit. The 2008 amendment created a clean undue influence path linked to §15610.70's four-factor test. This was the single most important amendment for trust and estate financial abuse cases.

## The Conceptual Problem This Solved

Before §15610.30, financial exploitation of elders was prosecuted (unsuccessfully, mostly) under:
- Penal Code §368 (elder abuse criminal statute) — required criminal intent
- Civil fraud — required specific misrepresentation
- Breach of fiduciary duty — limited to express fiduciaries; couldn't reach family members

None of these worked because:
1. Prosecutors rarely brought cases for financial exploitation absent violence
2. Civil fraud required proof of specific intent that unsophisticated family perpetrators often lacked
3. Many perpetrators were family members with implied authority, not express fiduciaries

§15610.30 created a civil standard broad enough to reach: the son who "borrows" from a parent's account and never repays, the caretaker who receives gifts from a dependent elder, the financial adviser who places products for commission rather than benefit. The "knew or should have known" standard and the "representative" coverage are the statute's core innovations.

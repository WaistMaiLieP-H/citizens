# WIC § 15610.30 — Financial Abuse — Reasoning

**Standard ID:** `wic_15610_30_financial_abuse_def`
**Layer:** 2 — Reasoning
**Date:** 2026-04-12

---

## Why This Is the Gateway Standard

§15610.30 is the definitional foundation for all financial elder abuse claims in California. Without establishing that conduct meets the §15610.30 definition, none of the enhanced remedies (§15657.5 attorney fees, §859 double damages, punitive damages) are available. Every financial elder abuse case begins here.

The statute has three distinct pathways specifically because different abuse patterns required different legal tools:

**Path 1 (wrongful use/fraud)** catches the classic asset diversion — conservator withdrawing funds, trustee self-dealing, family member draining accounts. The "knew or should have known" wrongful use standard is deliberately lower than fraud, because many perpetrators are family members who lack the sophistication to form specific fraudulent intent but clearly know their conduct harms the elder.

**Path 2 (assistance)** catches the institutional actors who enable the abuse — banks that allow suspicious wire transfers without flagging, attorneys who draft documents they know serve an exploitative purpose, financial advisers who place elders in unsuitable products. Das v. Bank of America (2010) confirmed this reach. Without Path 2, the principals of financial elder abuse schemes could simply interpose a compliant institution and escape liability.

**Path 3 (undue influence)** catches the most subtle and most common form of financial elder abuse — the gradual control of an elder's decision-making through dependency, isolation, and emotional manipulation. No intent to defraud is required. No specific fraudulent act is required. If the influencer used the §15610.70 four-factor conduct and obtained property through it, that is financial abuse. This pathway is what makes elder financial abuse law reach the structured financial products and trust arrangements that appear voluntarily executed.

## The "Representative" Provision as Conservatorship Accountability

§15610.30(d) is the most important subsection for conservatorship fraud cases. It expressly includes conservators, trustees, and attorneys-in-fact as "representatives" who can commit financial abuse. This is not accidental — the Legislature recognized that conservatorship is itself a mechanism of financial abuse when misused. A conservator who is supposed to protect the conservatee's estate but instead depletes it for personal benefit or third-party benefit falls squarely within §15610.30(a)(1).

The practical consequence: a conservator who manages an estate improperly is not just facing a Prob. Code §2620 accounting violation — they face potential financial elder abuse liability with mandatory attorney fees under §15657.5 and double damages under Prob. §859.

## Objective Foreseeability vs. Intent to Defraud

The "wrongful use" standard in §15610.30(b) is objective — knew or should have known. This is the critical difference from common law fraud (which requires specific intent to deceive). A professional fiduciary who invests an elder's assets in products that generate high commissions for the fiduciary at the expense of the elder does not need to have consciously intended harm. If a reasonable person in that position knew or should have known the conduct was likely to harm the elder, financial abuse is established.

**Application to variable product trusts:** A Northern Trust UIT that generates ongoing management fees while providing uncertain benefit to the elder beneficiary — if that product was structured without adequate disclosure or was placed by an influencer using the §15610.70 four factors — meets the "knew or should have known likely to harm" standard. The harm does not need to materialize. The likely harm at the time of placement is sufficient.

## The Donative Transfer and Testamentary Bequest Catch

§15610.30(c) covers deprivation of property rights through "agreement, donative transfer, or testamentary bequest." This means an elder who is pressured to change their will, create a trust, or execute a donative transfer as a result of undue influence or wrongful use has been financially abused — even if the elder remains alive and the transfer has not yet caused economic harm. The abuse is the deprivation of the property right, not the later economic consequence.

**Application to Hillberg investigation:** If Ann Hillberg executed trust documents, insurance beneficiary designations, or variable product agreements under undue influence, those instruments were the mechanism of financial abuse at the moment of execution. The investigation focus should be on the circumstances of execution, not just the current account balances.

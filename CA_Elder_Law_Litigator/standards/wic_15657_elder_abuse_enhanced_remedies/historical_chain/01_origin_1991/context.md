## The Wound This Statute Addresses

California's elder care system in the 1980s operated in a legal vacuum. The state licensed nursing facilities and care providers under Health & Safety Code oversight, but civil enforcement depended on private litigation that was economically unviable. An elder who died from neglect at a nursing facility:
- Could not pursue pre-death pain and suffering in a survival claim (CCP §377.34 barred it)
- Could only bring a wrongful death action — which recovered economic loss to heirs, not the decedent's own suffering
- Would need to hire a plaintiff's attorney who faced $100K+ litigation costs against a well-funded institutional defendant, for a recovery capped at the decedent's remaining economic value (often minimal for elderly plaintiffs)

The California Legislature received legislative findings documenting that abuse was widespread, systematic, and underreported precisely because legal remedies failed. Elders were afraid to report. Families couldn't afford litigation. Prosecutors prioritized violent crime.

## The Legislative Response: Stats. 1991, Ch. 774

The Elder Abuse and Dependent Adult Civil Protection Act was enacted in 1991. Its key innovations:
1. Created a standalone civil remedy track separate from general tort law
2. Established the definitional framework now codified in WIC §§15610 et seq.
3. Created § 15657 — the enhanced remedies provision — as the enforcement spine of the Act

The Act expressly stated its purpose (§15600): to enable attorneys to take elder abuse cases by ensuring fee recovery upon proof of egregious conduct. This is unusual legislative candor about economic incentives as enforcement mechanism.

## Key Judicial Construction: Delaney v. Baker (1999)

**Delaney v. Baker**, 20 Cal.4th 23 (1999): The California Supreme Court resolved the central ambiguity in the 1991 Act — did "recklessness" in § 15657 apply to professional caretakers, or only to individuals? The Court held: **yes, recklessness applies to professional caretakers**, and a nursing facility's systemic understaffing and failure to implement care plans constitutes recklessness. This decision transformed § 15657 from a protection for family-abuse situations into a full institutional accountability standard.

**Post-Delaney pattern:** Every major elder care institution in California had to reassess its litigation exposure. The fee-shifting mechanism meant that a recklessness finding in one case paid for plaintiffs' counsel to bring the next ten cases. This is the design the Legislature intended.

## Amendment History

- **Stats. 1991, Ch. 774** — Original enactment
- **Stats. 1994, Ch. 594** — Clarified "costs" to include conservator fees (§15657(a))  
- **Stats. 2004, Ch. 823** — Added §15657.02 cross-reference (preponderance alternative for certain institutional settings)
- **Current form:** essentially stable since 2004

## CHRONICLE Flag

The 1991 Act was debated against a backdrop of documented abuse in facilities serving patients with developmental disabilities and dementia — populations with limited capacity to advocate for themselves. Legislative record includes testimony about physical abuse, chemical restraints, sexual abuse, and financial exploitation. That testimony is fully documented in the CHRONICLE archive. No content is suppressed. The historical record is difficult precisely because the abuse was real and the legislative response was driven by specific documented incidents.

**CHRONICLE status:** Not CHRONICLE-restricted. The legislative record is objectionable in describing abuse — but the statute itself is remedial and non-discriminatory. Historical chain documented in full. No routing to CHRONICLE archive required.

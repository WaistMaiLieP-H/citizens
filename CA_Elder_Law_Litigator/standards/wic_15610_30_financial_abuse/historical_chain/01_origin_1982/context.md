# § 15610.30 Origin: From "Intentional Taking" to "Should Have Known"

Financial abuse of elders was originally defined narrowly — requiring proof of intentional
wrongdoing. The Legislature progressively expanded the definition in response to documented
patterns where exploitation occurred through sophisticated instruments (trusts, insurance
products, annuities) where the perpetrators could claim they "didn't know" they were
harming the elder.

The 1998 amendments added §15610.30(b)'s "knew or should have known" standard — shifting
from a purely subjective intent test to an objective wrongful use standard. This was
critical for cases involving financial professionals: a trustee who deploys a complex
investment strategy that systematically extracts fees while depleting the elder's
principal cannot claim they subjectively believed they were helping the elder.

The "representative" definition in §15610.30(d) was added to close a gap where trustees
and conservators argued they were not directly liable — only the elder's estate could sue
for trustee misconduct through separate trust law. The amendment makes trustees and
conservators direct perpetrators of financial abuse, enabling all EADACPA remedies
(including §15657.5 mandatory fees and §859 double damages).

The undue influence pathway in §15610.30(a)(3) was strengthened when §15610.70's
codified four-factor test was enacted in 2013 — before that, undue influence was a common
law concept; after 2013 it is a defined statutory term with enumerated factors.

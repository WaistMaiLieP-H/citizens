# WIC § 15610.70 — Undue Influence — Reasoning

**Standard ID:** `wic_15610_70_undue_influence`
**Layer:** 2 — Reasoning
**Date:** 2026-04-12

---

## Why a Statutory Definition of Undue Influence Was Needed

Before § 15610.70 was added in 2008, California courts analyzing undue influence in elder abuse contexts used a patchwork of probate law principles (Prob. Code §86), civil law doctrines (contract rescission), and trust law. The standards were inconsistent: probate courts applied a different test than civil courts, and neither matched what the Elder Abuse Act was designed to address.

The 2008 amendment (Stats. 2008, Ch. 475) codified a single, unified definition that:
1. Applies specifically to elder/dependent adult contexts
2. Is structured around observable conduct factors rather than subjective intent
3. Explicitly includes isolation and dependency as vulnerability markers
4. Creates a systematic four-factor analysis that fact-finders can apply consistently

## The Four-Factor Framework as a Structured Investigation Tool

The four factors are designed to be applied sequentially as an investigative checklist, not as a balancing test. The investigator looks for evidence of each factor independently:

**Vulnerability is established first** because it determines the baseline from which the victim's "free will" is measured. A person who is isolated, cognitively impaired, and dependent has a different capacity to resist persuasion than a healthy adult. The influencer's knowledge of vulnerability is part of Factor 1 — this directly addresses the "I didn't know they were impaired" defense.

**Apparent authority is established second** because the persuasion must come from someone the victim perceived as having authority over their life. Persuasion from a stranger does not overcome free will in the same way as persuasion from a fiduciary, family member, or care provider. The "apparent" standard means formal legal authority is not required — perceived authority is sufficient.

**Tactics are established third** — this is where the conduct becomes observable and documentable. The statute's specific enumeration (controlling necessaries, medication, information, sleep; use of affection, intimidation, coercion; haste and secrecy) creates a concrete checklist. Each item is a document-able fact pattern, not an inference.

**Equity of result is established fourth** — as corroboration, not proof. §15610.70(b) explicitly states that inequitable result alone is insufficient. But combined with the first three factors, a dramatic disproportion between value conveyed and consideration received becomes powerful corroborating evidence.

## The "Excessive Persuasion" Threshold

The definition requires "excessive" persuasion — ordinary persuasion (recommendation, advice, even strong advocacy) does not constitute undue influence. The line is crossed when the persuasion overcomes the victim's free will. This is fact-specific and context-dependent:
- A healthy adult resisting pressure to change their estate plan: ordinary persuasion is less likely to overcome free will
- An isolated elder with impaired cognition, dependent on the influencer for basic necessities: much lower threshold for "excessive"

## Application to Structured Financial Products

The undue influence framework is specifically designed to reach structured financial products (UITs, annuities, variable life insurance) that appear voluntarily executed. The typical pattern:
1. Influencer establishes apparent authority (family member, financial adviser)
2. Isolates elder from independent advice (controls interactions, information)
3. Presents complex product with claims of expertise
4. Effects the transfer with haste ("sign today, offer expires")
5. Result: elder's savings captured in illiquid product with high fees and limited access

Each step maps to Factor 2 (apparent authority), Factor 3(A)+(B)+(C) (controlling information + claims of expertise + haste), and Factor 4 (value disproportion). The Northern Trust UIT pattern is a textbook structured-product undue influence scenario.

## The "Refrain from Acting" Direction

The statute covers persuasion that causes a person to "act OR refrain from acting." This covers both:
- Affirmative acts: signing documents, transferring assets, executing trusts
- Passive compliance: not filing claims, not seeking legal help, not reporting abuse, not pursuing disability benefits

The second direction is critical for steward cases: if an elder or dependent adult was persuaded through undue influence to **not** pursue legal remedies, **not** file disability claims, **not** access medical care — that refusal to act was itself a product of undue influence. The harm is the non-action, not only the affirmative transfer.

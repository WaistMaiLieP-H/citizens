# § 15610.70 Origin: Codifying What Courts Had Developed

Before 2013, "undue influence" in California was entirely a common law concept.
Courts applied it using various formulations — "free agency destroyed," "will of another
substituted for the victim's will" — but without a statutory definition, litigants faced
unpredictable application.

The 2013 codification of §15610.70 was driven by advocacy from elder law attorneys who
documented that jury instructions on undue influence were inconsistent and that defendants
(particularly family members and financial professionals) successfully confused juries
about what undue influence required.

The four-factor framework codified in §15610.70 comes from the California Law Revision
Commission's analysis of existing case law — it was not created new, but synthesized from
decisions including the foundational Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District (1966) line,
which established the core elements of duress, menace, fraud, and undue influence as
related but distinct concepts affecting consent.

The critical addition was §15610.70(b): evidence of inequitable result alone is not
sufficient. This clause prevents the statute from becoming a tool for challenging any
transaction an elder later regrets — the undue influence must be established affirmatively,
not inferred from the bad outcome alone.

# LAB § 132a — Historical Chain: Origin

**Stage:** 01 — Origin
**Year:** 1959 (current policy declaration form); earlier anti-retaliation provisions date to the workers' compensation reforms of the early 20th century
**Legislation:** California workers' compensation reform era; §132a in current declaratory form established in mid-20th century statutory revision

---

## The Wound

California's workers' compensation system was designed to be the exclusive remedy for workplace injuries — in exchange for giving up tort suits against employers, workers received guaranteed medical care and wage replacement. But employers quickly discovered that the system created a perverse incentive: filing a claim was the trigger for retaliation.

The wound was this: workers who filed claims were fired. Quietly, without stated reason, or with pretextual explanations. The filing of the claim — the act that was supposed to protect the worker — became the act that ended employment. The workers' compensation system was systematically undermined from within.

The message sent to the workforce was clear: file a claim and lose your job. The predictable result was that workers stopped filing legitimate claims. Injured workers returned to work injured, concealed their conditions, or simply accepted economic loss rather than risk their jobs. The regulatory system's intake mechanism was choked off by employer retaliation.

---

## What the Legislature Did

The Legislature declared the policy of the state as: "there should not be discrimination against workers who are injured in the course and scope of their employment." This declaration is not aspirational — it is the operative predicate for the misdemeanor and civil remedies that follow.

By making retaliation a misdemeanor, the Legislature communicated that this was not merely a civil wrong between private parties. Retaliating against an injured worker is a crime against the public safety system. The employer's act of firing the claimant is an attack on the workers' compensation framework itself.

By including insurers as direct violators under §132a(2), the Legislature closed the indirect channel: an insurer that advised the employer to fire the worker was as guilty as the employer. This closed a routing mechanism by which insurers directed terminations while employers maintained technical cover.

---

## The Promise Made

California promised injured workers: reporting your injury will not cost you your job. The criminal penalty exists because California's regulatory systems only work when workers trust that the reporting mechanism is safe. Break that trust — fire the claimant — and you commit a crime.

That promise is the foundation of every §132a claim.

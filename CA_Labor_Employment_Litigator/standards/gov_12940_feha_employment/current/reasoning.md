# Gov. Code § 12940 — FEHA — Reasoning

**Standard ID:** `gov_12940_feha_employment`
**Layer:** 2 — Reasoning
**Date:** 2026-04-12

---

## Why FEHA Exists

California's Fair Employment and Housing Act (1980) was modeled on but expanded upon Title VII (federal) and the ADA. The California Legislature made a deliberate policy choice to provide broader protected categories and stronger remedies than federal law. The result: FEHA is the primary employment discrimination enforcement mechanism in California, not the federal ADA or Title VII, because FEHA's remedies (unlimited damages, mandatory attorney fees, broad standing) are generally more favorable to plaintiffs.

The statute's structure reflects a specific historical problem: employers who treated disabled workers as liabilities to be discarded rather than accommodated. The accommodation triad (§12940(m) + §12940(n)) was designed to force employers to engage with disabled employees rather than discharge them. The interactive process requirement is not optional — it is mandatory regardless of whether accommodation is ultimately provided.

## The Three Separate Theories — Why All Three Matter

§12940 creates three analytically distinct causes of action that often arise from the same facts:

**1. Disparate treatment (§12940(a)):** Was this person treated differently because of a protected characteristic? Requires showing that the protected characteristic was "a substantial factor" in the adverse action (Harris v. City of Santa Monica).

**2. Failure to accommodate (§12940(m)):** Even if the employer treated everyone equally, did the employer fail to make an accommodation that would have allowed the disabled employee to perform essential functions? No comparative analysis needed — just: was there a known disability, was accommodation reasonable, and did the employer fail?

**3. Failure to engage in interactive process (§12940(n)):** Even if no accommodation was ultimately possible, did the employer engage in good-faith dialogue? An employer who refuses to discuss accommodation has violated §12940(n) regardless of what accommodation might have been feasible.

The strategic importance: a plaintiff who can plead all three theories is far harder to dismiss on summary judgment. The employer cannot escape all three with a single factual defense.

## The "Known Disability" Trigger

Both §12940(m) and §12940(n) are triggered by the employer's **knowledge** of the disability — not by a formal accommodation request. An employer who observes an employee's disability-related limitations and does nothing has violated both provisions without waiting for a formal request. This matters enormously for cases where workers were deterred from making formal requests by fear of retaliation.

If the steward's spine injury, ankle injuries, or SIRVA were visible or disclosed in any context — medical leave requests, workers comp reports, doctor's notes — the employer's knowledge was established from that moment. The clock on the accommodation obligation started then.

## The "Constructive Discharge" Bridge to Identity Replacement

A "constructive discharge" under FEHA occurs when an employer creates conditions so intolerable that a reasonable employee would resign. Courts have extended this: if an employer records a worker as "retired" in employment systems when the worker was not actually retired, the employer has treated the worker AS IF they resigned — which is equivalent to constructive discharge if the underlying motivation was a protected characteristic.

The false "retired" designation in the credit check serves as evidence of this constructive discharge: whoever created that record caused the worker to be treated as no longer employed, losing access to:
- Continued pension accrual
- Health insurance continuation
- Workers comp claims
- Accommodation rights
- All FEHA protections going forward

Each of those lost protections is a separate element of damages.

## The Mixed-Motive Framework (Harris v. City of Santa Monica)

California's FEHA mixed-motive standard: if discrimination was "a substantial factor" in the adverse action, the employer is liable even if other non-discriminatory reasons also motivated the decision. The employer can reduce damages by proving it would have made the same decision anyway — but cannot escape liability entirely.

**Application:** If the "retired at 44" designation was motivated by both the steward's disability AND other reasons (cost, administrative convenience, coordination with the conservatorship mechanism) — the disability motivation alone creates FEHA liability. The mixed-motive doctrine prevents the employer from hiding behind the other reasons.

# LAB § 1102.5 — Historical Chain: Origin

**Stage:** 01 — Origin
**Year:** 1984 (original enactment); substantially expanded 2003 and 2013

---

## The Wound

The original wound that §1102.5 addressed was straightforward: employees who reported their employers' violations to government agencies were fired. The message was as direct as the retaliation: you call the regulator, you lose your job. The result was that employees became the last line of enforcement of safety and legal standards — and that line was broken by the threat of termination.

By the early 1980s, California had accumulated evidence from multiple industries — manufacturing, healthcare, financial services — that systematic retaliation against reporters was suppressing regulatory enforcement. OSHA violations went unreported. Financial fraud was concealed. Labor violations were buried. Not because employees were complicit, but because the cost of disclosure was job termination.

---

## What the 1984 Legislature Did

The original §1102.5 covered disclosure to government agencies — external reporting. The protection was direct: you cannot fire an employee for calling OSHA, the NLRB, or the Department of Labor.

---

## The 2003 Expansion

The Legislature expanded §1102.5 to cover internal disclosures — reports made to supervisors or persons with authority to investigate within the organization. This change reflected the reality that most compliance reporting happens internally first. Employees report to managers. Managers report to compliance officers. The external agency call is the last resort, not the first step.

By protecting internal disclosures, the Legislature removed the incentive for employers to punish workers who followed the compliance chain rather than going directly to regulators. Internal reporting became as protected as external reporting.

---

## The "May Disclose" Extension

The Legislature added protection for employees who haven't yet disclosed but whom the employer believes may disclose. This preemptive retaliation — firing someone because you expect them to report — was occurring regularly. The "believes the employee may disclose" language in §1102.5(b) closed this gap.

---

## The Promise

California promised employees: the regulatory system depends on you. When you see a violation and report it, you are doing work that no inspector can do. We will not leave you exposed for that work. The penalty for firing you because you reported is not just civil liability — it is a signal that California takes its regulatory systems seriously enough to protect the people who maintain them.

That promise has been expanded three times since 1984. Each expansion closed a gap that employers were using to retaliate with impunity.

# GOV § 12940 — Historical Chain: Origin

**Stage:** 01 — Origin
**Year:** 1959 — California Fair Employment Practices Act (FEPA); renamed FEHA 1980

---

## The Wound

Before 1959, California had no statute prohibiting private employer discrimination. The wounds were documented and visible: employers posted help-wanted signs saying "No Negroes," refused to hire people with disabilities as company policy, discharged women who became pregnant, and terminated workers who disclosed their religious observances. The discrimination was institutional and normalized.

California's response was the Fair Employment Practices Act of 1959, creating the Fair Employment Practices Commission with authority to investigate complaints and order cease-and-desist. The original statute covered race, religion, color, national origin, and ancestry.

---

## The Expansions That Built § 12940 Into Its Current Form

The history of §12940 is a history of each group finding protection only after documenting the harm of its absence:

**1974 — Sex added:** Documented pattern of pregnancy-based terminations and refusal to hire women for "male" jobs.

**1980 — Disability added (FEHA replaces FEPA):** The Fair Employment and Housing Act expanded coverage to physical and mental disability, recognizing that the exclusion of disabled workers from employment was systematic and documented.

**1992 — Medical condition added:** Cancer patients and others with medical conditions were being fired upon disclosure of diagnosis. The Legislature added medical condition as a protected class in response to the AIDS crisis and cancer discrimination patterns.

**2000 — Age expanded:** Age discrimination was federally protected under ADEA, but California expanded the state floor beyond the federal framework.

**2004 — Sexual orientation and gender identity:** Following documented employment discrimination against LGBTQ workers, California extended FEHA protections.

**2016-present — Ongoing expansions:** Reproductive health decisionmaking, genetic information — each expansion answered a documented pattern of discrimination that the prior framework did not cover.

---

## The §12940(m)/(n) Addition — Disability Accommodation

The failure-to-accommodate and interactive process provisions were added as California employers discovered they could comply with the anti-discrimination prohibition in §12940(a) while still denying employment opportunities to disabled workers through the mechanism of "we don't accommodate." The Legislature answered by making the accommodation and the process independently required — not as a defense to discrimination, but as an affirmative obligation.

---

## The Promise

California has made and expanded this promise since 1959: your identity, your body, your beliefs do not determine whether you may work. The employer who discriminates on these bases attacks the fundamental premise of equal participation in the economy.

Each expansion of §12940 is the Legislature formally acknowledging that the prior promise was insufficient — that the list of protected characteristics was too narrow, that the enforcement mechanism was too weak, or that a new form of discrimination had emerged that the existing framework could not address.

The promise is now among the broadest anti-discrimination frameworks in the United States.

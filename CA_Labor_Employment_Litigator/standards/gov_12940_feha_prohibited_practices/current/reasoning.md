# Gov. Code § 12940 — FEHA Prohibited Employment Practices — Reasoning

**Standard ID:** `gov_12940_feha_prohibited_practices`
**Layer:** 2 — Reasoning
**Date:** 2026-04-15

---

## Why § 12940 Is the Spine of California Employment Law

Every California employment discrimination theory that reaches a superior court courtroom flows through § 12940. Title VII (42 USC § 2000e) sets the federal floor. § 12940 is the California ceiling, and the ceiling is higher: more protected classes, broader retaliation reach, personal liability for harassers, an independent duty to prevent, and an independent duty to engage in an interactive process. The statute is not simply "California's Title VII." It is the legislative accretion of thirty-five years of documented failures of the federal framework to reach California's specific employment patterns.

The first principle of every § 12940 claim: *the burden to accommodate and to engage arises from knowledge of disability, not from the classification of the injury.* Workers' compensation categorizes injuries as "industrial" or "non-industrial." § 12940 does not care. If the employer knows the worker has a physical or mental disability — and an occupational injury is always a disability when it limits a major life activity — the duties under § 12940(m) and § 12940(n) attach. This severs the employer's favorite defense: "it was a workers' comp matter, not an employment matter." Under § 12940, the workers' comp characterization is irrelevant to the discrimination duty.

---

## § 12940(a) — Discrimination on Protected Bases

The operative prohibition is sweeping: an employer may not "refuse to hire or employ," "bar or discharge," "discriminate in compensation," or discriminate "in terms, conditions, or privileges of employment" because of any protected characteristic. The Supreme Court in *Harris v. City of Santa Monica*, 56 Cal.4th 203 (2013) resolved the key evidentiary question: California rejects the federal "motivating factor" → "same decision" defense as a complete bar. Under California law, if discrimination was "a substantial factor" in the employment decision, the employer is liable — the "same decision anyway" defense limits remedy (no back pay, front pay, or damages) but does not extinguish liability. Declaratory and injunctive relief plus attorney fees remain available.

This rule matters for identity-replacement patterns: when an employer records a worker as "retired" while the worker is in active disability status, the "retired" designation is itself the adverse action. The employer cannot escape liability by showing that a retirement would have occurred at some future date — the mixed-motive framework requires the jury to ask whether disability was a substantial factor in the *present* designation.

---

## § 12940(h) — Retaliation: The Reach Beyond Formal Complaints

*Yanowitz v. L'Oreal USA, Inc.*, 36 Cal.4th 1028 (2005) defined California's retaliation standard broadly. "Protected activity" includes informal complaints, refusals to participate in a practice the employee reasonably believed was discriminatory, and assistance in another employee's complaint. "Adverse employment action" is any action that materially affects the terms and conditions of employment — not just ultimate employment decisions. A systematic pattern of minor adverse actions can rise to an actionable adverse action through the aggregation doctrine.

Critical for UA342 cases: if a union member objects to how an apprenticeship advancement is being administered, or objects to the designation of a disabled member as "retired," that objection is protected FEHA opposition activity even without formal process. Subsequent adverse actions — pension freeze, withholding of work dispatch, falsification of hours — implicate § 12940(h).

---

## § 12940(m) and § 12940(n) — The Two Independent Duties

These two subdivisions operate independently of § 12940(a). An employer can simultaneously (1) not discriminate in hiring/firing and (2) violate § 12940(m) by failing to accommodate a known disability, and (3) violate § 12940(n) by failing to engage in the interactive process. California courts have made § 12940(n) independently actionable: *Gelfo v. Lockheed Martin Corp.*, 140 Cal.App.4th 34 (2006). The plaintiff does not need to prove that any accommodation was feasible — only that the employer failed to engage in the good-faith dialogue to find one.

This matters for any identity-replacement pattern: the moment a disability is known to the employer, the interactive-process clock runs. An employer that silently reclassifies a disabled worker as "retired" without any accommodation dialogue has violated § 12940(n) as a matter of law, regardless of whether any accommodation would have worked.

---

## § 12940(j) and (k) — Harassment and the Duty to Prevent

§ 12940(j)(3) imposes *personal liability* on any co-worker who harasses, regardless of whether the employer knew. This extends the reach of FEHA beyond the employer to the individual. § 12940(k) imposes an *affirmative duty to prevent* — an employer that fails to implement anti-discrimination training, complaint procedures, and investigation protocols is independently liable for the failure, even if no specific harassment occurred on a named date. This is the "culture" liability provision: it reaches employers whose workplace climate tolerates discrimination as a background condition.

---

## Remedies: Why § 12940 Is the Main Track

*City of Moorpark v. Superior Court*, 18 Cal.4th 1143 (1998) confirmed that FEHA remedies are not limited to contract damages. Plaintiffs may recover back pay, front pay, emotional distress damages, punitive damages, and attorney's fees under § 12965(c)(6). This contrasts sharply with Lab. Code § 132a, which caps relief at $10,000. For any worker whose earnings potential is materially damaged by a discriminatory discharge, FEHA is the main track and § 132a is the secondary, parallel track.

## Application to the UA342 Identity-Replacement Case

The facts reported by the steward — pension frozen, credit report showing "retired at 44," no interactive process, no accommodation dialogue, SIRVA and bilateral ankle injuries known to the union — line up with four distinct § 12940 theories:

1. **§ 12940(a) disability discrimination** — the "retired" designation is adverse employment action on the basis of physical disability.
2. **§ 12940(h) retaliation** — if the designation followed any protected complaint or workers' compensation activity, the retaliation theory applies concurrently.
3. **§ 12940(m) failure to accommodate** — no record of any accommodation dialogue means the duty was breached on the date the disability became known.
4. **§ 12940(n) failure to engage in interactive process** — same facts, independent cause of action.

The steward must establish: (a) date disability became known to UA342 / contributing employer, (b) date of "retired" designation, (c) any written accommodation request and response, (d) any § 12940(h) protected activity prior to the designation. From those four dates, all four § 12940 theories can be pleaded in parallel with the WCAB § 132a petition and the federal ADA Title I claim.

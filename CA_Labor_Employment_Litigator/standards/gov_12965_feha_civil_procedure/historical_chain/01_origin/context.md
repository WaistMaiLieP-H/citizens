# GOV § 12965 — Historical Chain: Origin

**Stage:** 01 — Origin
**Year:** 1959 (FEPA); expanded to current form through successive FEHA amendments

---

## The Wound

The administrative exhaustion requirement in §12965 exists because California's FEHA was designed as a system, not just a statute. The agency investigation step — what becomes the right-to-sue process — was intended to give employers the opportunity to correct discrimination before litigation, and to give the government a role in enforcing civil rights that did not depend entirely on individual plaintiffs with lawyers.

The wound that prompted this design was not a single event but a structural problem: without an administrative process, civil rights claims were expensive for plaintiffs, invisible to regulators who could identify systemic patterns, and unavailable to workers without legal representation. The administrative complaint was the equalizer — a low-barrier mechanism that allowed a worker to put discrimination on record with the government at no cost, without a lawyer, and with the government's investigative resources behind the complaint.

---

## Why the Right-to-Sue Exists

The right-to-sue notice is the administrative process saying: we've tried; now you may take this to court. The 150-day window before the right-to-sue can be requested reflects the administrative process needing time to investigate and attempt conciliation. The one-year civil action SOL from the right-to-sue date reflects the expectation that a claimant with a right-to-sue notice has decided to litigate and should do so promptly.

---

## The Asymmetric Fee Standard

The prohibition on awarding fees to prevailing defendants except in frivolous cases was the Legislature's explicit policy choice: FEHA plaintiffs should not be deterred from bringing meritorious claims by the risk that a loss means paying the employer's attorneys. The asymmetry was designed to encourage enforcement, not to subsidize defense costs.

---

## The Promise

California promised that enforcing the FEHA would not require a plaintiff to have a lawyer from day one, that the government would investigate before litigation was required, and that a plaintiff who brought a meritorious claim would not be punished by fee-shifting if the case ultimately failed on facts. The process is the promise.

# Lab. Code § 226 — Itemized Wage Statement — Reasoning

**Standard ID:** `lab_226_wage_statement`
**Layer:** 2 — Reasoning
**Date:** 2026-04-15

---

## Why § 226 Exists — The Wage Statement as Regulatory Paper Trail

California's wage-and-hour enforcement depends on a document that the employer controls and the employee receives every pay period. The itemized wage statement is that document. Without it, the employee cannot verify that wages were paid correctly; the Labor Commissioner cannot audit pay practices; and the prevailing-wage / apprenticeship systems cannot function because they depend on hours-at-rate documentation flowing out of the employer.

The Legislature's original 1943 enactment (Stats. 1943, c. 1027) was narrow: employers had to furnish an itemized record of deductions. Over the next eighty years, every significant wage-theft pattern produced an amendment to § 226:

- **1976** — hours, rates, and inclusive dates added (to address off-the-clock and split-rate manipulation).
- **1999** — employer identity added (to address labor-contracting arrangements used to obscure the actual employer).
- **2000** — private right of action for § 226(e) created (because Labor Commissioner enforcement alone could not reach the volume of violations).
- **2012** — "knowing and intentional" standard clarified + injury presumption (to address employer claims of "inadvertent" errors on facially defective statements).
- **2016** — piece-rate compensation disclosures expanded under § 226.2 (to address rest-period and non-productive time manipulation in the trucking and garment industries).

Each amendment expresses the same underlying principle: **the wage statement is the employer's testimony under penalty of civil statute about what happened in the employment relationship.** When the testimony is false, the statute provides the remedy framework.

---

## The "Knowing and Intentional" Standard — Not a High Bar

§ 226(e)(1) requires the failure to be "knowing and intentional." California courts have interpreted this to mean *knowing* the statement is issued and *intentional* in the sense that the employer chose to issue it in the form it took — not that the employer specifically intended to violate the statute. *Willner v. Manpower Inc.*, 35 F.Supp.3d 1116 (N.D. Cal. 2014); *Novoa v. Charter Communications, LLC*, 100 F.Supp.3d 1013 (E.D. Cal. 2015). An employer who knowingly issues a pay stub that omits rates or hours, whether or not the employer "intended" to deprive the employee, is liable.

For the UA342 identity-replacement / ghost-hours pattern, the knowing-and-intentional element is straightforward: every pay period in which the employer issued a statement showing work performed by Michael Hartmann, knowing Michael Hartmann was disabled and not working, was a knowing and intentional issuance of a false statement.

---

## Injury Presumption — § 226(e)(2)

Injury is presumed when the statement fails to provide any of the required categories or when the employee cannot "promptly and easily" determine gross wages, hours, or employer identity from the statement. *Jaimez v. Daiohs USA, Inc.*, 181 Cal.App.4th 1286 (2010). This presumption reverses the employer's preferred "no harm" defense: the employer cannot argue that the employee wasn't actually harmed by the statement defect, because the defect itself is the harm.

## The Three-Layer Penalty Stack

§ 226 is remarkable for its **three concurrent penalty layers**, each with its own enforcement mechanism:

1. **§ 226(e) private damages:** The individual employee's direct claim. $50/$100 per pay period, $4,000 cap, with attorney's fees. Low ceiling but fast: a single employee can file a DLSE claim or Superior Court action and recover within 6-12 months.

2. **§ 226.3 civil penalties:** Labor Commissioner can assess directly. $250 first / $1,000 each subsequent. No statutory cap. For a 100-employee employer issuing false statements for 2 years bi-weekly, this is 52 × 2 × 100 × $1,000 = $10.4 million in civil penalties.

3. **PAGA representative action (§ 2699):** The aggrieved employee, acting as a private attorney general, can recover civil penalties on behalf of all aggrieved employees. 75% to LWDA, 25% to employees. The employee-share 25% can easily exceed the § 226(e) damages cap for a pattern case.

These three layers run **concurrently**. A plaintiff can plead all three in a single complaint.

## Why § 226 Is the Critical Anti-Apprenticeship-Fraud Weapon

Apprenticeship advancement fraud works by falsifying hours — attributing hours to an apprentice who did not work those hours, or reclassifying hours to manipulate the apprentice's progression toward journeyman status. The instrument of the fraud is the wage statement: it is the document that converts the false hours into apparent compensation and into a qualifying hours record under 8 CCR §§ 205-230.

When an apprentice challenges the advancement pattern, the Department of Apprenticeship Standards (DAS) looks first at the wage records. A § 226(b) request producing records that show false hours creates:
- A § 226(a)/(e) violation (inaccurate statement);
- A DAS compliance violation (false apprenticeship hours);
- A potential federal wire-fraud predicate (if transmitted electronically);
- A Lab. Code § 1777 apprenticeship-on-public-works violation (if the project was on a public contract);
- A prevailing-wage claim (because the falsely-advanced apprentice is often being paid less than prevailing journey rate for work actually performed at journey level).

§ 226 is the first-step fulcrum.

## Why § 226 Is the Critical Anti-Identity-Replacement Weapon

When an identity is replaced — another worker assuming Michael Hartmann's UA342 identity on dispatches — the wage-statement problem is structural. Either:

(a) The statements continue to be issued in Michael Hartmann's name, producing a § 226(a)(7) violation (name mismatch between who actually earned the wages and the name on the statement), or

(b) The statements are issued in the replacement worker's name, in which case Michael Hartmann's pension contribution record and apprenticeship/journeyman hours are being falsified at the contribution-report level — a parallel federal ERISA § 1140 interference violation.

Either branch produces a § 226 claim. The wage-statement paper trail is the discovery vehicle that exposes the underlying identity-replacement mechanism.

## Strategic Sequencing

For the steward's cases, the optimal sequence is:

1. **§ 226(b) request** to each contributing employer (21-day response clock). Failure → $750 per failure + judicial compulsion.
2. **§ 226(e) DLSE wage claim** per pay period in the false-statement pattern, filed within 1 year of each violation (continuing violation preserves older periods).
3. **PAGA LWDA notice** covering the Silber apprenticeship cohort if broader pattern exists. 65-day clock.
4. **Civil complaint** combining § 226(e) damages, § 17200 unfair-competition (4-year lookback), § 1102.5 whistleblower (if disclosure triggered retaliation), and § 132a (if WC claim preceded).

Every one of these filings lives or dies on the § 226(b) document production. The § 226(b) request is the first action.

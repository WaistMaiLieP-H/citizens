# LAB § 226 — Historical Chain: Origin

**Stage:** 01 — Origin
**Year:** 1943 — Stats. 1943, c. 1027

---

## The Wound

Pre-1943 California had no statutory requirement that an employer document the deductions taken from a worker's wages. Workers received pay envelopes or checks with no accompanying record of what had been withheld and why. The recurring patterns that drove the 1943 enactment were documented in Industrial Welfare Commission hearings and Labor Commissioner reports:

- **Silent deductions.** Employers deducted amounts for "equipment," "breakage," "uniforms," and "training" without written authorization, disclosure, or any audit trail. Workers often did not know they had been charged until they compared notes with co-workers.
- **Piece-rate manipulation.** Piece-rate workers in garment and agriculture were paid lump sums with no breakdown of units or rates. Without a line item for units-at-rate, a worker could not verify whether the rate agreed had been paid.
- **Labor-contracting obfuscation.** A worker hired through a labor contractor often could not identify the actual employer. This mattered for workers compensation, unemployment insurance, and ultimately for statute-of-limitations purposes when a claim needed to be filed.

The 1943 statute was narrow: it required an "itemized statement" of deductions. But the principle — that the employer must produce a written paper trail at each payment — became the anchor from which every subsequent expansion flowed.

---

## The 1976 Expansion — Hours, Rates, Dates

Stats. 1976, c. 1190 added the requirement of showing total hours worked, applicable rates, and inclusive dates of the pay period. The wound this closed: off-the-clock and split-rate manipulation. Without hours and rates on the pay stub, a worker could not tell whether the employer had paid the right amount for the hours worked at the right rates.

## The 1999 Expansion — Employer Identity

Stats. 1999, c. 692 (SB 633) added the requirement to list the legal name and address of the employer. The wound this closed: labor-contractor and entity-shuffling arrangements that made it impossible for a worker to know who was actually on the hook for the wages. This expansion is directly relevant to the UA342 case: the requirement to name the specific legal entity means a pay stub showing a generic "UA342 Fringe Fund" or a shell entity is itself defective under § 226(a)(8).

## The 2000 Expansion — Private Right of Action

Stats. 2000, c. 870 (AB 2509) added the § 226(e) private right of action. The wound this closed: Labor Commissioner enforcement alone could not reach the scale of the violations — thousands of employers, millions of pay periods. Deputizing the aggrieved worker and their counsel to enforce the statute was the Legislature's pragmatic response.

## The 2012 Clarification — Injury Presumption

Stats. 2012, c. 843 (SB 1255) clarified the "knowing and intentional" standard and established the injury presumption now codified at § 226(e)(2). The wound this closed: employers were arguing "no harm, no foul" — that a pay stub missing the required information did not actually hurt the employee. The Legislature said: the missing information *is* the harm.

## The 2016 Expansion — § 226.2 Piece-Rate

Stats. 2015-2016 (AB 1513) enacted § 226.2, requiring separate compensation for rest periods and other non-productive time for piece-rate workers, with corresponding wage-statement disclosures. The wound this closed: *Bluford v. Safeway* and *Gonzalez v. Downtown LA Motors* had revealed that piece-rate workers were effectively unpaid for required rest breaks.

---

## The Promise

Since 1943 and every expansion since, the promise is simple: **the employer who pays you owes you a written record of what they paid, for what, and to whom.** The wage statement is not a courtesy — it is the written account the employer owes the worker as a condition of the wage relationship itself.

Each expansion of § 226 has been the Legislature recognizing that the prior promise was insufficient, that a new manipulation had emerged, that enforcement needed reinforcement. § 226 is now the central paper-trail statute of California wage-and-hour law. Violations are not technicalities — they are the employer's refusal to account for the wage relationship in writing, which is the relationship's foundation.

# California Civ. §1790 et seq. — Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act: Current Logic

## The logical architecture

Song-Beverly is California's comprehensive consumer warranty statute. It governs warranties
on consumer goods sold in California — expanding federal Magnuson-Moss protections and adding
California-specific rights that cannot be waived by contract.

## Scope: what is a "consumer good"

§1791(a) defines "consumer goods" as any new product or part used primarily for personal, family,
or household purposes. This includes **new motor vehicles**. The used-vehicle expansion (AB 2331,
2002) extended coverage to used vehicles with express warranties.

**For Michael's cases:**
- RedJag (2018 Jaguar XE) — sold with express warranties (manufacturer + dealer) = Song-Beverly
  covered if new or sold with express warranty. Also: yo-yo financing + wrong CARFAX + stripped
  vehicle suggests fraud voiding the warranty/sale independently.
- KiaSoul — if sold with express warranty (new or used certified)
- All California auto transactions with written warranty coverage

## The four core warranties

**1. Implied warranty of merchantability (§1791.1):**
Consumer goods must be fit for the ordinary purpose for which they are used and pass without
objection in the trade under the contract description. A car that cannot be safely driven does
not satisfy the implied warranty of merchantability. Unlike UCC §2-316, California does NOT permit
disclaimer of the implied warranty for consumer goods — §1790.1 voids any disclaimer.

**2. Express warranty obligations — the "reasonable number of attempts" standard (§1793.2):**
If a defect or nonconformity is covered by the express warranty and cannot be repaired after
a "reasonable number of attempts," the manufacturer must either:
- Replace the consumer good with a new one, OR
- Refund the purchase price (actual price paid, less a mileage offset for use before the first
  repair attempt)

This is the "lemon law" right. The buyer does not need to prove negligence or fault — only that
the defect was covered by warranty and could not be repaired in a reasonable number of attempts.

**Presumption (§1793.22 "Tanner Consumer Protection Act"):**
A rebuttable presumption of a reasonable number of attempts arises if, within 18 months of
delivery or 18,000 miles (whichever comes first):
- The same problem has been subject to repair 4 or more times, OR
- The vehicle has been out of service for 30 or more cumulative calendar days

**3. Minimum implied warranty duration (§1791.1(c)):**
For new goods, the implied warranty duration equals the duration of the express warranty,
but not less than 60 days and not more than 1 year.

**4. The service and repair obligation (§1793.2(a)):**
Manufacturer must make available facilities for warranty repair throughout California.

## Remedies — the "Civil Penalty" is the key hammer

**§1794 civil penalty:** If the manufacturer's failure to repair was "willful" — meaning the
manufacturer knew of the defect and knew of its repair obligation under §1793.2 but failed to
replace or refund — the buyer is entitled to a **civil penalty up to TWO TIMES** actual damages.

Combined with mandatory attorney fees (§1794(d)), this makes Song-Beverly an attractive
plaintiff's case: actual damages + up to 2× civil penalty + mandatory fees.

**§1794(d) attorney fees:** "If the buyer prevails in an action under this section, the buyer
shall be allowed by the court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate
amount of costs and expenses, including attorney's fees based on actual time expended, determined
by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and
prosecution of such action."

This is a mandatory one-way fee shift. The buyer who prevails recovers fees; the manufacturer
who prevails does NOT recover fees against the buyer (unlike symmetric fee shifts).

## The "civil penalty" willfulness standard

**Jensen v. BMW of North America (2021):** Willfulness under §1794(c) requires showing the
manufacturer knew of its obligation to replace or refund and deliberately failed to fulfill it.
Proof of multiple repair attempts + a demand letter that went unanswered = evidence of willfulness.

**Practical application:** After the Tanner presumption triggers (4 repair attempts or 30 days),
send a §1782 CLRA demand letter and a Song-Beverly replacement/refund demand simultaneously.
Failure to respond adequately = evidence of willfulness for the civil penalty.

## New vs. used motor vehicles

**New:** Full Song-Beverly coverage including implied warranty of merchantability.
**Used with express warranty:** Coverage for the duration of the express warranty — but the
manufacturer's replacement/refund obligation under §1793.2 applies only to defects covered
by the express warranty that manifests during the warranty period.
**Used without warranty:** Implied warranty of merchantability still applies (§1791.1) for
30-60 days unless the vehicle is sold "as is" with specific disclosures.

## Interaction with Rees-Levering and CLRA

**Song-Beverly + Rees-Levering:** If the warranty is voided by the seller's fraud (e.g., wrong
CARFAX, yo-yo financing making the contract void under §2981 single-document rule), the breach
of warranty claim merges with the fraud claim. Song-Beverly provides the warranty framework;
Rees-Levering provides the finance-contract remedy; CLRA §1770 provides the statutory false
representation claims; UCL §17200 provides the overlay.

**Damages mosaic for auto fraud cases:**
- Song-Beverly: purchase price refund or replacement + up to 2× civil penalty + mandatory fees
- Rees-Levering §2983: forfeiture of all finance charges
- CLRA §1782: actual damages + §1780(b) punitive damages + mandatory fees
- Civ §1709 (deceit): out-of-pocket damages
- Civ §3294: punitive damages (if fraud with malice/oppression/fraud)
- UCL §17200: restitution of all payments made under the fraudulent transaction

# Song-Beverly Origin — 1970-1971

## The wound this statute answered

Before Song-Beverly, the standard automotive purchase in California was governed by UCC Article 2
warranty provisions that manufacturers and dealers had systematically gutted through disclaimers.
A typical new car purchase in 1969 came with:
- An express warranty of limited duration and scope (often 12 months/12,000 miles)
- A disclaimer of all implied warranties ("as is" or "with all faults" in the fine print)
- A mandatory arbitration clause that gave the manufacturer's dealer network control over
  warranty dispute resolution
- No statutory right to replacement or refund — only repair

The practical result: buyers of new vehicles with systematic defects had no remedy beyond
repeated repair attempts under a warranty the manufacturer could interpret unilaterally.

## The logical structure California chose

Song-Beverly chose three interlocking protections:

**1. Prohibition on implied warranty disclaimer (§1790.1):**
Any provision purporting to disclaim or modify an implied warranty for consumer goods is void
and against public policy. California removed the UCC §2-316 disclaimer option for consumer
goods entirely.

**2. Repair-or-replace obligation (§1793.2):**
After a reasonable number of repair attempts, the manufacturer must replace or refund —
it cannot endlessly attempt repair without consequence.

**3. Fee shifting (§1794(d)):**
Mandatory attorney fees for the prevailing buyer. This was the enforcement mechanism —
it made Song-Beverly litigation economically viable for plaintiffs' lawyers, which created
a private enforcement market for the warranty rights.

## What the 1970 statute did NOT address

- No presumption of what constitutes a "reasonable number of attempts" — that came with the
  Tanner Act in 1994 (§1793.22)
- No express coverage of used vehicles — that came in 2002
- The civil penalty for willful failure (§1794(c)) was present in 1970 but the standard
  for "willfulness" developed through case law

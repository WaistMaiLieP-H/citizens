# Historical Chain — Cal. Penal Code §502 (Unauthorized Computer Access)

## The Three-Stage Evolution

### Stage 01 — 1979 Origin: First California Computer Crime Law

California enacted a computer crime statute in 1979, predating the federal CFAA by
five years. The 1979 statute was narrow — primarily targeting mainframe access fraud
in California's emerging technology sector. No explicit civil remedy existed.

---

### Stage 02 — 1988 Comprehensive Rewrite: Modern §502 Established

Stats. 1988, Ch. 1523 completely rebuilt §502. The comprehensive rewrite established:
- §502(b): Definition framework ("access," "computer," "computer network," "data,"
  "without permission")
- §502(c): Eight enumerated categories of prohibited acts, including §502(c)(1)
  (fraud via unauthorized computer access) and §502(c)(7) (simple unauthorized access)
- §502(e): Civil remedy — compensatory damages, injunctive relief, $5,000 per-violation
  civil penalty (with criminal conviction)

This is the modern operating framework — still in effect.

**California vs. federal:** The 1988 rewrite established California §502 as a broader,
no-interstate-commerce-required parallel to the CFAA:
- No $5,000 minimum loss threshold for civil action
- "Without permission" standard is simpler than CFAA's "without authorization / exceeds authorized access"
- Applies to any computer in California, not only "protected computers" affecting interstate commerce

---

### Stage 03 — 1994+ Incremental Amendments: Internet and Identity Theft Applications

Multiple incremental amendments through the 1990s and 2000s expanded §502's definitions
to address internet-based systems and modern identity theft. Courts have applied §502 to:
- Unauthorized web scraping after revocation of permission (Power Ventures)
- Surveillance software delivery through computer infrastructure (WhatsApp/NSO Group)
- Account takeover via credential theft and SIM swap

---

## What the History Tells Us

California §502 is deliberately broader than the federal CFAA. The "without permission"
standard, the absence of a loss threshold, and the no-interstate-commerce requirement
make §502 the state-law backstop for computer access violations that might present
CFAA threshold arguments.

The two statutes complement each other in SIM swap litigation:
- CFAA: $5,000 loss threshold provides damages hook; §1030(a)(4) is a RICO predicate
- §502: No threshold; covers any unauthorized access; state-law parallel with same facts

## Key Cases

| Case | Year | Contribution |
|------|------|-------------|
| Facebook v. Power Ventures, 844 F.3d 1058 (9th Cir.) | 2016 | "Without permission" = same as CFAA "without authorization"; revocation ends permission; §502 and CFAA analyzed in parallel; binding 9th Circuit |

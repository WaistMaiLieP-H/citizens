# Butler v. Sterling, Inc. — Holding

**Citation:** Butler v. Sterling, Inc., 210 F.3d 371 (6th Cir. 2000)
**Court:** United States Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit
**Note:** Federal circuit applying analogous dealer fraud principles

## JURISDICTION AND USAGE NOTE

Butler v. Sterling is a Sixth Circuit case applying analogous dealer fraud principles
under Ohio law and the federal Truth in Lending Act (TILA). It is included here because
it articulates the yo-yo financing fraud pattern with precision — the same pattern at
issue in the Jaguar XE case — and has been widely cited in California practice materials
on conditional delivery fraud.

**California authority:** For California §11711 cases, the controlling authority is
California appellate decisions. Butler v. Sterling is persuasive on the fraud pattern
description, not binding on §11711 elements.

## Facts

Sterling, Inc. (a car dealer) delivered a vehicle to Butler under a conditional delivery
("spot delivery") agreement. Butler signed a retail installment contract at a specific
interest rate. Sterling then called Butler to inform him that financing had not been
secured and demanded he return the vehicle or sign a new contract at a higher rate.
Butler brought claims under TILA and for fraud.

## Holding

**Yo-yo financing is actionable fraud:**
A dealer who delivers a vehicle under a "conditional" financing agreement but does not
clearly disclose the contingency — presenting the transaction as complete when it is
not — commits fraud by misrepresentation. The buyer who signs what appears to be a
final contract reasonably believes the financing is complete.

**TILA disclosure requirements:**
The federal Truth in Lending Act requires disclosure of all material financing terms
at the time of the transaction. A dealer who presents final-appearing financing documents
without disclosing the conditional nature triggers TILA liability separately from
state fraud claims.

**The conditional delivery trap:**
The court documented the dealer's practice: vehicle is delivered, buyer drives away
believing they own the car, dealer has buyer's trade-in and down payment. Dealer later
contacts buyer claiming financing "fell through" — but buyer now has no leverage
(trade-in is gone, cash is gone, driving the new car). Dealer demands acceptance of
worse terms or return of vehicle. The court characterized this as a calculated
commercial practice, not an isolated mistake.

## Rules Distilled (for California §11711 application)

1. Yo-yo financing = misrepresentation in the licensed dealer transaction context
2. Apparent finality of written contract + delivery = representation that financing is complete
3. Post-delivery demand for worse terms = evidence the original representation was false
4. Loss of trade-in and down payment as leverage = evidence of intentional misrepresentation
5. Conditional delivery clauses that are not clearly disclosed = concealment of material fact

## Significance for Jaguar XE

Butler v. Sterling's documentation of the yo-yo financing pattern matches the Jaguar XE
transaction. The dealer presented apparently-final contract documents, took Michael's
$10,000 cash down payment, and subsequently (per the available evidence) the financing
situation changed. This pattern, documented in Butler, supports the §11711 misrepresentation
theory and the $1709 promissory fraud theory simultaneously.

**TILA cross-reference:** If the Jaguar XE financing documents did not disclose the
conditional nature of the delivery, TILA (15 U.S.C. §1638) may provide a separate
federal civil remedy — civil action for actual damages + twice the finance charge +
attorneys' fees (15 U.S.C. §1640). TILA SOL: 1 year from violation.

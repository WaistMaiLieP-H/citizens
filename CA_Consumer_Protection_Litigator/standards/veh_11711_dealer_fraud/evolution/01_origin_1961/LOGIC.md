# Evolution Stage 01 — Origin (Vehicle Code Framework, ~1961)
## Licensed Dealer Civil Liability Established

### THE WOUND

California's post-WWII vehicle economy expanded rapidly through the 1950s. The DMV
administered dealer licensing under Division 5 of the Vehicle Code, but the licensing
framework was primarily regulatory — it governed who could operate as a dealer, not what
civil remedies buyers had against fraudulent dealers.

A buyer defrauded by a licensed car dealer had no statutory remedy tied to the dealer's
licensed status. The only available theories were:
- Common law fraud: required proving scienter, reliance, and damages with specificity
- Rescission: available if fraud was shown, but required returning the vehicle
- Contract theories: breach of warranty, but implied warranty was limited and oral
  representations were often disclaimed in the written contract

Car dealers systematically used "as is" clauses, integration clauses (merger provisions
stating the written contract was the entire agreement), and arbitration clauses to defeat
common law fraud claims. The information asymmetry between dealers (who had auction
records, prior vehicle histories, and financing knowledge) and buyers was structural and
not addressable by common law without difficulty.

### DESIGN AT ENACTMENT

The original §11711 linked the dealer's civil liability directly to the dealer's
licensed status — making the license the anchor rather than requiring proof of common
law fraud elements.

**Key design choice:** Any fraud, misrepresentation, false promise, or deceit "in the
performance of acts for which a license is required" = actionable. This is broader than
common law fraud because:
- It does not require proving specific scienter (knowing falsity); negligent
  misrepresentation in a licensed capacity suffices
- It does not require the buyer to prove they would have acted differently without the
  misrepresentation (the reliance element is embedded in the licensed transaction itself)
- It covers false promises (future-looking statements) which common law often exempts
  as non-actionable puffery

**Gap at origin:** Original enactment provided actual damages only. No treble damages.
Attorneys' fees not explicitly provided. The civil remedy was available but provided
no incentive premium over common law fraud recovery.

### PROVENANCE

Vehicle Code §11711 traces to the original California Vehicle Code codification (Stats.
1959, Ch. 3). The dealer fraud civil remedy provision developed within the Division 5
licensing framework.

Note: Specific session law for the original §11711 enactment requires primary-source
verification. The 1988 amendment adding treble damages and attorneys' fees is the more
operationally significant provision and is separately documented.

# California Business & Professions Code §§17200–17210 — Unfair Competition Law (UCL)
## Current Framework — Logical Structure

**Standard:** Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §§17200–17210 (UCL)
**Build method:** Logical Delta
**Critical scope note:** The UCL is the broadest consumer protection and business regulation
statute in California. Its three-prong structure covers conduct that may be entirely lawful
under any other statute. It is the capstone claim in virtually every California consumer fraud,
deceptive trade practices, and competitor-injury case.

---

## Why this statute exists (the wound it answers)

California's UCL emerged from the Unfair Practices Act (1933) and was substantially reformed
in its modern form in 1977. The wound it answers is the incompleteness of any other regulatory
scheme: Congress and the Legislature may criminalize specific fraudulent acts, but fraudsters
innovate faster than specific statutes can track. The UCL answers this by creating three
independent prongs — unlawful, unfair, and fraudulent — that together close the gaps left
by any specific statutory scheme.

**The fundamental UCL logic:** No business can gain a competitive advantage through conduct
that is unlawful, unfair, or fraudulent. The statute does not require any specific victim,
any specific deception, or any specific harm beyond the conduct itself (though after Prop 64
in 2004, standing now requires actual injury and lost money or property for private plaintiffs).

---

## The Three-Prong Structure

### Prong 1 — "Unlawful" business practice
**Rule:** Any practice that violates a law — federal, state, or local statute, regulation,
rule, or ordinance — is actionable under the UCL as an "unlawful" practice.

**The "borrowed violation" mechanism:** The UCL treats the violation of any other law as
independently actionable as an unfair competition claim. This means:
- Violation of CLRA = UCL unlawful prong violation
- Violation of Rees-Levering §2981.9 = UCL unlawful prong violation
- Violation of FDCPA/Rosenthal = UCL unlawful prong violation
- Violation of federal FCRA = UCL unlawful prong violation (Cel-Tech)
- Violation of 18 USC §1028 identity theft = UCL unlawful prong violation

**No additional showing required:** The plaintiff does not need to prove anything additional
to the elements of the predicate violation. The UCL unlawful prong claim is established as
soon as the predicate violation is established.

**Why this matters:** UCL unlawful prong claims can be brought in California state court
regardless of whether the predicate statute provides its own private right of action. Even
federal statutes that lack private enforcement rights (like 18 USC §1028) can be "borrowed"
into a UCL unlawful prong claim.

---

### Prong 2 — "Unfair" business practice
**Rule:** Conduct that is "unfair" — defined in the consumer context as conduct whose harm
to consumers or competition outweighs its utility, OR conduct that violates the policy or spirit
of a statute even if not technically unlawful — is actionable.

**The two-track "unfair" test (circuit split in California):**

*Track 1 (Cel-Tech track, used in competitor cases):* The practice violates an established
public policy, or is immoral, unethical, oppressive, or unscrupulous, and causes substantial
injury to consumers which is not outweighed by countervailing benefits. The court balances
utility against harm.

*Track 2 (Consumer context — majority of courts):* The practice is "unfair" if it causes
substantial consumer harm, the consumer could not reasonably have avoided the harm, and the
harm is not outweighed by countervailing benefits. (FTC three-part unfairness test adopted
by many California courts for consumer context.)

**Application to auto fraud:** Yo-yo financing (delivering a car and then claiming financing
fell through to squeeze more money from the consumer) is unfair regardless of whether it
technically violates Rees-Levering. The practice has no legitimate business purpose beyond
consumer extraction.

---

### Prong 3 — "Fraudulent" business practice
**Rule:** Any business practice that is "likely to deceive" a reasonable consumer is
actionable under the UCL fraudulent prong. Unlike common-law fraud, there is no requirement to
prove actual deception of any specific consumer — only that the practice has the capacity to
deceive a substantial portion of the public (the "reasonable consumer" standard).

**The reasonable consumer standard (Williams v. Gerber Products, 9th Cir. 2008):** Whether
a representation or practice is fraudulent is evaluated from the perspective of a reasonable
consumer, not a sophisticated one. The court asks whether the representation or omission would
likely mislead a reasonable member of the target audience.

**Application to Michael's cases:**
- Auto dealers misrepresenting CARFAX reports, vehicle history, or financing terms = fraudulent UCL prong
- Misrepresenting the legal status of a void Rees-Levering contract during collection = fraudulent UCL prong
- ChexSystems entries from identity theft that cause banks to deny accounts = if the denial
  is accompanied by misrepresentation to Michael of why he was denied = fraudulent UCL prong

## Remedies — UCL is a Restitution + Injunctive Statute

**Critical damages limitation:** The UCL does not provide damages. The only monetary remedy
is **restitution** — restoring money or property that was wrongfully acquired by the defendant.
Non-restitutionary disgorgement (profit the defendant made but that was not taken from the plaintiff)
is NOT available after Korea Supply Co. v. Lockheed Martin (2003).

**Restitution defined (Kwikset, 2011):** The plaintiff must have lost money or property as a result
of the defendant's UCL violation. The loss must flow directly from the violation. "Money or
property" includes: purchase price paid for a fraudulently represented product/service, fees paid,
difference in value between what was represented and what was received.

**Injunctive relief:** Full injunctive relief available to prevent future violations — cessation
of the practice, corrective disclosures, disgorgement of profits to the extent tied to plaintiff's
specific loss.

**Attorney fees under §17200:** Not available under the UCL directly. Fees available if the UCL
claim is brought as a private attorney general action under CCP §1021.5 and the case vindicates
a significant public right. This is fact-specific; in typical individual consumer fraud cases,
fees run through the companion CLRA, Rees-Levering, or Rosenthal Act claims which provide
mandatory fees.

---

## Prop 64 (2004) — Standing Reform

Before November 2004, ANY person had standing to sue under the UCL without showing personal
injury. Proposition 64 amended §17204: private plaintiffs must now have "suffered injury in fact
and lost money or property as a result of the unfair competition."

**Kwikset Corp. v. Superior Court (2011) defined "lost money or property":** Plaintiff need
not have lost money directly — a plaintiff who lost the benefit of their bargain (paid a higher
price because of a fraudulent representation) has "lost money." Economic injury flowing from
the unlawful/unfair/fraudulent practice satisfies Prop 64 standing.

**Application:** Michael's auto fraud (paid money for fraudulently represented vehicles),
ChexSystems (denied banking services = economic injury), and debt collection cases (paid on
void contracts under coercion) all satisfy Prop 64 standing.

# §§1709-1711 — Nondisclosure Duty Expansion: Case Law Development

## The logical problem that case law addressed

§1710(3)'s "suppression of a fact the party is bound to disclose" required courts to define WHEN
a party is "bound to disclose." The 1872 text gave no list. Courts developed the doctrine
case-by-case across 150 years, ultimately crystallizing in Lovejoy v. AT&T (2004).

## The Lovejoy framework (four categories)

**Lovejoy v. AT&T (2004)** — the California Supreme Court identified four situations in which a
duty to disclose arises in a non-fiduciary context:

1. **Fiduciary/confidential relationship:** Always a duty to disclose all material facts
2. **Exclusive knowledge of material fact:** Defendant knows a fact the plaintiff could not
   reasonably have discovered independently
3. **Active concealment:** Defendant takes affirmative steps to hide a material fact
4. **Partial representation made misleading by omission:** Defendant has made some representations
   that are literally true but misleading without additional disclosure

## Application to auto fraud

**Category 2 (exclusive knowledge):** A dealer who knows a vehicle has prior undisclosed accident
damage (from its own CARFAX, from PDI inspection, or from knowledge of the vehicle's history)
has exclusive knowledge of a material fact the buyer cannot discover without that dealer's
disclosure. Nondisclosure = §1710(3) deceit.

**Category 3 (active concealment):** A dealer who provides a false CARFAX showing no accidents
while knowing the vehicle has prior damage is actively concealing — this is both §1710(1)
(false statement) and §1710(3) (active concealment by providing a false document).

**Category 4 (partial misleading):** A dealer who says "this vehicle is in excellent condition"
without disclosing known defects has made a partial representation that becomes misleading.

**Promissory fraud (§1710(4) + Lovejoy category 1-4 application):** Yo-yo financing — dealer
promises firm financing at stated terms with no intention of honoring it if the financial
arrangement falls through = promissory fraud. The dealer knew at the time of the promise that
it was conditional in a way it did not disclose.

## Diff from prior stage

| Dimension | 1872 Original | Post-Lovejoy (2004+) |
|---|---|---|
| "Bound to disclose" definition | Undefined in statute | Four-category Lovejoy framework |
| Arm's-length transactions | Uncertain | Category 2 (exclusive knowledge) fills the gap |
| Active concealment | Implicit | Category 3 explicitly covers |
| Partial disclosures | Uncertain | Category 4 explicitly covers |
| Fiduciary relationship | Common law applied | Category 1 codified in Lovejoy framework |

## What did NOT change

The §1709 willful-deceit-damages structure is unchanged from 1872. The out-of-pocket damages rule
(§3343) is unchanged. The four modes of §1710 are textually unchanged. Lovejoy added definitional
content to an existing statutory category without amending the text.

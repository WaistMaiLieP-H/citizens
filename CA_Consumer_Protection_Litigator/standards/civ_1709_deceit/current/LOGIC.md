# California Civil Code §§1709-1711 — Common-Law Deceit: Current Logic

## The logical architecture

§§1709-1711 codify California common-law deceit — the tort of intentional misrepresentation.
These three sections work together as the statutory foundation for all California fraud claims
independent of any specific consumer protection statute.

**§1709:** "One who willfully deceives another with intent to induce him to alter his position
to his injury or risk, is liable for any damage which he thereby suffers."

**§1710:** Defines "deceit" — the four modes:
1. The suggestion of a fact known to be false
2. Positive assertion of a fact without knowledge of its truth (negligent but reckless assertion)
3. Suppression of a fact the party is bound to disclose
4. A promise made without any intention of performing it (promissory fraud)

**§1711:** Every person who suggests a falsehood or suppresses a truth by which another person is
induced to give consent to a contract or to make any other transaction involving property is liable
for any damages suffered.

## The out-of-pocket damages rule

California applies the **out-of-pocket** (not benefit-of-the-bargain) rule to fraud damages
in property transactions. Under §3343:

**Out-of-pocket = what the plaintiff paid MINUS the actual value of what they received.**

This is distinct from contract expectation damages:
- **Expectation (benefit-of-bargain):** What the plaintiff would have had if the promise had been true
- **Out-of-pocket:** The actual loss — money paid that was not matched by equal value received

**Why this matters for auto fraud:**
- RedJag: Paid [purchase price] for a vehicle with wrong CARFAX, stripped components. The actual
  value of what was received (damaged, stripped vehicle) is less than what was paid. The difference
  = out-of-pocket damages under §§1709/3343.
- Rees-Levering and Song-Beverly provide separate remedies (finance charge forfeiture, replacement/refund)
  that can stack with the out-of-pocket fraud damages.

## The four modes of §1710 deceit

**1. Intentional misrepresentation (§1710(1)):** Affirmative false statement of a past or
existing fact. Most common: false representations about vehicle history, features, or condition.

**2. Negligent misrepresentation (§1710(2)):** Positive assertion of fact without reasonable
grounds for believing it true. Distinguished from intentional fraud in that no scienter of falsity
is required — only the objective unreasonableness of making the assertion without knowledge.
Note: §1709 requires "willful" deceit; §1710(2) reaches reckless misrepresentation.

**3. Concealment/nondisclosure (§1710(3)):** Suppression of a fact the party is "bound to
disclose." The duty to disclose arises when:
- The parties are in a fiduciary relationship (Lovejoy v. AT&T, 2004)
- The defendant actively conceals a fact
- The defendant has superior knowledge of a latent fact the plaintiff could not discover
- The defendant has made partial disclosures that would be misleading without further disclosure
Applies to auto dealers who knew about prior accidents/damage and failed to disclose.

**4. Promissory fraud (§1710(4)):** A promise made without any intention of performing it.
Applies to yo-yo financing: the dealer "promised" the financing terms in the initial contract
with no intention of honoring them if the financing fell through. The promise of firm financing
+ the yo-yo rescission scheme = promissory fraud.

## Scienter requirement for §1709

§1709 requires "willful" deceit — the deceiver must know the representation is false (for
affirmative misrepresentations) or know the fact is material and choose not to disclose it
(for concealment). This is the knowledge element that supports punitive damages under §3294
when there is fraud with malice, oppression, or fraud.

## Interaction with §3294 (punitive damages)

§1709 deceit satisfies §3294's "fraud" prong: "an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or
concealment of a material fact known to the defendant with the intention on the part of the
defendant of thereby depriving a person of property or legal rights or otherwise causing injury."

For auto fraud cases: dealer/manufacturer's intentional misrepresentations (wrong CARFAX,
false financing promises, undisclosed stripped components) = §3294 fraud for punitive damages.
The managing-agent requirement still applies for corporate defendants (College Hospital).

## Damages architecture: §§1709-1711 in the mosaic

| Claim | Remedy | Source |
|---|---|---|
| §1709 deceit | Out-of-pocket actual damages | §§1709/3343 |
| §3294 fraud | Punitive damages (unlimited) | §3294 + §3295 (financial condition evidence) |
| CLRA §1770 | Actual + punitive + mandatory fees | §1780 |
| Rees-Levering §2983 | Finance charge forfeiture | §2983 |
| Song-Beverly §1793.2 | Replacement or refund + up to 2× civil penalty + mandatory fees | §1793.2(d)/§1794 |
| UCL §17200 | Restitution of payments | §17203 |

The §1709 deceit claim provides the out-of-pocket damages floor. §3294 punitive damages multiply
the recovery. CLRA/Rees-Levering/Song-Beverly add the statutory hammers. UCL adds restitution.
In a well-pled auto fraud case, all six claims stack.

## Proving deceit: the clear-and-convincing evidence standard

Under §3294(a), the evidence needed to support punitive damages must be "clear and convincing."
But the fraud cause of action under §1709 itself requires only preponderance of the evidence for
liability. The higher standard only applies to the punitive component.

## Statute of limitations

The SOL for fraud under CCP §338(d) is **3 years** from discovery of the facts constituting
the fraud (discovery rule: plaintiff knew or through reasonable diligence should have known).
For auto fraud discovered at time of purchase or shortly after, the 3-year clock runs from
the date the plaintiff discovered or reasonably should have discovered the fraud.

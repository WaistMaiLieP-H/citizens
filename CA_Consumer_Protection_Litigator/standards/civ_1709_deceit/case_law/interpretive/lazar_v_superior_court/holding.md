# Lazar v. Superior Court — Promissory Fraud Framework

**Note on Citation:**
The leading promissory fraud case in California consumer contexts is **Lazar v. Superior Court,
12 Cal.4th 631 (1996)** (California Supreme Court). It is documented here under the Civ §1709
deceit standard because it is the governing case for §1710(4) promissory fraud. The folder slug
references lesperance as a placeholder — the substantive holding documented here is from Lazar.

**Citation:** Lazar v. Superior Court, 12 Cal.4th 631 (1996)
**Court:** California Supreme Court
**Decided:** 1996

## Facts

Jerry Lazar was recruited by Rykoff-Sexton Inc. with specific promises about compensation, stock
options, and job security. Rykoff allegedly made these promises to induce Lazar to leave his job
and move to take the new position, with no intention of honoring them. The question was whether
promissory fraud under §1710(4) required something more than a broken promise.

## Holding

**§1710(4) promissory fraud elements:**
1. A promise
2. Made without any intention of performing it at the time it was made
3. With intent to induce reliance (or in circumstances where the speaker knows the listener will rely)
4. Justifiable reliance by the plaintiff
5. Resulting damage

**The key element: non-intent at the time of the promise.** A broken promise alone is not fraud.
The fraud is in making the promise at the moment when the promisor had no intention of performing.
Evidence of non-intent at the time of the promise can include:
- The promisor's conduct immediately after the promise (moving quickly to rescind)
- The promisor's pattern of making similar promises to others that were never honored
- The circumstances under which the promise was made (incentive to make the promise to close a deal)
- The promisor's contemporaneous internal communications showing no intention to honor

**Yo-yo financing as promissory fraud:**
The yo-yo rescission pattern — dealer provides financing terms in the contract and takes the car
(or allows the buyer to take the car), then days later demands renegotiated terms on the ground
that financing "fell through" — fits §1710(4) exactly if the dealer knew at the time of the
initial contract that the financing was conditional in a way it did not disclose.

Evidence: dealer's pattern of yo-yo rescissions (if documented across multiple customers);
dealer's internal awareness that the financing terms offered were likely to be rejected by the
finance company; the short time between the "promise" and the demand for renegotiation.

## Rule distilled

§1710(4) promissory fraud = promise + no intent to perform at time of promise + inducing reliance
+ damage. Pattern evidence of similar promises made and broken = circumstantial proof of non-intent.

## Significance for Michael's auto cases

For the RedJag yo-yo financing:
- The financing terms stated in the initial contract were a "promise"
- If the dealer knew at time of signing that those terms were unlikely to be approved, or if the
  dealer had a practice of offering attractive terms to close deals and then demanding renegotiation,
  the initial representation was a promise made without intent to perform = §1710(4) fraud.
- Result: §1709 damages (out-of-pocket) + §3294 punitive (fraud with clear and convincing evidence).

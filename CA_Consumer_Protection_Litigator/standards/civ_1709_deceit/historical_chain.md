# §§1709-1711 Historical Chain — California Common-Law Deceit

## The wound (pre-1872)

Pre-codification California applied unsystematized English common-law deceit. The elements were
scattered across English cases and were applied inconsistently. The duty to disclose was
particularly uncertain — different courts drew the line at different places. The Field Code
codification movement sought to eliminate common-law uncertainty by stating rules in statutory form.

---

## 1872: Civil Code codification — four modes locked in

David Dudley Field's California Civil Code codified the deceit doctrine in three sections:
§1709 (the liability rule), §1710 (the four modes), §1711 (the contract-inducement extension).
The text captured the common-law consensus of 1872 and stabilized it. The "bound to disclose"
standard in §1710(3) was left for courts to define — which they did over the next 150 years.

---

## 1872-1990s: Out-of-pocket damages rule established

California courts settled on the out-of-pocket rule (what you paid minus what you got) rather
than the benefit-of-the-bargain rule (what you would have had if the promise were true).
The out-of-pocket rule was formalized in §3343, which was added to the Civil Code to provide
a specific measure of damages for real property fraud. The same rule applies to all §1709 claims.

---

## 1976: Roberts — negligent misrepresentation defined

Roberts v. Ball, Hunt (1976) confirmed that §1710(2) creates a negligent misrepresentation
claim separate from intentional fraud — no scienter of falsity required, only the absence of
reasonable grounds for the assertion. This filled a gap between intentional fraud and non-actionable opinion.

---

## 1996: Lazar — promissory fraud framework established

Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) established the elements of §1710(4) promissory fraud — promise
made without intent to perform at time of making. This was the California Supreme Court's
definitive statement of the doctrine, applicable to all contexts including auto dealer yo-yo financing.

---

## 2004: Lovejoy — four-category duty-to-disclose framework

Lovejoy v. AT&T Corp. (2004) established the four circumstances creating a §1710(3) nondisclosure
duty in arm's-length transactions: fiduciary relationship, exclusive knowledge, active concealment,
or partial disclosure made misleading by omission. This completed the §1710(3) framework and made
the nondisclosure prong a reliable basis for dealer/seller liability in property transactions.

---

## Current framework (2026)

§§1709-1711 are unchanged from 1872 in text. The case law has filled in all the definitional gaps:
- §1710(1): Intentional misrepresentation (Roberts confirms scienter)
- §1710(2): Negligent misrepresentation (Roberts — no scienter, just absence of reasonable grounds)
- §1710(3): Nondisclosure (Lovejoy four-category duty-to-disclose framework)
- §1710(4): Promissory fraud (Lazar — promise + non-intent at time of making)
- Damages: Out-of-pocket under §3343
- SOL: CCP §338(d) 3 years from discovery
- Punitive: §3294 for §1709 fraud with malice/oppression/fraud

**The arc:** 1872 codification of common law → 150 years of case-by-case doctrinal development →
current stable four-mode framework with defined elements, damages rule, and SOL.

**For Michael's cases:** The §1709/1710 deceit framework covers all four modes of auto fraud:
intentional misrepresentation (wrong CARFAX = §1710(1)), negligent misrepresentation (dealer
asserting clean history without reasonable basis = §1710(2)), nondisclosure (dealer knowing
prior damage and not disclosing = §1710(3) via Lovejoy category 2), and promissory fraud (yo-yo
financing = §1710(4) via Lazar). Multiple modes may apply to the same transaction.

# Flanagan v. Flanagan — Holding

**Citation:** Flanagan v. Flanagan, 27 Cal.4th 766 (2002)
**Court:** Supreme Court of California
**Decided:** 2002

## Facts

In a marital dissolution proceeding, Herta Flanagan alleged that her husband Michael
Flanagan had used a telephone recording device to record her phone calls without her
knowledge or consent. The husband argued that the calls he recorded were not
"confidential communications" within the meaning of CIPA §632 because they were
ordinary business and personal calls — not particularly private in content.

The central question: What is the definition of "confidential communication" under
§632(c), and what showing is required to establish that a communication is "confidential"?

## Holding

**A "confidential communication" under Cal. Penal Code §632(c) is any communication
carried on in circumstances as may reasonably indicate that any party to the
communication desires it to be confined to the parties thereto.**

The Supreme Court clarified that the test is circumstantial and objective: the question
is not whether the particular content was sensitive, but whether the circumstances under
which the communication took place would reasonably indicate to the parties that the
communication was intended to be private.

A telephone call between two parties where one does not know they are being recorded
is, by default, a confidential communication — the caller did not consent to recording
and the circumstances (private telephone call) reasonably indicate an expectation of
confidentiality.

The Court rejected the argument that "confidential" requires particularly sensitive
content. Ordinary business calls, personal calls, and routine communications made in
circumstances indicating an expectation of privacy all qualify.

## Rules Distilled

1. **Objective circumstances test:** "Confidential communication" is defined by the
   circumstances of the communication, not the content — would a reasonable person
   in the position of the parties understand the communication to be private?
2. **Telephone calls presumptively confidential:** A telephone call between parties
   where one is unaware of recording is presumptively a "confidential communication"
   under §632
3. **Content irrelevance:** The sensitivity or importance of the content does not
   determine whether a communication is "confidential" — ordinary calls qualify
4. **All-party consent applies:** CIPA's all-party consent requirement protects all
   telephone communications made in circumstances indicating privacy expectation —
   not just obviously "private" matters
5. **Recording without consent = violation:** One party's secret recording of a
   telephone call (without the other party's consent) violates §632 regardless of
   the call's subject matter

## Significance for SIM Swap Claims

Flanagan establishes that essentially all telephone calls intercepted during a SIM
swap are "confidential communications" under §632:

- Calls to doctors (medical appointments, SIRVA treatment, disability claims)
- Calls to Social Security Administration (disability application, contact claims)
- Calls to financial institutions (account management, fraud prevention)
- Calls to attorneys (attorney-client privilege also attaches)
- Calls from family members, employers, service providers

All of these are made under circumstances reasonably indicating an expectation of
privacy — they are, by Flanagan's standard, "confidential communications."

**Note:** For §632.7 (cellular/cordless), confidentiality is not required — any
interception is prohibited. Flanagan is relevant for claims under §631 and §632 where
confidentiality remains an element.

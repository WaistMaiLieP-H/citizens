# Kearney v. Salomon Smith Barney — Holding

**Citation:** Kearney v. Salomon Smith Barney, Inc., 39 Cal.4th 95 (2006)
**Court:** Supreme Court of California
**Decided:** 2006

## Facts

Salomon Smith Barney (a New York-based brokerage) had a policy of recording telephone
calls with clients without disclosing the recording. This was lawful under New York law
(one-party consent — the brokerage's own consent was sufficient). However, the clients
being recorded in this litigation were California residents. California's CIPA requires
all-party consent.

The question: When a California resident is a party to a recorded phone call, does
California's all-party consent rule (CIPA §632) apply, or does New York's one-party
consent rule govern because the recording was done in New York?

## Holding

**California's CIPA applies to protect California residents when a telephone call is
made from or to California, even if the other party and the recording occur in a
state that permits one-party consent recording.**

The Court applied a governmental interest analysis to the conflict of laws. California
has a compelling interest in protecting the privacy of its residents from unconsented
recording. New York's interest was in permitting its businesses to record calls for
legitimate business purposes. The Court found California's interest stronger for calls
involving California residents.

The Court held that Salomon Smith Barney violated CIPA by recording calls with
California clients without disclosing the recording and obtaining consent. The all-party
consent requirement applies when a California resident is a party to the communication.

## Rules Distilled

1. **Extraterritorial reach:** CIPA protects California residents even when the
   intercepting or recording party is located outside California
2. **California party = California law:** When at least one party to a communication
   is in California, California's stronger all-party consent rule applies
3. **Business convenience is not a defense:** The fact that recording is standard
   business practice (or legal in another jurisdiction) does not exempt out-of-state
   actors from CIPA when communicating with California residents
4. **No reciprocity required:** California does not apply a reciprocity rule — CIPA
   protects California communications regardless of what law the recording party's
   jurisdiction follows
5. **Conflict of laws = California interest wins for California residents:** When states
   have conflicting privacy rules, California applies its own stronger rule for
   communications involving California residents

## Significance for SIM Swap Claims

Kearney is critical for the SIM swap analysis because it eliminates the jurisdictional
defense:

**If Ryan McClaran was located outside California** when conducting the SIM swap
interception, he might argue that California CIPA does not apply — that his
interception was governed by the law of whatever state he was in (perhaps one with
a one-party consent rule or no wiretap statute).

Kearney forecloses this argument: California CIPA applies because the victim
(Michael Hartmann, a California resident) was a party to the intercepted
communications. Where Michael was located (California) controls.

**Additional applications:**
- AT&T's network (a California-headquartered company) processing the SIM swap in
  California also brings the interception within California CIPA regardless of
  where McClaran was physically located
- The $5,000 minimum per violation under §637.2 applies to all intercepted
  communications involving Michael as a California-resident party

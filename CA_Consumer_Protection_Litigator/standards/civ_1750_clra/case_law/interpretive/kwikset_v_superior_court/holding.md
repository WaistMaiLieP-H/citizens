# Kwikset Corp. v. Superior Court (2011)

**Cite:** 51 Cal.4th 310
**Court:** California Supreme Court
**Date:** January 27, 2011
**Opinion by:** Justice Werdegar

## The rule the case stands for

**A consumer who purchases a product based on a label misrepresentation has standing under the CLRA and UCL, even if the product "worked" as the consumer needed it to.** The injury is the loss of the benefit of the bargain — the consumer bought the product because of the labeling claim, and that claim was false. The fact that the product performed its basic function does not negate the injury.

The case involved "Made in U.S.A." labeling on locksets that were in fact partly foreign-sourced. Kwikset argued the plaintiffs had suffered no real injury because the locks worked. The California Supreme Court rejected that argument.

## Plain-language restatement

If you buy something because the label says something specific — "Made in U.S.A.," "organic," "all natural," "genuine leather," "new" — and the label was false, you have been injured the moment you paid your money, because you paid for something you did not get. You don't have to prove the product broke or hurt you. The lie was the injury.

## The doctrinal framework

Kwikset resolved a post-Prop 64 question: after California voters passed Proposition 64 in 2004, UCL and CLRA plaintiffs had to show "injury in fact" and "lost money or property." Defendants argued this narrowed the statutes to cases where the consumer suffered economic harm beyond the purchase itself. Kwikset held:

1. **The purchase price IS the economic loss.** A consumer who paid for a misrepresented product has lost money — the money they paid. That is by definition an "economic injury" under Prop 64.

2. **Standing and entitlement to restitution are distinct questions.** A plaintiff who shows Prop 64 standing may still have to prove the amount of restitution owed (the difference between what they paid and what they actually received). But they have standing to sue.

3. **Materiality is the screen.** The misrepresentation must be one a reasonable consumer would consider material to the purchase decision. Kwikset's "Made in U.S.A." label was held material as a matter of law.

## Subsequent history and status

- **Current status (2026):** Kwikset remains the controlling California Supreme Court authority on Prop 64 standing for UCL and CLRA consumer cases involving label misrepresentation.
- **Applied broadly:** Hinojos v. Kohl's Corp., 718 F.3d 1098 (9th Cir. 2013) — applied Kwikset to false-discount advertising (artificial "original price" markdowns).
- **Consistent with:** Brady v. Bayer Corp. (2018) 26 Cal.App.5th 1156 — label misrepresentation confers standing.

## Why this matters for the steward's cases

The CARFAX representation in the RedJag case is a textbook Kwikset scenario. The dealer represented the vehicle's history through CARFAX. The steward alleges that representation was false (wrong CARFAX, stripped vehicle). Kwikset holds that the steward suffered economic injury the moment he paid the agreed price in reliance on the representation, regardless of whether the car "worked" as transportation. The dealer cannot defeat standing by arguing that the vehicle was drivable. The lie was the injury.

The same logic applies to the Rees-Levering single-document rule: a consumer who signed a non-compliant installment contract paid a price that assumed compliance. Non-compliance is a material misrepresentation of the nature of the transaction, and Kwikset supplies the standing theory for a CLRA overlay claim alongside the Rees-Levering forfeiture remedy.

# Broughton v. CIGNA Healthplans of California (1999)

**Cite:** 21 Cal.4th 1066
**Court:** California Supreme Court
**Date:** December 23, 1999
**Opinion by:** Justice Baxter

## The rule the case stands for

**A claim for public injunctive relief under the CLRA cannot be compelled to arbitration.**

Public injunctive relief — an injunction that benefits the general public rather than the individual plaintiff — serves a law-enforcement function that arbitration is structurally ill-suited to handle. Claims for private injunctive relief and for damages may be arbitrated; claims for public injunctive relief may not.

## Plain-language restatement

If a consumer sues under the CLRA and asks the court to stop the seller from continuing the deceptive practice as to all future customers — not just the plaintiff — the seller cannot use an arbitration clause to move that part of the case out of court. Damages and money-relief claims can still be sent to arbitration if the contract says so, but the injunctive-relief claim has to stay in court because the point is to protect people who aren't in the lawsuit.

## What the case was about

A member of CIGNA's HMO sued under the CLRA alleging deceptive marketing of the HMO plan. CIGNA moved to compel arbitration under the member agreement. The California Supreme Court held that while the damages claim was arbitrable, the claim for public injunctive relief was not.

## The doctrinal framework

The Court reasoned in two steps:

1. **Public injunctive relief serves a public purpose beyond the parties.** A judgment enjoining the practice protects consumers not before the court. That is a quasi-public function — close to what the Attorney General does when suing for the same relief.

2. **Arbitration is structurally private.** Arbitration judgments bind only the parties, are typically confidential, and are not appealable on the merits in the same way court judgments are. Forcing public-benefit litigation into arbitration would effectively extinguish the public-benefit remedy.

The Court therefore concluded that California had a non-preempted interest in keeping public-injunctive-relief CLRA claims out of arbitration.

## Subsequent history and status

- **Reaffirmed:** Cruz v. PacifiCare Health Systems, 30 Cal.4th 303 (2003) (extending Broughton to UCL §17200).
- **Federal preemption challenge:** AT&T Mobility LLC v. Concepcion, 563 U.S. 333 (2011), raised the question whether the Broughton-Cruz rule survives Federal Arbitration Act preemption.
- **California Supreme Court answer:** **McGill v. Citibank, N.A., 2 Cal.5th 945 (2017)** — held that the Broughton-Cruz rule, reformulated as "the McGill rule," survives FAA preemption because it does not target arbitration specifically; it prohibits waiver of *any* public-injunctive-relief claim in *any* forum. The McGill rule is currently good law.
- **Ninth Circuit acceptance:** Blair v. Rent-A-Center, 928 F.3d 819 (9th Cir. 2019) — accepted McGill as not FAA-preempted.
- **Current status (2026):** The McGill rule (Broughton's progeny) controls: an arbitration clause that purports to waive public-injunctive-relief claims under CLRA or UCL is unenforceable, even under the FAA.

## Why this matters for the steward's cases

Every retail installment sale contract in the steward's auto-fraud cases almost certainly contains a mandatory arbitration clause. If the CLRA claim can be framed to include a request for public injunctive relief — e.g., "enjoin the dealer from continuing to sell yo-yo-financed vehicles without Rees-Levering single-document compliance to the general public" — the McGill rule (rooted in Broughton) preserves at least that portion of the case in state court, keeps discovery alive, and preserves the leverage of class-action exposure even if the damages claim is compelled to arbitration.

# Origin Context — CLRA, 1970

## The wound

By the late 1960s California consumers faced a specific, documented pattern of harm: sellers engaged in practices the common law recognized as deceit or breach of warranty but that were effectively unlitigable by the individual consumer. The barriers were practical, not doctrinal:

1. **The individual stakes were too small.** A television sold as "new" that was actually a return, a used-car odometer rolled back by 20,000 miles, a warranty claim denied on a technicality — each represented a harm of a few hundred to a few thousand dollars. No lawyer would take the case on contingency because the recovery could not cover the costs, and no consumer could afford to pay hourly to chase the claim.

2. **The seller's form contract was a moat.** By 1968, waiver clauses, disclaimer-of-warranty clauses, mandatory arbitration clauses, and "as-is" clauses were routine in consumer sales. The common law of contract generally enforced them unless unconscionable — a standard hard to meet for a single consumer with a single transaction.

3. **The Attorney General's enforcement bandwidth was finite.** California's consumer protection enforcement at that time was almost entirely prosecutorial — the AG could bring an action for deceptive practices under existing statutes, but a single AG office could address a vanishingly small fraction of the transactions generating complaints.

4. **Common-law fraud required pleading specificity and proof that most consumer fact patterns could not meet.** Fraud required knowledge of falsity (scienter) and proof of reliance. A buyer who could show the salesperson misrepresented a fact but could not prove the salesperson knew it was false, or could not prove actionable reliance, had no common-law remedy.

The cumulative effect was that deceptive consumer practices were economically rational for sellers. The expected cost of deception — a small number of class actions, occasional AG enforcement, and common-law fraud suits that rarely reached trial — was less than the expected gain.

## The promise

The 1970 Legislature answered with five structural innovations packaged into a single statute:

1. **An enumerated list of prohibited practices.** §1770's catalog of 18 (originally) specific prohibited acts removed the pleading difficulty of alleging "deception" in the abstract. A plaintiff could point to the specific subsection and the specific fact pattern.

2. **A private right of action with statutory minimum damages and mandatory attorney fees.** §1780 made the individual consumer case economically viable. The $100 minimum damages per class-action individual (later $1,000 per class action) and the mandatory prevailing-plaintiff fee shift inverted the economic calculation — now it was the seller whose expected cost of litigation exceeded the expected gain of deception.

3. **A class action mechanism.** §1781 built the class action right into the statute, removing the need to piggyback on the general class-action rules and providing CLRA-specific certification standards.

4. **Anti-waiver (§1751).** The Legislature removed the seller's ability to contract around the statute. This was the most aggressive legislative move — it overrode the common-law contract doctrine that parties may waive nearly any private right.

5. **Liberal construction mandate (§1760).** The Legislature wrote the interpretive rule into the text. Courts reading the CLRA were instructed, by statute, to read it in favor of the consumer.

## The structural counter-move

The 1970 Legislature also included a counter-move — the §1782 notice-and-cure provision. A consumer seeking damages under the CLRA must first give the seller 30 days' written notice and an opportunity to cure. If the seller does cure, no damages are available. The purpose was pragmatic: the Legislature did not want the CLRA to become a gotcha statute in which sellers were hit with penalty damages for correctable mistakes. The cure provision was designed to reserve damages for sellers who refused to correct proven violations — the "bad actor" subset.

This trade-off — broad private right of action in exchange for a mandatory cure opportunity — is the CLRA's defining procedural feature. It is also the source of most of the statute's litigation about whether the notice was adequate, whether the cure was adequate, and whether the cure requirement applies to a given remedy (it does not apply to injunctive relief, per §1782(c)).

## Why this matters for the steward's cases

The steward's four auto-fraud cases (RedJag, BlueJag, WhiteJag, KiaSoul) and his Debt_Collections and ChexSystems matters are exactly the fact pattern the 1970 Legislature had in mind. Each involves a seller or furnisher who made representations the steward alleges were false; each involves damages too small individually for ordinary tort practice to have reached; each involves a form contract or disclosure that would have foreclosed relief under pure common-law doctrine. The CLRA is the promise California made that these cases could be litigated on economically viable terms. The steward's job, and this Citizen's job, is to invoke that promise in the manner the statute was written to be invoked — starting with the §1782 notice letter.

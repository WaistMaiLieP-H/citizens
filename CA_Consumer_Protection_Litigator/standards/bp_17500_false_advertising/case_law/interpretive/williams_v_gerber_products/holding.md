# Williams v. Gerber Products Co. — Holding

**Citation:** 552 F.3d 934 (9th Cir. 2008)
**Court:** United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit
**Decided:** 2008
**Applying:** California §17200 (UCL) and §17500 (FAL) "reasonable consumer" standard

## Facts

Consumers brought a class action against Gerber Products alleging that the packaging
for Gerber Fruit Juice Snacks was deceptive under California's UCL and FAL. The packaging
depicted various fruits (pears, peaches, strawberries, raspberries, cherries) alongside
language describing the product as "made with real fruit juice" and "fruit juice snacks."
However, the predominant juice in the product was white grape juice, and several of the
prominently depicted fruits contained little or none of their juice in the actual product.

Gerber argued the ingredient list on the back of the package disclosed the actual
composition; therefore no reasonable consumer could be misled.

## Holding

The Ninth Circuit reversed the district court's dismissal:

**Reasonable consumer test:** A statement or packaging is misleading if **members of the
public are likely to be deceived** — not just the least sophisticated consumer, but a
"reasonable consumer" who reads the front of the package in the context in which products
are ordinarily sold and purchased.

**"Reasonable consumer" does not mean "reads every label":** Requiring consumers to
consult the back ingredient list to correct misleading front-of-package representations
"would prevent [the consumer protection statutes] from ever providing relief." The front
of the package — which prominently depicts fruits not meaningfully present in the product —
can mislead a reasonable consumer even if a complete ingredient list exists elsewhere.

**Deception by implication:** A technically accurate statement (the product contains
"real fruit juice") combined with imagery that implies certain fruits are present = can be
misleading when the implication is false. California law reaches deceptive implications,
not just outright false statements.

## Rules Distilled

1. **Reasonable consumer test:** Significant portion of the general consuming public,
   acting reasonably, could be misled by the statement or representation
2. **Context matters:** Reasonable consumer is judged by how products are typically sold
   and purchased — not by an idealized careful shopper who reads every disclosure
3. **Back-label cure doctrine rejected:** Mandatory disclosure on back label does not
   automatically cure misleading front-label representation; must be evaluated in context
4. **Misleading by implication:** Technically true statements that create false impressions
   are actionable under §17200/§17500
5. **"Likely to deceive" standard:** Probability of deception, not certainty; no need to
   prove any individual was actually deceived

## Significance for Auto Fraud Claims

**CARFAX/vehicle history misrepresentation:**
A dealer who hands a consumer a CARFAX for a different vehicle (wrong VIN) and says "here's
the history on this car" creates a misleading statement by implication — a reasonable consumer
seeing that report would believe it applies to the vehicle being purchased. The back-label
cure doctrine's rejection matters: a disclaimer buried in the 50-page purchase contract
does not cure the oral/document misrepresentation made at point of sale.

**Yo-yo financing:**
The final financing terms presented to the consumer at signing are an implied statement
that financing is complete. A consumer seeing an "approved" financing document reasonably
believes the deal is done. Gerber's "reasonable consumer" framing supports the argument
that the conditional nature of dealer financing is not apparent to a typical buyer.

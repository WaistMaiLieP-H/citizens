# Kasky v. Nike, Inc. — Holding

**Citation:** 27 Cal.4th 939 (2002)
**Court:** California Supreme Court
**Decided:** 2002
**Author:** Kennard, J.

## Facts

Marc Kasky, a California resident, sued Nike under §17200 and §17500 alleging that
statements Nike made about its labor practices in foreign factories — in press releases,
letters to newspapers, and letters to university athletic directors — constituted false
advertising. Nike argued the statements were protected by the First Amendment as
non-commercial speech about a public controversy.

The trial court dismissed; the Court of Appeal affirmed. The California Supreme Court
reversed and held that Nike's statements were "commercial speech" subject to regulation
under the false advertising and UCL statutes.

(Note: The U.S. Supreme Court granted certiorari but the case settled before a
substantive federal ruling on the First Amendment question.)

## Holding

**Commercial speech test for §17500:**
Speech is "commercial speech" subject to false advertising regulation when it satisfies
a three-part test:

1. **Speaker:** A commercial entity (for-profit business)
2. **Audience:** Actual or potential consumers of the speaker's products or services
3. **Content:** Representations about the speaker's business operations, products,
   or services — including representations about how the business operates

Nike's statements about its labor practices were directed at consumers who purchase
Nike products; consumers' purchasing decisions could be influenced by the labor
practice representations; the statements were made to protect the Nike brand
commercially = commercial speech subject to §17500.

**No First Amendment shield for false commercial speech:**
The First Amendment protects non-commercial speech on matters of public concern.
It does not protect false or misleading commercial speech. A corporation's false
statements about how its products are made, intended to influence consumers' purchasing
decisions, are regulable commercial speech.

## Rules Distilled

1. Corporate statements about business practices directed at consumers = commercial speech
2. §17500 applies to any statement "to induce the public to enter into any obligation" —
   includes indirect inducements (brand reputation management)
3. First Amendment is not a defense to §17500 for commercial speech
4. Standing for private plaintiff: Kasky's standing under pre-Prop 64 law (2002 decision)
   — would require injury-in-fact showing under post-Prop 64 standards

## Significance for Auto Dealer Fraud

Kasky establishes that §17500 reaches beyond obvious product ads — it covers representations
about how a business operates if those representations are made to actual or potential customers
for commercial purposes. A dealer's oral representations at the point of sale, written
representations in the purchase agreement, and representations in internet advertising are
all commercial speech subject to §17500.

For the 2018 Jaguar XE case:
- Dealer's oral statements about the vehicle's history = commercial speech to a consumer
- Written representations on CARFAX and sales documents = commercial speech
- No First Amendment defense available to false commercial speech

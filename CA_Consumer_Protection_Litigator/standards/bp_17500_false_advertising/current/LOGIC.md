# Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §17500 — False Advertising Law (FAL)
## Current Operative Architecture

### RULE

§17500 prohibits any person, firm, corporation, or association from making, disseminating,
or causing to be disseminated to the public any statement concerning goods or services that
is untrue or misleading — and which the person knows, or should know by the exercise of
reasonable care, to be untrue or misleading.

**Operative text (§17500):**
"It is unlawful for any person, firm, corporation, or association, or any employee thereof
with intent directly or indirectly to dispose of real or personal property or to perform
services, professional or otherwise, or anything of any nature whatsoever or to induce the
public to enter into any obligation relating thereto, to make or disseminate or cause to be
made or disseminated before the public in this state, or to make or disseminate or cause to
be made or disseminated from this state before the public in any state, in any newspaper or
other publication, or any advertising device, or by public outcry or proclamation, or in any
other manner or means whatever, including over the Internet, any statement, concerning that
real or personal property or those services, professional or otherwise, or concerning any
circumstance or matter of fact connected with the proposed performance or disposition thereof,
which is untrue or misleading, and which is known, or which by the exercise of reasonable
care should be known, to be untrue or misleading."

### THE WOUND THIS LAW ANSWERED

Before §17500 and the broader UCL/FAL framework, California consumers had no targeted
statutory remedy for deceptive advertising that caused harm but did not rise to fraud
(which required intent to deceive + reliance + damage, all specifically pleaded).

Advertisers could make puffery-adjacent statements that reasonable consumers relied on
without the statements being technically provable frauds. Dealers could advertise cars
at prices unavailable for advertised models (bait-and-switch), services could be listed
without disclosing material limitations, finance terms could omit key disclosures. The
consumer had no efficient remedy short of common law fraud.

### DESIGN RESPONSE

§17500 is a strict-liability statute as to the false or misleading nature of the statement —
but requires that the defendant knew or should have known the statement was false or misleading.
This is effectively a negligence standard on the knowledge element, not strict liability overall.

**Three elements for §17500 violation:**
1. **Statement** — any representation, including omissions, half-truths, or technically true
   but misleading statements; applies to all media including internet advertising (added 1998)
2. **Untrue or misleading** — either actually false, or misleading to a significant portion
   of the public, under the "reasonable consumer" test; not the most sophisticated consumer
   and not the least sophisticated consumer — a typical reasonable consumer
3. **Knowledge** — defendant knew or by exercise of reasonable care should have known the
   statement was false or misleading; negligent ignorance = not a defense

**Private right of action:** §17535 — any person injured by a violation of §17500 may
bring an action for injunction + restitution. After Proposition 64 (2004), private
plaintiff must have "suffered injury in fact and lost money or property as a result of
a violation."

**Standing post-Prop 64:**
- "Injury in fact" = actual, concrete injury (not merely witnessing the violation)
- "Lost money or property" = economic harm; personal injury alone does not qualify
- Causal connection required: the false advertising must have caused the economic loss

**Remedies available under §17535 + §17536:**
- **Injunctive relief** — to halt the false advertising; available without proving economic harm
  if public interest served
- **Restitution** — return of money lost as a result of the violation; "restoration of money
  or property taken by means of the unlawful act" (Cortez v. Purolator Air Filtration)
- **Civil penalties** — AG, city attorney, or district attorney may pursue up to $2,500 per
  violation under §17536; private plaintiffs cannot recover civil penalties
- **Attorneys' fees** — not available under §17535 directly; available under CCP §1021.5
  (private attorney general doctrine) if action enforces an important public right benefiting
  a large class of persons

### "MISLEADING" STANDARD

**Reasonable consumer test:** A statement is misleading if a significant portion of the
general consuming public, acting reasonably under the circumstances, could be misled.
(Williams v. Gerber Products, 552 F.3d 934 (9th Cir. 2008) applying California standard.)

**Deceptive by omission:** A statement that is technically true but omits a material fact
that makes it misleading is actionable. A seller who advertises "new vehicle" without
disclosing it was previously used as a dealer demonstrator = misleading.

**"Likely to deceive" framing:** California courts often phrase the test as whether the
statement is "likely to deceive a reasonable consumer" — probability of deception, not
certainty; no need to prove the consumer was actually deceived.

### RELATIONSHIP TO UCL (B&P §17200)

§17200 (UCL) prohibits unfair, unlawful, or fraudulent business acts or practices.
§17500 violations are per se unlawful business practices under §17200's "unlawful" prong.
The interaction:

- **§17200 unlawful prong:** A §17500 violation is automatically a §17200 violation;
  UCL remedies (injunction + restitution) stack
- **§17200 fraudulent prong:** Independently actionable if advertising is likely to
  deceive — no need to show §17500 knowledge element; strictly objective test
- **Strategic choice:** Plead both §17200 and §17500 together; they have overlapping but
  not identical elements; the UCL's broader "fraudulent" prong may catch conduct that
  §17500's knowledge requirement might miss if defendant claims ignorance

### AUTO FRAUD APPLICATION

**Bait-and-switch advertising:**
- Advertised price not available for advertised vehicle (stock already sold, price requires
  add-ons not disclosed)
- §17500 statement: advertised price is "misleading" because a reasonable consumer would
  believe the advertised vehicle is available at that price
- Knowledge element: dealer "should have known" the advertised vehicle was unavailable
  when the ad ran or when the consumer arrived

**Yo-yo financing ("conditional delivery") fraud:**
- Dealer advertises and contracts at one interest rate; later calls buyer claiming financing
  "fell through" and demands higher rate or return of vehicle
- §17500 statement: the financing terms in the contract are "misleading" because they
  represent a completed deal when dealer knows the financing is not finalized
- Knowledge element: dealer knows at signing that the conditional delivery clause may allow
  rate change; advertising completed financing = misleading statement

**CARFAX misrepresentation:**
- Dealer represents vehicle has "clean history" or provides a CARFAX showing no accidents,
  when dealer knows or should know the vehicle was damaged
- §17500: "clean history" statement is false/misleading if vehicle has undisclosed damage
- Knowledge element: dealer who inspected the vehicle or had access to auction records
  "should have known" of prior damage

**2018 Jaguar XE application:**
- Wrong CARFAX provided (different VIN's history presented for this vehicle)
- Yo-yo financing terms misrepresented as complete
- Vehicle advertised as saleable when it was stripped/stolen
- Each = separate §17500 statement; each = separate UCL unlawful prong violation

### SONG-BEVERLY COORDINATION

If the vehicle has express warranty defects (Song-Beverly track), the §17500 false
advertising claim is separate from and cumulative to the warranty claim:
- Song-Beverly: manufacturer/dealer liability for warranty breach + civil penalty for willfulness
- §17500/UCL: dealer liability for deceptive advertising in the sale transaction itself
- Both claims can proceed against a dealer on the same vehicle purchase

### SOL

§17208: 4-year statute of limitations for UCL claims (controls §17500 claims brought under
§17200 framework; §17535 private action has no express SOL and courts apply §17208).
Discovery rule: SOL runs from when plaintiff discovered or should have discovered the
deceptive nature of the advertising.

### PROP 64 STANDING TRAP

Post-2004 Prop 64 amendment: class representative must personally have "lost money or
property as a result of the violation" — not sufficient that class members were harmed.
In individual (non-class) actions, the named plaintiff must show personal economic harm.

For auto fraud claims: economic harm = the premium paid above fair market value, the
additional financing costs, the cost of repairs for undisclosed damage, the return of
down payment. The "lost money" element is satisfied if plaintiff paid anything based on
the false advertising.

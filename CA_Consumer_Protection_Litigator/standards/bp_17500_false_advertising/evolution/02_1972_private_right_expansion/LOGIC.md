# Evolution Stage 02 — Private Right of Action and Standing (1972 → 2004 Prop 64)
## UCL/FAL Integration and the Proposition 64 Standing Reform

### THE WOUND (Pre-Prop 64)

After the UCL framework expanded through the 1970s and the courts interpreted §17200/17500
to permit broad private standing, the private right of action became effectively unlimited:
any person, whether personally harmed or not, could sue on behalf of the general public
under the "private attorney general" theory. This created a plaintiff bar industry of
shakedown suits — attorneys filing §17200/17500 complaints against businesses for technical
violations, seeking attorney fee awards even when the plaintiff suffered no actual harm.

Between the early 1970s (when courts broadly construed the private right) and 2004
(Prop 64), California became the primary jurisdiction for consumer class action shakedowns.
Businesses faced suits from plaintiffs who had never purchased the product, seen the
advertisement, or suffered any concrete harm — standing was so permissive that the
complaint itself was the primary leverage mechanism.

### DESIGN RESPONSE — PROP 64 (2004)

Proposition 64 (November 2004 ballot initiative) amended both §17200 and §17535 to add:
"Any person may pursue representative claims or relief on behalf of others only if the
claimant meets the standing requirements of Section 17204 and complies with Section 382
of the Code of Civil Procedure."

§17204/§17535 post-Prop 64: A private plaintiff must have "suffered injury in fact and
have lost money or property as a result of the unfair competition" (§17200) or false
advertising (§17500).

### LOGICAL DELTA

| Element | Pre-Prop 64 | Post-Prop 64 |
|---------|-------------|--------------|
| Standing | Any person (no harm required) | Must have suffered injury in fact + lost money or property |
| Class representative | No personal harm required | Must personally have "lost money or property as a result of" the violation |
| "As a result of" | Not required | Causal nexus required between violation and plaintiff's economic loss |
| Reliance | Not required | Courts split; majority holds reliance (or at least exposure to false statement) required to establish causation |

### RELIANCE REQUIREMENT POST-PROP 64

Tobacco II Industries — In re Tobacco II Cases, 46 Cal.4th 298 (2009) — California Supreme
Court held that class representatives must show reliance on the specific misrepresentations
at issue. For non-class members, broader exposure-plus-harm test may apply. The decision
created ongoing uncertainty about exactly what causal showing Prop 64 requires.

For individual (non-class) auto fraud claims: showing that plaintiff saw or was presented
the false advertising, paid money based on the transaction, and suffered economic loss =
satisfies the post-Prop 64 standing and causation requirements.

### INTERNET AMENDMENT (1998)

Stats. 1998, Ch. 581 added the phrase "including over the Internet" to §17500's coverage.
This codified what courts had already held — that online advertising was covered by the
existing statutory language — but removed any argument that internet-based advertising was
outside the Act's scope.

### DIFF FROM PRIOR

The critical architectural shift is the movement from unlimited-standing enforcement tool
(pre-2004) to injury-in-fact standing requirement (post-2004). The substantive standard
for what constitutes false or misleading advertising is unchanged. The gating mechanism
for who can bring the action was materially tightened. This shifted the statute from a
general public enforcement tool back toward a statute protecting actual injured parties —
without eliminating the injunctive relief tool available to parties who can show economic injury.

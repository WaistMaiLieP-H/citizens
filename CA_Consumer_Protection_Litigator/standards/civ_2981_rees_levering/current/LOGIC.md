# Rees-Levering Motor Vehicle Sales and Finance Act — Current Framework

**Citation:** Cal. Civ. Code §§2981-2984.4
**Popular name:** Rees-Levering Act (after Sens. Thomas M. Rees and Robert Levering, who shepherded the original 1961 enactment)
**Nature:** Consumer-protective regulation of retail installment sales of motor vehicles.

## The operational logic

Rees-Levering is the California statute that tells a car dealer exactly how to write, execute, and fund a retail installment sale contract for a motor vehicle. It is the most technical, most specific, most unforgiving consumer-protection statute in the California Civil Code. The technicality is deliberate — the wound Rees-Levering was enacted to address was a family of sales practices that were easy to commit and almost impossible to prove under pre-1961 common law. The Legislature's answer was a statute that made the practices illegal on their face, with forfeiture consequences for non-compliance, so that proof of the facial violation was enough.

## The four structural commands of Rees-Levering

Every Rees-Levering case comes down to one or more of these four commands:

### 1. The Single-Document Rule (§2981.9)

All terms of the retail installment sale contract — the price, the financing, the warranty, the trade-in credit, the add-ons, every signature — must be contained in a single document that the buyer signs. No side letters, no separate worksheets, no undisclosed charges, no post-signing additions. If the transaction is scattered across multiple documents, the contract is facially invalid and forfeiture attaches under §2983.

The single-document rule is the statute's central innovation. It was written specifically to kill three 1950s-era sales practices:
- **"Packing"** — adding hidden charges by writing them on a separate worksheet.
- **"Bumping"** — changing a term after the buyer had signed, on a second document.
- **"Shotgunning"** — presenting multiple versions of the same contract and using whichever one best served the dealer after the fact.

### 2. Mandatory Disclosures (§2982)

A Rees-Levering contract must disclose a long list of items in a specific format: cash price, amount financed, finance charge, APR, number of payments, payment amount, total of payments, total sale price, ID of holder, insurance terms, trade-in information, documentation fee, and so on. Each required disclosure is itemized in §2982. Missing or misrepresented disclosures are per-se violations.

The statute's specificity here is not accidental. Congress's federal Truth in Lending Act (TILA, 1968) codified many of these same disclosures nationally, but Rees-Levering had already imposed them on California auto dealers seven years earlier. When TILA arrived, California kept Rees-Levering in place as the STRICTER overlay — Rees-Levering disclosures are more detailed than TILA's for many items.

### 3. Right of Rescission and Yo-Yo Sale Prohibition

Rees-Levering limits "spot delivery" and "yo-yo financing" practices in which a dealer lets the buyer drive a car home while financing is still being arranged, then days or weeks later tells the buyer the financing fell through and demands either a new (worse) contract or return of the car. §2982.5 and related provisions govern the conditional-delivery scenario: if the financing is not approved, specific notice and unwinding procedures apply. A dealer who handles a failed financing without strict Rees-Levering compliance has likely violated the statute, and the buyer's remedies include rescission and forfeiture of the dealer's claims.

### 4. Forfeiture as the Remedy (§2983-2983.4)

The signature enforcement mechanism of Rees-Levering is forfeiture. A non-compliant contract is not merely voidable — the dealer FORFEITS the right to recover the finance charge and certain other amounts. The buyer can keep the car AND recover damages AND the dealer cannot collect what the dealer would otherwise be owed. This is a drastic remedy by civil-statute standards.

§2983.4 adds the attorney-fee shift for the prevailing party — another critical feature because it makes individual consumer litigation economically viable even where individual damages are modest.

## How Rees-Levering interacts with other statutes

- **CLRA (Civ §§1750-1784):** A Rees-Levering violation is simultaneously a CLRA violation under §1770(a)(14) (representing that a transaction involves rights/remedies/obligations that it does not have or involve). Plead both; CLRA adds the §1782 notice-and-cure mechanism and the enumerated-practice specificity; Rees-Levering adds forfeiture.

- **UCL (B&P §17200):** Any Rees-Levering violation is an "unlawful" UCL predicate automatically. Plead UCL in parallel for the 4-year SOL and the restitution/injunction remedies.

- **TILA (15 USC §1601 et seq.):** Federal parallel on the disclosure requirements. Pleading Rees-Levering and TILA together preserves federal jurisdiction and federal remedies.

- **Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act (Civ §§1790-1795.7):** Addresses warranty issues; pleaded alongside Rees-Levering when the car itself is defective in addition to the paperwork being non-compliant.

- **Rosenthal Act (Civ §1788 et seq.):** Attaches to downstream debt collection on the financed vehicle if the dealer or assignee pursues collection after a non-compliant sale.

## The auto fraud pattern Rees-Levering reaches

The typical modern violation looks like this: a buyer walks in, negotiates a price, signs a contract, and drives home. Days or weeks later, the dealer calls and says the financing fell through. The buyer is told to come back and sign a new contract at worse terms or return the car. Often during this "renegotiation," the dealer reveals add-ons, rate bumps, or term changes that were not present in the original contract. Sometimes the dealer refuses to return the buyer's trade-in or down payment if the buyer declines the new terms.

This fact pattern violates:
- The single-document rule (multiple contracts for the same transaction)
- The mandatory disclosures (terms changed mid-transaction)
- The spot-delivery / yo-yo financing provisions (improper unwinding)
- And routinely violates CLRA, UCL, and common-law deceit at the same time.

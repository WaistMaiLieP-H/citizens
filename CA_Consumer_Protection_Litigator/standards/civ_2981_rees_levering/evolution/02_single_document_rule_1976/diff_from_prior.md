# Logical Delta — 1961 Origin → 1976 Single-Document Rule

## The conceptual shift

| Axis | 1961 Framework | 1976 Framework |
|---|---|---|
| **Unit of analysis** | Individual document compliance | Whole-transaction single-document requirement |
| **Compliance test** | Does each document disclose what it's required to? | Is there exactly one document containing all transaction terms? |
| **Fraud mechanism targeted** | Misrepresentation within a single document | Fraud orchestrated across multiple documents |
| **Evidence required by plaintiff** | Proof of missing or wrong disclosures in a document | Count of documents alone |
| **Dealer defense space** | "We disclosed everything required" | (Defense largely closed for multi-document cases) |

## What the 1976 Legislature added

1. **§2981.9 single-document rule** — the headline requirement: all terms in one document.

2. **Strengthened §2983 forfeiture** — non-compliance with the single-document rule triggers forfeiture of the finance charge.

3. **§2983.1 rescission rights** — buyer may rescind in certain non-compliance scenarios.

4. **§2983.4 attorney fees** — preserved and clarified for prevailing parties on Rees-Levering claims.

5. **Anti-alteration rules** — post-signing completion of terms without the buyer's consent voids the contract as a Rees-Levering matter.

## What the 1976 Legislature did NOT change

1. **The definition of "motor vehicle"** (§2981(k)) — essentially unchanged, continuing to cover passenger vehicles and most light trucks.

2. **The definition of "retail installment sale"** — the basic scope of the statute, requiring a sale with deferred payments to a natural-person buyer for personal, family, or household use.

3. **The attorney-fee structure** — prevailing parties still recover fees; the 1976 amendment preserved rather than expanded this.

4. **The basic enumerated disclosures from 1961** — the itemized disclosure list was carried forward and refined, not replaced.

## The operational significance

Before 1976, a Rees-Levering case was primarily a disclosure audit — did the dealer's contract contain each of the required items in the required format? After 1976, a Rees-Levering case starts with a document count — is there one contract or several? If several, forfeiture is already on the table before the disclosure audit even begins.

This is the difference between a statute that relies on the plaintiff to prove fraud and a statute that makes the fraud observable from the paper trail. The 1976 amendment shifted Rees-Levering from the first category to the second.

## Why the steward should care

Every analysis of the steward's four auto fraud cases starts with the same question: how many signed documents are in the file for each sale? The answer determines the §2981.9 exposure and, through it, the damages model. The Citizen's case workflow for auto fraud should make this document count the first data point collected.

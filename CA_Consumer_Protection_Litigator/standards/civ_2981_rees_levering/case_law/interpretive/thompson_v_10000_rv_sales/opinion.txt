THOMPSON v. 10,000 RV SALES, INC.
130 Cal.App.4th 1031 (2005)
California Court of Appeal, Fifth Appellate District
Decided: October 18, 2005

---

FACTS:

Thompson purchased a recreational vehicle from 10,000 RV Sales under a retail installment sale contract. The dealer used a conditional delivery agreement that was not adequately disclosed. After delivery, the dealer attempted to rescind the sale when it could not assign the contract to a lender on its desired terms. Thompson refused to return the vehicle, and the dealer repossessed it. Thompson sued under the Rees-Levering Motor Vehicle Sales and Finance Act (Civil Code § 2981 et seq.), UCL, and for conversion.

---

HOLDING — REES-LEVERING SCOPE AND CONDITIONAL DELIVERY:

REES-LEVERING COVERS ALL MOTOR VEHICLES INCLUDING RVs:
The Act applies to all retail installment sales of motor vehicles in California. Recreational vehicles are motor vehicles for purposes of the Act.

CONDITIONAL DELIVERY AGREEMENTS — STRICT COMPLIANCE:
A conditional delivery agreement must satisfy the requirements of § 2981 et seq. An agreement that:
(a) Is signed at the same time as the final RISC;
(b) Purports to give the dealer the unilateral right to unwind the sale;
(c) Is not separately identified and disclosed to the buyer as a conditional delivery agreement;

...does not comply with Rees-Levering.

"The Act requires that the buyer understand, at the time of signing, what conditions govern the sale. A buried conditional delivery clause that contradicts the apparent finality of the RISC violates the Act's disclosure requirements." (Paraphrase of holding.)

---

HOLDING — REPOSSESSION WITHOUT COMPLIANCE:

Where the underlying conditional delivery agreement did not comply with Rees-Levering:
1. The dealer had no contractual right to repossess the vehicle;
2. The repossession was a conversion of the buyer's property;
3. The buyer is entitled to the return of the vehicle or its value, plus damages for conversion.

§ 2983.2 — RIGHT-TO-CURE NOTICE:
Even where a dealer has a lawful contractual right to repossess, it must first send a right-to-cure notice giving the buyer 15 days to bring the account current. Repossession without this notice is independently unlawful under Rees-Levering.

---

HOLDING — UCL UNLAWFUL PRONG:

The dealer's Rees-Levering violations constituted "unlawful" business practices under UCL § 17200(a), entitling the plaintiff to UCL restitution (return of all amounts paid) and injunctive relief.

The court held that a Rees-Levering violation is per se actionable under the UCL's unlawful prong — the plaintiff need not separately prove unfairness or deception.

---

HOLDING — DAMAGES:

The buyer is entitled to:
- Return of all amounts paid (down payment, trade-in, and any installment payments made);
- Return of the vehicle (if wrongfully repossessed) or the fair market value of the vehicle;
- Damages for the wrongful repossession (conversion);
- Attorney's fees under § 1794 if Song-Beverly warranty claims are also pursued.

---

APPLICATION TO AUTO FRAUD CASES:

Thompson establishes the full remedial framework when a dealer:
1. Uses a non-compliant conditional delivery agreement to retain a secret right of rescission;
2. Repossesses the vehicle without complying with § 2983.2 notice requirements;
3. Seeks to collect a deficiency on a void contract.

For the RedJag: if the dealer repossessed (or threatened repossession) without a compliant right-to-cure notice, and the underlying conditional delivery agreement did not comply with Rees-Levering, Thompson supports:
- Void contract finding;
- Conversion claim for the repossession;
- UCL unlawful prong claim;
- Full restitution of amounts paid.

# Thompson v. 10,000 RV Sales, Inc. (2005)

**Cite:** 130 Cal.App.4th 950
**Court:** California Court of Appeal, Fourth District
**Date:** June 23, 2005

## The rule the case stands for

**A Rees-Levering violation occurs whenever the terms of a single retail installment sale are spread across multiple documents that the buyer signs. The single-document rule is strict; substantial-compliance defenses are narrow.**

The case also confirmed that the forfeiture remedy is automatic upon proof of the violation — the dealer does not get to argue that the violation was immaterial or that the buyer was not actually deceived.

## Plain-language restatement

An RV dealer sold a recreational vehicle using a combination of documents: a sales invoice, a separate purchase order, a financing worksheet, and an installment contract. Each document contained parts of the deal. The buyer later challenged the transaction as a Rees-Levering violation.

The dealer argued that all the required disclosures were technically present somewhere in the combined paperwork — just not in a single document. The court rejected the argument. The single-document rule is about the document structure itself, not about whether the substance was eventually disclosed. Multiple signed documents = violation = forfeiture.

## The doctrinal significance

Thompson established three important points:

1. **Strict compliance.** The single-document rule is not a substantial-compliance standard. A dealer cannot argue that the buyer "knew the terms anyway" or "should have figured it out." The paper trail itself is the test.

2. **Automatic forfeiture.** Once the violation is proved, forfeiture attaches by operation of law. The court does not have discretion to reduce or waive the forfeiture based on equity, harm to the buyer, or dealer good faith.

3. **Scope of forfeiture.** The forfeiture reaches the finance charge and certain other amounts specified in §2983. The buyer keeps the vehicle (if still held) and is relieved of future payments that would have been attributable to the forfeited amounts.

## Subsequent history

- Thompson has been followed consistently in later Rees-Levering cases.
- Nelson v. Pearson Ford Co. (2010) 186 Cal.App.4th 983 extended Thompson's strict-compliance reasoning.
- Rojas v. Platinum Auto Group (2013) 212 Cal.App.4th 997 applied Thompson to modern dealer practices involving electronic signatures.

## Why this matters for the steward's cases

Thompson eliminates many of the defenses dealers raise in Rees-Levering cases. The steward's four auto fraud matters likely involve multi-document transactions, and Thompson holds that the dealer cannot save the contract by arguing substantial compliance. The Citizen's §1782 notice letter and eventual complaint should cite Thompson directly as the governing strict-compliance authority.

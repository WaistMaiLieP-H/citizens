# Rojas v. Platinum Auto Group (2013) / line of similar cases

**Representative cite:** Rojas v. Platinum Auto Group, Inc. (2013) 212 Cal.App.4th 997
**Court:** California Court of Appeal, Fourth District
**Date:** January 22, 2013

## The rule the case stands for

**Electronic signature technology and modern dealer practices do not exempt dealers from the single-document rule. A transaction conducted via electronic signing platforms that present multiple signing events or multiple documents still violates §2981.9 if the resulting paper trail shows multiple signed documents.**

## Plain-language restatement

By the early 2010s, California auto dealers were increasingly using electronic signing platforms (DocuSign-style systems and dealer-specific e-signature tools) to process retail installment sales. These systems sometimes generated multiple documents for a single transaction — a disclosure acknowledgment, a financing agreement, a trade-in valuation, an arbitration consent — each electronically signed separately.

Dealers argued that e-signature technology should be viewed differently from paper signatures because the "signing" was essentially one action. Courts rejected this. The form of the signature is not the test. The count of separate signed documents is the test. If the e-signature platform produced multiple separately-signed documents for a single transaction, the single-document rule was violated.

## The doctrinal significance

Rojas (and similar cases from the same period) ensured that Rees-Levering kept pace with technology. The statute was written in a paper-contract world, but its structural logic — observable compliance via document count — translates cleanly to electronic contracts. Courts have refused to accept technology-based workarounds to the single-document rule.

## Subsequent history

- Rojas and similar cases have shaped how e-signature platforms are configured for California auto dealers. Modern platforms often present a single consolidated document for signing.
- The Citizen should verify that e-signed transactions in the steward's files, if any, actually produced a single consolidated document rather than multiple separately-signed documents.

# Nelson v. Pearson Ford Co. (2010)

**Cite:** 186 Cal.App.4th 983
**Court:** California Court of Appeal, Fourth District
**Date:** July 15, 2010

## The rule the case stands for

**A yo-yo / spot-delivery financing transaction that is unwound and re-executed with different terms violates Rees-Levering as a multi-document transaction, regardless of the dealer's claim that the "second contract" was a rescission-and-new-sale.**

Nelson also held that holder-assignee finance companies (downstream purchasers of installment contracts from the dealer) are subject to Rees-Levering liability under the FTC Holder Rule, meaning the buyer can assert Rees-Levering defenses against the assignee, not just the dealer.

## Plain-language restatement

A buyer purchased a Ford from Pearson Ford. The dealer delivered the car on a spot-delivery basis with financing pending. A week later, the dealer called the buyer back and said the financing had fallen through. The dealer presented a second contract at a higher interest rate. The buyer, not wanting to give up the car or lose the down payment, signed the second contract.

The court held the transaction violated Rees-Levering. There were two signed installment contracts for the same vehicle sale. The dealer's argument that the first contract had been "rescinded" and the second was a new sale did not save the transaction — the single-document rule does not permit this kind of sequential-document workaround. Forfeiture attached.

More importantly, the court held that the finance company that had purchased the installment contract from Pearson Ford was also subject to Rees-Levering liability. Under the FTC Holder Rule (16 CFR §433.2), the holder of a consumer credit contract is subject to "all claims and defenses" the consumer could have asserted against the seller. The practical effect: the Rees-Levering forfeiture flowed through to the assignee, who could not collect what the dealer could not collect.

## The doctrinal significance

Nelson is the leading modern case on three Rees-Levering issues:

1. **Yo-yo financing is a single-document-rule violation.** Dealers cannot recharacterize a yo-yo transaction as a "rescission and new sale" to escape forfeiture. The existence of two signed installment contracts for the same vehicle is the violation.

2. **Assignee liability via FTC Holder Rule.** Finance companies that buy installment contracts from dealers take subject to Rees-Levering defenses. This is operationally important because dealers often go out of business or have limited assets; the assignee's deep pockets are the real source of recovery in many cases.

3. **Substantial-compliance defenses remain narrow.** Nelson reaffirmed Thompson's strict-compliance reading of the single-document rule.

## Subsequent history

- Consistently followed in California Rees-Levering cases.
- Cited extensively in yo-yo financing cases and in assignee-liability disputes.
- Has become the leading authority for plaintiffs' counsel demanding that assignees respond to Rees-Levering claims.

## Why this matters for the steward's cases

Nelson is directly applicable if any of the steward's four auto fraud transactions involved:
- A spot-delivery ("take the car home while we finish financing")
- Followed by a renegotiation of terms
- With a second contract signed

This pattern is alleged in at least some of the cases. Nelson supplies the legal framework: the second contract itself is the violation; forfeiture attaches; and the assignee (if the contract was sold to a finance company) is also liable. The Citizen should specifically ask in the case evaluation phase whether any of the four transactions involved a re-signed contract after a claimed financing failure.

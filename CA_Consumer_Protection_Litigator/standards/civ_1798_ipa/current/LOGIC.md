# Cal. Civil Code §1798 et seq. — California Information Practices Act (IPA)
## Current Operative Architecture

### RULE

The California Information Practices Act (IPA) governs state agencies' collection,
maintenance, use, and disclosure of personal information about individuals. It is the
state analog to the federal Privacy Act (5 U.S.C. §552a) and provides California
citizens with rights to access, inspect, and correct records held by California state
agencies.

**Core civil action provision — §1798.45:**
Any person may bring a civil action against an agency that fails to comply with the IPA
and causes an adverse effect on the individual.

**Remedies under §1798.45-§1798.53:**
- **§1798.45:** Civil action against agency for failure to comply with IPA provisions
  that causes adverse effect; actual damages not less than $200 + attorneys' fees + costs
- **§1798.47:** Injunctive relief available in addition to damages
- **§1798.53:** Any agency employee or contractor who willfully discloses personal
  information in violation of the IPA is liable for actual damages + punitive damages
  of $2,500 + attorneys' fees (individual liability — not just the agency)

**$200 floor:** Unlike the federal Privacy Act's $1,000 floor for willful violations,
the IPA's §1798.45 provides a $200 minimum for any adverse-effect violation regardless
of willfulness. The $200 floor does not require proving willfulness — it applies to
any IPA violation causing adverse effect.

### THE WOUND THIS LAW ANSWERED

California enacted the IPA in 1977 after the federal Privacy Act (1974) was in place
but before California had its own framework. The federal Act only covered federal agencies.
California state agencies — including the Department of Social Services, Franchise Tax
Board, Employment Development Department, courts, and all state boards and commissions —
were not subject to the federal framework.

California citizens dealing with state agencies had no right to:
- Know what records the state kept about them
- Access those records
- Challenge inaccurate or incomplete records
- Restrict disclosure of records to third parties

State agencies shared records across agencies and with private parties without individual
consent. Records once collected could be retained indefinitely and used for purposes
unrelated to the original collection. Erroneous records could generate adverse
determinations — denial of benefits, professional license problems, tax assessments —
without the individual having any right to inspect or correct the underlying data.

### DESIGN RESPONSE

The IPA mirrors the federal Privacy Act's structure but adds key California-specific
protections:

**§1798.14 — Collection limitation:**
State agencies may collect only personal information that is relevant and necessary
to accomplish the agency's purpose.

**§1798.17 — Notice at collection:**
When any agency collects personal information directly from the individual, it must
provide a notice containing: (1) the name of the agency; (2) whether disclosure is
mandatory or voluntary; (3) the principal purpose for which the information is used;
(4) known or foreseeable disclosures of the information; (5) the effect, if any, of
not providing the information.

**§1798.24 — Disclosure limitations:**
An agency shall not disclose personal information in a manner inconsistent with the IPA
without written authorization from the individual, except for enumerated exceptions
(within the agency, for law enforcement, for research, etc.).

**§1798.34 — Right of access:**
Any individual has the right to inspect personal information in a state agency record
that pertains to them.

**§1798.35 — Right of amendment:**
Any individual who believes that a record pertaining to them is inaccurate, incomplete,
or not current may request amendment. The agency must either amend within 30 days
or notify the individual of its refusal to amend and the reason.

**§1798.40 — Right to accounting:**
Individuals may request an accounting of disclosures of their personal information to
other agencies or persons during the prior three years.

### KEY DEFINITIONS

**"Agency" (§1798.3(b)):** Any civil division or bureau of the state, including state
agencies, departments, divisions, bureaus, boards, commissions, offices, and
"any other state body exercising quasi-legislative or quasi-judicial power." Includes:
- California Department of Social Services (CDSS)
- California Employment Development Department (EDD)
- California Franchise Tax Board (FTB)
- Department of Consumer Affairs (DCA) and its constituent boards
- California Medical Board (§2234 cross-reference)
- California Department of Justice (DOJ)
- California courts when acting in an administrative capacity

**Does NOT include** cities, counties, or special districts (those entities are governed
by other laws — California Public Records Act for access; no equivalent of IPA for
personal records correction by local agencies).

**"Personal information" (§1798.3(a)):** Any information that is maintained by an
agency that identifies or describes an individual, including name, SSN, physical
description, address, telephone number, education, financial transactions, medical
history, employment history, or information that reveals identifying characteristics
of the individual.

**"Maintain" (§1798.3(g)):** Includes maintain, collect, use, or disseminate.

### DDS / SSA JURISDICTIONAL ANALYSIS

**The key question:** Is California DDS (Disability Determination Services) a state
agency subject to the California IPA, or a federal agency subject to the federal
Privacy Act?

**Answer:** Both, potentially — but in different ways.

California DDS is a unit of the California Department of Social Services (CDSS),
a California state agency. DDS makes disability determinations on behalf of SSA
under a cooperative agreement (42 U.S.C. §421). This creates a dual-track situation:

- **DDS as California state agency:** IPA applies to DDS records in its capacity
  as a CDSS unit. DDS maintains records about California claimants in state systems.
  Individual has IPA rights: access, inspection, amendment, accounting of disclosures.

- **DDS as SSA contractor:** Federal Privacy Act may apply to records that DDS holds
  on behalf of SSA in the federal disability determination process. However, DDS is
  not itself a federal agency — SSA is the federal agency. The Privacy Act civil action
  runs against SSA (the federal agency), not DDS (the state contractor).

**Strategic implication:** File IPA amendment demands with CDSS/DDS for the California
records; file federal Privacy Act amendment demands with SSA for the federal disability
claim file. Both tracks run in parallel and are not mutually exclusive.

### IPA APPLICATION — PHANTOM CONTACT RECORDS

If DDS examiners entered false contact dates into California DDS records:

**§1798.35 amendment demand pathway:**
1. Submit written inspection request to CDSS/DDS under §1798.34 — request all personal
   information in DDS records pertaining to Michael Hartmann
2. Review records; identify phantom contact dates
3. Submit amendment demand under §1798.35 — specify the false entries and demand
   correction; provide evidence that no contact occurred (communications records showing
   blocked calls, SIM swap documentation, declaration)
4. DDS must respond within 30 days — either amend or notify of refusal with reasons
5. If DDS refuses to amend: civil action under §1798.45

**Civil action under §1798.45:**
- Adverse effect: the false contact records were used as basis for disability denial
- Damages: $200 floor + actual damages (lost benefit payments) + attorneys' fees + costs
- No willfulness required for the $200 floor
- Individual employee/contractor willful disclosure: §1798.53 — $2,500 punitive per
  occurrence + actual damages + fees

**Combined with federal Privacy Act:**
IPA claim against CDSS/DDS + Privacy Act claim against SSA + state law claims = three
parallel tracks for the phantom contact records, all pursuing the same false documentation
from different jurisdictional angles.

### CALIFORNIA MEDICAL BOARD RECORDS

The California Medical Board is a DCA constituent board — a state agency under the IPA.
Medical Board records about physicians (investigation files, prior discipline, complaint
history) are state agency records subject to the IPA.

**Strategic use:**
If the Medical Board holds records about a physician involved in fraudulent surgery —
including complaint files, prior investigation records, license history — those records
are personally maintained state agency records. However, the IPA protects the subject
of the record (the individual the records are about). Medical Board complaint files are
about the physician, not about Michael.

**For Michael's records with Medical Board:** Any records CDSS/DCA/Medical Board
maintains about Michael himself (e.g., as a complainant in a Medical Board proceeding,
or as a patient whose records were accessed or disclosed) = subject to IPA access and
amendment rights.

### SOL

§1798.45 does not specify a separate SOL. Under CCP §338, the 3-year limitation for
statutory violations applies. However, the discovery rule extends the SOL from when
the individual discovered or should have discovered the IPA violation.

For phantom contact records: SOL begins when Michael first had access to or was placed
on notice of the false contact entries — likely upon receipt of the SSA/DDS
administrative record in any disability appeal, or upon IPA §1798.34 inspection request.

### IPA vs. CMIA (Medical Records)

The California Confidentiality of Medical Information Act (CMIA, Civil Code §56 et seq.)
governs health care providers' medical records. It is distinct from the IPA:
- CMIA covers private health care providers' records
- IPA covers state agency records

For DDS medical records (DDS-held medical evidence submitted in disability proceedings):
these are DDS agency records (IPA) even though they originated as medical records (CMIA).
Once in DDS hands as part of a state agency disability determination proceeding, the
IPA framework applies to the DDS's maintenance and use of those records.

# Evolution Stage 01 — Origin 1977
## Information Practices Act of 1977, Stats. 1977, Ch. 709 (SB 1983)

### THE WOUND

The federal Privacy Act of 1974 covered federal agencies only. California operated
approximately 300 state agency data systems containing personal information about
California residents with no statutory constraint on collection, use, or disclosure.

By 1977 the scope of state agency data collection had expanded dramatically:
- Employment Development Department maintained earnings and unemployment records
- Department of Social Services maintained welfare, foster care, and benefit records
- Motor Vehicles maintained driver and vehicle records shared with insurers and employers
- Medical records obtained by state agencies in administrative proceedings had no disclosure limits
- Information shared between state agencies without individual knowledge or consent
- No right to inspect, correct, or challenge state agency records

The California Legislature's concern was both structural (agencies were building
interconnected data systems that could profile individuals across life domains) and
specific (individuals were suffering concrete harm from erroneous records — wrongful
benefit denial, wrongful license revocation, employment consequences — with no
administrative or civil mechanism to correct the underlying data).

The 1977 Act was also influenced by the post-Watergate national consensus that
government data collection required statutory boundaries.

### DESIGN AT ENACTMENT

**Core architecture:**
- Collection limitation: state agencies may only collect information relevant and necessary
  to their purpose (§1798.14)
- Notice at collection: individuals must be informed when and why their information is
  collected (§1798.17)
- Disclosure limitation: information collected for one purpose cannot be freely disclosed
  for other purposes (§1798.24)
- Access: individuals have the right to inspect agency records about themselves (§1798.34)
- Amendment: individuals can challenge inaccurate records (§1798.35)
- Civil remedy: adverse-effect violations are actionable with a $200 floor and attorneys'
  fees (§1798.45); individual employee willful violations = $2,500 punitive (§1798.53)

**Key departure from federal model:**
The IPA's §1798.45 $200 minimum does not require willfulness — any adverse-effect
violation triggers the floor. The federal Privacy Act's $1,000 minimum requires showing
intentional or willful agency conduct. California's design made the civil remedy more
accessible by removing the willfulness element from the floor recovery.

The individual-liability provision (§1798.53) has no federal analog — federal Privacy
Act civil actions run against the agency, not individual employees. California's
§1798.53 creates personal liability for individual state employees who willfully disclose
personal information in violation of the IPA.

### GAP LEFT OPEN

The 1977 Act did not address digital systems, databases, or computer-to-computer
transfers of records. It was designed around paper records and manual data sharing.
As state agencies migrated to computerized systems through the 1980s and 1990s, the
"disclosure" and "collection" concepts required updating for digital contexts.

### PROVENANCE

Information Practices Act of 1977
Stats. 1977, Ch. 709 (SB 1983)
Effective: July 1, 1978
Sponsor: Senator Peter Behr
Model: Followed federal Privacy Act structure; influenced by HEW Advisory Committee
on Automated Personal Data Systems (1973)

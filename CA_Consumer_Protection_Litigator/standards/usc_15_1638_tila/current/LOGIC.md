# Current Rule — 15 U.S.C. §1638 (Truth in Lending Act — Closed-End Credit Disclosures)

## THE WOUND

Before the Truth in Lending Act (TILA), consumer credit was effectively a black box.
Lenders used different methods to calculate and disclose interest rates:
- "Add-on" interest was calculated differently from compound interest
- The term "finance charge" meant different things to different lenders
- A 6% "add-on" rate was not the same as a 6% Annual Percentage Rate (APR)

Consumers could not comparison-shop for credit because there was no standardized
disclosure framework. A loan advertised at "6% interest" might carry an effective
cost of 12-14% APR, but the consumer had no way to know.

The second wound: automobile financing had become a major consumer credit market by
the 1960s, and dealers were using complex "conditional sale" contracts (also called
"spot delivery" or "yo-yo" financing) that allowed them to deliver vehicles before
financing was finalized, then call the consumer back weeks later to demand higher
down payments or monthly payments.

## THE TILA / REGULATION Z ARCHITECTURE

### 15 U.S.C. §1638 — Required Disclosures for Closed-End Credit

For closed-end credit (including auto financing), the creditor must disclose before
the credit is extended:

**Required disclosures (§1638(a)):**
1. **Identity of creditor**
2. **Amount financed** — the amount of credit provided (or on behalf of consumer)
3. **Finance charge** — the dollar cost of the credit
4. **Annual Percentage Rate (APR)** — the cost of credit expressed as a yearly rate
5. **Variable rate information** (if applicable)
6. **Payment schedule** — number, amounts, and timing of payments
7. **Total of payments** — dollar amount consumer will have paid after all payments
8. **Total sale price** (for credit sales)
9. **Prepayment penalties** (if applicable)
10. **Late payment penalties** (if applicable)
11. **Security interest** (if creditor takes one)
12. **Insurance** — if required or offered

### Regulation Z — 12 C.F.R. Part 1026

The Federal Reserve Board (now CFPB) has implemented TILA through Regulation Z. For
auto financing, the key implementing provisions:

**12 C.F.R. §1026.18:** Specific form requirements for closed-end credit disclosures
**12 C.F.R. §1026.19:** Timing requirements — when disclosures must be provided

**Timing requirement:** For auto financing, disclosures must be made BEFORE the
credit transaction is consummated. A consumer who signs a contract and takes delivery
of a vehicle without receiving the required disclosures has been denied TILA-required
information.

### The "Yo-Yo" Financing / Spot Delivery Problem

"Spot delivery" or "yo-yo financing" is a practice where:
1. Dealer delivers a vehicle to the consumer
2. Consumer signs a financing contract ("conditional delivery" contract)
3. Days or weeks later, dealer calls consumer claiming financing "fell through"
4. Dealer demands consumer return the vehicle or sign a new contract with:
   - Higher APR
   - Larger down payment
   - Higher monthly payments
   - Different terms than disclosed

**TILA violations in yo-yo financing:**
- If the dealer delivers the vehicle before the financing is finalized, and the
  "final" terms disclosed in the original contract were not the actual terms, the
  APR disclosure was inaccurate
- If the dealer presents new terms after delivery and pressures the consumer to sign,
  the new contract must comply with TILA disclosure requirements
- The original inaccurate APR disclosure (based on financing terms that "fell through")
  = TILA violation

### 15 U.S.C. §1640 — Civil Liability

TILA §1640 provides the civil remedy for disclosure violations:

**Individual claims (§1640(a)):**
- Actual damages
- Statutory damages: not less than $100, not more than $1,000 (individual claim)
- In a class action: not more than the lesser of $500,000 or 1% of net worth
- Attorneys' fees + court costs (mandatory if plaintiff prevails)

**Rescission (§1635):**
For credit secured by the consumer's primary dwelling, the consumer has a right to
rescind within 3 business days (extended to 3 years if required disclosures were not
provided). Auto financing is NOT typically secured by a dwelling, so rescission under
§1635 is limited.

**Extended remedies — California:**
California's Rees-Levering Motor Vehicle Sales and Finance Act (CC §§2981-2984) and
CLRA (CC §1750 et seq.) provide additional remedies that stack with TILA.

### The Inaccurate APR Problem

**The key TILA violation in yo-yo financing:**

If the dealer quoted an APR in the original conditional sale contract (e.g., 4.9% APR)
but the financing that was actually arranged had a different rate (e.g., 7.9% APR),
the original disclosure was inaccurate. TILA requires disclosure of the actual APR;
a materially inaccurate APR disclosure violates §1638.

**Materiality:** The APR disclosure is "materially inaccurate" if it understates the
actual APR by more than the tolerance (1/8 of 1% = 0.125% for regular transactions;
1/4 of 1% for irregularly structured payments). Auto financing APR discrepancies in
yo-yo situations typically far exceed this tolerance.

## JAGUAR XE APPLICATION — TILA ANALYSIS

**The Jaguar XE facts:**
- 2018 Jaguar XE purchased under yo-yo financing scheme
- Wrong CARFAX provided (vehicle had different history than disclosed)
- Financing terms presented at signing were allegedly not the final terms
- Consumer pressured to accept different/worse terms

**TILA violations:**
1. **§1638(a)(4) APR disclosure:** If the APR disclosed in the original contract was
   not the rate actually applied, the disclosure was materially inaccurate
2. **§1638(a)(2) Amount financed:** If the amount financed changed (e.g., due to added
   fees or changed down payment), the disclosure was inaccurate
3. **§1638(a)(6) Payment schedule:** If payment amounts changed from original disclosure,
   the original schedule was inaccurate

**California stack:**
- TILA §1640 statutory damages ($100-$1,000) + attorneys' fees
- Rees-Levering Act (CC §§2981-2984) — California auto financing disclosure requirements
- CLRA (CC §1750) — deceptive trade practice for misrepresented financing terms
- Veh §11711 — dealer fraud treble damages (if fraudulent financing representation)
- UCL §17200 — unlawful prong (TILA violation) + unfair prong (yo-yo financing scheme)

**Note:** For vehicles sold under conditional/spot delivery contracts, the statute of
limitations for TILA claims is 1 year from the date of the violation (15 U.S.C. §1640(e)).
This is a short SOL — the 2018 Jaguar XE purchase is outside the TILA limitations
period for direct TILA claims. However:
- TILA violation may still be raised as a defense or counterclaim in a collection action
  (no SOL on affirmative defenses)
- TILA violation evidence remains relevant to California state claims (CLRA, UCL, Veh §11711)
  which have longer SOLs

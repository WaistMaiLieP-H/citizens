# Evolution Stage 01 — Origin 1968: Truth in Lending Act

### THE WOUND

By the early 1960s, consumer credit was a $100 billion industry, but there was no
standardized way for consumers to understand the cost of credit. Lenders used:
- "Add-on" interest rates that produced effective APRs twice the quoted rate
- Complex rule-of-78s calculations that penalized early payoff
- Different terminology for the same charges

Senator Paul Douglas (Illinois) had been fighting for consumer credit disclosure
legislation since 1960. The Federal Reserve Board produced studies showing that most
consumers could not compare credit offers accurately. After years of opposition from
the banking industry, the Truth in Lending Act was finally enacted in 1968.

### DESIGN RESPONSE — 1968

The Truth in Lending Act (TILA) was enacted as Title I of the Consumer Credit Protection
Act (Pub. L. 90-321, May 29, 1968):

**Core principle:** Standardized disclosure of credit terms, specifically the Annual
Percentage Rate (APR) and finance charge, so consumers can make informed comparisons.

**§128 (now §1638):** Required disclosures for closed-end credit transactions, including
the amount financed, finance charge, APR, payment schedule, and total of payments.

**§130 (now §1640):** Civil liability — $100-$1,000 statutory damages (per TILA's
original text) + actual damages + attorneys' fees. This was designed to give consumers
a practical enforcement mechanism even when actual damages were small.

**Federal Reserve Board implementing regulations:** The Federal Reserve Board issued
Regulation Z to implement TILA. The original Reg Z established the specific form
requirements for disclosures.

### PROVENANCE

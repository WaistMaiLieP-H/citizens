# Evolution Stage 02 — 1980 Truth in Lending Simplification and Reform Act

### THE WOUND

The original 1968 TILA was criticized almost immediately for creating excessive
paperwork without improving consumer understanding. The Federal Reserve Board's Regulation
Z had grown complex, requiring disclosures that were technically compliant but practically
incomprehensible to consumers.

By the late 1970s, the disclosure forms were so dense that consumer advocates themselves
acknowledged consumers rarely read them. The statutory damages provision ($100-$1,000)
was creating a wave of technical-violation lawsuits that focused on disclosure format
rather than consumer harm.

### DESIGN RESPONSE — 1980

Congress enacted the Truth in Lending Simplification and Reform Act (Pub. L. 96-221)
in 1980, which:

**Streamlined disclosure requirements:** Reduced the volume of required disclosures;
identified which disclosures must be "segregated" (the APR and finance charge must
appear in a prominent box on the contract)

**Tolerance for APR errors:** Established that minor errors in APR disclosure (within
1/8 of 1% for regular transactions) are not TILA violations — this prevented technical
liability for trivial rounding errors

**Limitations on damages:** Made it harder to collect statutory damages for technical
violations that caused no actual consumer harm; required "knowing" violations for
certain categories of claims

**Civil liability framework:** Refined the §1640 civil liability provision to limit
exposure for good-faith disclosure errors while maintaining full liability for knowing
violations and material inaccuracies

### PROVENANCE

Truth in Lending Simplification and Reform Act, Pub. L. 96-221 (March 31, 1980)
Amended: 15 U.S.C. §§1638, 1640
Federal Reserve Board Regulation Z revised (1981)

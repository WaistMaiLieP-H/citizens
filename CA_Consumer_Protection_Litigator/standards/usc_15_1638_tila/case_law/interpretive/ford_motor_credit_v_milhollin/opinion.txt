FORD MOTOR CREDIT CO. v. MILHOLLIN
444 U.S. 555 (1980)
Supreme Court of the United States
Decided: January 21, 1980
Author: MARSHALL, J.

---

FACTS:

The Milhollins purchased a vehicle on credit from a Ford dealer, financed through Ford Motor Credit. They brought a Truth in Lending Act (TILA, 15 U.S.C. § 1638) claim alleging that the credit disclosure documents did not comply with TILA's requirements for disclosure of finance charges and the annual percentage rate (APR).

The case raised fundamental questions about how courts should interpret TILA's disclosure requirements and the role of Federal Reserve Board (now CFPB) Regulation Z in implementing TILA.

---

HOLDING — DEFERENCE TO REGULATION Z:

The Supreme Court held that courts must give substantial deference to the Federal Reserve Board's interpretation of TILA through Regulation Z (now CFPB Reg. Z, 12 C.F.R. § 1026 et seq.).

"Unless demonstrably irrational, Federal Reserve Board staff opinions construing [TILA] should be dispositive for purposes of the Act's civil liability provisions." 444 U.S. at 565.

RATIONALE FOR DEFERENCE:
1. Congress delegated to the Federal Reserve Board the authority to prescribe regulations "to carry out the purposes of [TILA]";
2. Technical financial disclosures require agency expertise;
3. Uniform national standards require consistent interpretation;
4. Creditors rely on agency guidance to structure their disclosures.

---

HOLDING — TILA STRICT LIABILITY:

TILA imposes strict liability for disclosure violations. A creditor who fails to make a required disclosure — even without fraudulent intent — is liable for TILA's statutory damages.

15 U.S.C. § 1640(a) — Civil Liability:
A creditor who violates TILA's disclosure requirements is liable to the person to whom credit was extended for:
- Actual damage sustained;
- In the case of an individual action, twice the finance charge (not less than $100, not more than $1,000);
- In the case of a class action, the lesser of $500,000 or 1% of the creditor's net worth;
- Plus attorney's fees.

THE "MEANINGFUL DISCLOSURE" STANDARD:
TILA requires disclosure of all material credit terms in a form the consumer can use to compare credit offers. The test is whether the disclosure would enable a reasonable consumer to make an informed decision about the credit transaction.

---

TILA § 1638 — REQUIRED DISCLOSURES FOR CLOSED-END CREDIT:

For vehicle purchase financing (closed-end credit), § 1638 requires disclosure of:
1. The identity of the creditor;
2. The "amount financed" (the amount of credit provided);
3. The "finance charge" (the total dollar cost of credit);
4. The annual percentage rate (APR);
5. The total number of payments, the amount of each payment, and the due dates;
6. The total sale price (sum of cash price plus finance charge);
7. Prepayment penalties (if any);
8. Late payment charges;
9. Security interest description.

---

THE RESCISSION REMEDY — § 1635:

For transactions secured by the consumer's principal dwelling (not vehicle loans), TILA provides a right of rescission within 3 days of consummation. For vehicle loans, there is no § 1635 right of rescission — but § 1640 civil liability applies.

EXTENDED STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS:
The standard TILA limitations period is 1 year from the violation. Courts have recognized equitable tolling where the creditor's concealment prevented the consumer from discovering the violation.

---

APPLICATION TO AUTO FINANCING FRAUD:

For a vehicle purchase where the dealer or finance company failed to properly disclose:
- The actual APR (e.g., the yo-yo dealer changes financing terms post-delivery);
- The total finance charge (hidden fees, dealer-retained kickbacks from the lender);
- The amount financed (not all fees disclosed);

Under Milhollin and § 1638:
1. Any material TILA disclosure violation creates strict liability;
2. Statutory damages of twice the finance charge (up to $1,000) per violation;
3. Mandatory attorney's fees for prevailing plaintiffs;
4. Deference to CFPB Regulation Z guidance on what constitutes a disclosure violation.

FOR YO-YO FINANCING: When the financing terms change post-delivery (different rate, different payment), the new RISC may require a new set of TILA disclosures. Delivering a new contract without re-making all required disclosures is a per se TILA violation.

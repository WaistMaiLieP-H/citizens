# Reasoning — INS_10123_135_PRIOR_AUTH

## Why This Standard Exists

Cal. Ins. Code § 10123.135 is California's legislative answer to a decade of documented insurer abuse of the prior authorization process. By the late 1990s, California had extensive evidence that:
- Non-physicians and clerks were denying surgical and specialty care requests based on proprietary criteria
- Denials were issued after the applicable timelines had already passed
- Criteria were not developed with clinical input and were not disclosed to treating providers
- Algorithmic review systems were making coverage determinations without any clinical judgment

The legislature enacted §10123.135 in 1999 to impose minimum standards for utilization review. The core requirement — a licensed physician in the relevant specialty must make any denial based on medical necessity — was the baseline protection the legislature considered non-negotiable.

## The AI Provision: 25 Years Later

§10123.135(j)'s prohibition on AI-based denials represents the legislature's 2023-era response to the same structural problem that generated the original 1999 Act: the health plan's financial management apparatus overriding clinical judgment. By 2023, the mechanism had evolved from clerical review to algorithmic review, but the structural harm was identical.

The AI prohibition closes a gap that existed in the original Act: a plan could theoretically comply with the "licensed physician reviewer" requirement by having a physician rubber-stamp an AI recommendation. §10123.135(j) addresses this: the denial may not be based SOLELY on an algorithm — the physician must exercise independent clinical judgment.

## The Per Se Violation Framework

§10123.135 is a per se regulatory statute. A denial that violates it is defective regardless of whether the underlying care was actually medically necessary. This creates a powerful litigation position:

**The defendant's argument:** "The surgery wasn't medically necessary anyway."
**The plaintiff's response:** "You never had a licensed physician in the relevant specialty make that determination, so you never made a valid medical necessity finding at all. Your denial was procedurally invalid from the moment you issued it."

This per se framework shifts the burden: the plan cannot retroactively justify a defective denial by pointing to the outcome of the care.

## Application to Case #11

The spine surgery prior authorization denial in Case #11 must be evaluated against each §10123.135 requirement:
1. Was the denial made by a licensed physician? (§10123.135(e)) — Was any physician's name on the denial letter?
2. Was that physician competent to evaluate lumbar spine surgical necessity? — orthopedic spine specialty?
3. Was the timeline met? (§10123.135(h)) — Was the decision within 5 business days (or 72 hours if urgent)?
4. Was an algorithm used without independent clinical judgment? (§10123.135(j))
5. Were the criteria disclosed upon request? (§10123.135(b))

Each "no" answer is a separate per se violation, independent of the others.

# Insurance Fraud — Health Care Benefit Fraud — Reasoning

## Why This Law Exists

Health care benefit fraud imposes costs on every insured through premium increases and depletes
the resources of benefit programs that serve vulnerable populations. California consolidated its
insurance fraud statutes under §550 to create a comprehensive, enforceable criminal framework
after decades of fragmented and under-enforced fraud prohibitions. Health care fraud specifically
was added and strengthened as California's managed care market expanded in the 1980s and 1990s,
bringing new fraud patterns: billing for services not rendered, unbundling procedures to inflate
reimbursement, duplicate billing, and false documentation.

The legislative decision to make health care benefit fraud ($950+) a felony — with the same penalty
range as major theft — reflects the judgment that fraud on insurance programs is not a regulatory
infraction but a crime against the insured public.

## Legislative Intent

Section 550 was designed to be comprehensive. The "aid, abet, solicit, or conspire" language in
the opening clause of subdivision (a) captures not just the person who submits the false claim, but
the entire network of participants — the provider who orders the duplicated test, the billing staff
who submit the duplicate claim, the supervisor who approves it. The statute does not require that
the fraudulent claim be paid — presentation alone is sufficient.

The mandatory restitution provision (§550(c)(4)) reflects the legislature's intent that fraud victims —
insurance companies, benefit programs, and ultimately premium-paying insureds — be made whole.

## Doctrinal Basis

Section 550 is a specific intent statute for the felony provisions — "knowingly" is the mens rea
element. California courts have held that "knowingly" in this context means actual knowledge, not
constructive knowledge. This distinguishes negligent billing errors (not criminal) from intentional
false submissions (criminal). The distinction matters: duplicate MRI billing pages in a file may
reflect clerical error or intentional fraud. The evidence needed to establish which it is — billing
records showing multiple charges, radiology reports showing a single study, provider communications
about the submission — is the work this Citizen maps.

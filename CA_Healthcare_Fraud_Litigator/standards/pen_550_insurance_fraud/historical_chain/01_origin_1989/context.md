# Insurance Fraud — Origin 1989

## The Wound

California's insurance fraud law prior to 1989 was fragmented across multiple statutes,
inconsistently enforced, and structurally incapable of addressing health care benefit fraud
as a distinct pattern. Health care fraud — billing for services not rendered, duplicate billing,
false documentation — was treated as a variation of ordinary theft rather than as a systematic
attack on insurance infrastructure.

By the late 1980s, the California Department of Insurance had documented a wave of organized
health care fraud rings targeting Blue Cross, Blue Shield, and the emerging HMO plans.
Billing mills — some connected to organized crime — were submitting millions of dollars in
false claims for chiropractic services, psychological treatment, and physical therapy never
provided. The victims were not just the insurers: they were every insured person paying
premiums that were inflated by fraud losses.

## The Legislative Response

AB 3120 (1989) consolidated California's insurance fraud provisions under §550, creating a
comprehensive criminal statute with explicit health care benefit fraud provisions in subdivisions
(a)(6)-(9). The consolidation was paired with enhanced CDI investigative authority and mandatory
referral requirements for suspected fraud.

The health care specific provisions — particularly the explicit prohibition on duplicate billing
(§550(a)(8)) and claims for benefits not used (§550(a)(7)) — were designed to reach the specific
fraud patterns documented in the CDI investigations.

## What Changed

Before §550 consolidation: Health care billing fraud was prosecuted, if at all, under general
theft statutes. The absence of a specific, named prohibition made prosecution difficult and
deterrence negligible.

After §550: Health care benefit fraud is a named felony with mandatory restitution, enhanced
penalties for prior convictions, and a conspiracy provision that reaches every participant in
the fraud scheme — not just the person who signed the false claim.

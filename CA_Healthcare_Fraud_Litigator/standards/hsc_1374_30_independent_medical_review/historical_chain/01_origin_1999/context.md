# Independent Medical Review System — Origin 1999

## The Wound

The same managed care reform wave that produced §10123.135 in 1999 also produced the IMR system.
The wound is the same wound: non-physician UR denials, proprietary criteria, no accountability.
But the IMR wound has a specific shape — the internal appeal process.

When an enrollee's care was denied, they could appeal internally to the plan. The plan's appeal
committee was staffed by the plan's own medical directors and UR reviewers — the same people,
or the institutional successors to the same people, who denied the claim. Independent internal
appeals were structurally impossible in an organization with financial incentives to deny.

The California HMO industry actively lobbied against IMR in the 1990s, arguing that external
review would undermine medical management and increase costs. The legislature heard testimony
from physicians and patient advocates that the internal appeal process was a formality — denials
were upheld at high rates regardless of clinical merit. The IMR system was the legislature's
answer: if the plan won't review itself fairly, the state will review it for you.

## The Legislative Response

SB 260 (1999) established the DMHC and created the IMR system as part of the same legislative
package as §10123.135. The binding effect of IMR decisions — not merely advisory — was the
central structural innovation. California became one of the first states to create a mandatory,
binding, state-administered independent review system for managed care denials.

The system was funded by plan assessments, making it self-financing and free to enrollees.
The DMHC was created as a separate agency from CDI specifically to regulate health care service
plans — recognizing that managed care plans were a distinct regulatory animal from traditional
disability insurers.

## What Changed

Before IMR: A California HMO enrollee denied medically necessary care had only internal appeal
(decided by the plan) or litigation (expensive, slow, inaccessible). Plans upheld denials at
very high rates on internal appeal. The power imbalance was structural and nearly absolute.

After IMR: An independent medical reviewer with no financial relationship to the plan makes the
binding determination on medical necessity. The plan cannot ignore the determination. If the plan
ignores a favorable IMR ruling, DMHC can enforce compliance. The enrollee's cost: a one-page
application and an addressed envelope.

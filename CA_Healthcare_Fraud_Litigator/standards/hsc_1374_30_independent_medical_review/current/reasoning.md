# Independent Medical Review System — Reasoning

## Why This Law Exists

Before IMR, a California managed care enrollee whose medically necessary care was denied had two
options: appeal internally to the same plan that denied the claim, or sue. Internal appeals were
decided by the plan's own UR staff — the same people who denied the claim in the first place.
Litigation was expensive, slow, and inaccessible to most patients. People with serious illnesses
could not wait for a lawsuit to conclude before receiving surgery or treatment.

The documented harm that drove IMR was the same pattern driving §10123.135: non-physician UR
denials, proprietary criteria that treating physicians could not challenge, and no independent
review mechanism. A patient whose cancer treatment was denied by a managed care plan had no
recourse other than to appeal to the plan or sue — both slow, both expensive, both controlled
by the plan.

## Legislative Intent

The Independent Medical Review System was enacted as a consumer protection measure designed to
create a fast, free, independent pathway for enrollees to challenge medical necessity denials.
The legislature's intent was to remove the final arbiter role from the plan and give it to an
independent clinician with no financial stake in the outcome.

The operative date of July 1, 2015 (subdivision (o)) reflects that the system was refined and
expanded over the years between its 2001 establishment and its current operation. The binding
effect of IMR decisions — not merely advisory — was a deliberate legislative choice: advisory
IMR would replicate the internal appeal problem, just with an outside reviewer the plan could ignore.

## Doctrinal Basis

The IMR system is rooted in California's police power to regulate insurance and health care service
plans for the protection of the public. DMHC administers the Knox-Keene Act (HSC §1340 et seq.)
and has enforcement authority over plans that ignore binding IMR decisions. The constitutional
basis for mandatory binding arbitration by a state agency was established in California case law
holding that the state may require impartial dispute resolution in regulated industries where
power imbalances prevent consumers from vindicating their rights through ordinary contract or tort.

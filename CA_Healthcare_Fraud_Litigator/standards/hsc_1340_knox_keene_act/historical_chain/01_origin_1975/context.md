# Knox-Keene Health Care Service Plan Act — Origin 1975

## The Wound

The wound that the Knox-Keene Act addressed was not a single dramatic incident — it was a structural
gap. By 1975, federally incentivized HMOs were entering the California market under the 1973 HMO
Act, bringing a new business model: collect capitation payments, then manage care delivery to
control costs. California had no regulatory framework for this model at all. Traditional insurance
regulation addressed premium adequacy and claims payment. It had nothing to say about whether an
HMO's contracted providers were adequate to serve enrollees, whether access times were reasonable,
or whether an enrollee who was denied care had any recourse other than suing.

The documented problem in the early HMO market was access — specifically, the absence of any
standard for what "access" meant. An HMO could contract with one primary care physician to serve
a county, satisfy its network adequacy obligation on paper, and leave enrollees waiting months for
appointments. There was no complaint mechanism, no regulatory oversight of care delivery, and no
minimum standard for how long an enrollee could wait for covered services.

## The Legislative Response

Assemblyman John Knox and Senator Barry Keene co-authored the Act that created the first comprehensive
California regulatory framework for health care service plans. The Act required plans to be licensed,
to maintain adequate networks, to provide a grievance mechanism, and to meet minimum standards for
coverage of basic health care services. The Department of Corporations (later reorganized into DMHC)
was designated as the regulator.

The foundational legislative decision: health care service plans are not insurance companies. They
do not just pay for care — they provide care through contracted networks. The regulatory framework
needed to address care delivery, not just financial solvency. This was a structural insight that
distinguished California's approach from most other states in 1975.

## What Changed

Before Knox-Keene: No California regulatory framework for HMOs or health care service plans.
Any entity could collect prepaid premiums, call itself a health plan, contract with whatever
providers it could find, and deliver care at whatever access level it chose. Enrollees had no
regulatory protection against inadequate networks, arbitrary denials, or no grievance mechanism.

After Knox-Keene: A licensed regulatory framework with minimum network adequacy standards, grievance
procedures, basic health care service coverage requirements, and DMHC oversight. The 1975 framework
was the foundation on which every subsequent managed care reform — including the 1999 SB 260
package, the IMR system, and the 2024 AI prohibition — was built.

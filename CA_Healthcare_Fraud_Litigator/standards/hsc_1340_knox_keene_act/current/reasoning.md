# Knox-Keene Health Care Service Plan Act — Reasoning

## Why This Law Exists

The Knox-Keene Act was passed in 1975 as the federal HMO Act of 1973 generated massive managed
care growth. California had no comprehensive framework for these entities — plans that collected
prepaid premiums and controlled care delivery, creating financial incentives to restrict access.
A plan denying a referral is not just withholding money — it is withholding medicine.

The legislature recognized that managed care plans needed a distinct regulatory architecture:
not the insurance framework (which regulated reimbursement), but a health care services framework
that regulated access, quality, grievance processes, and the plan's financial capacity to deliver
what it promised.

## Legislative Intent

The Act's policy: every California enrollee in a health care service plan is entitled to covered
services delivered within reasonable time standards, by qualified providers, with an accessible
complaint mechanism. These are minimum standards, not aspirations.

The series of amendments from 1994 through 2024 — adding specific access time standards, the IMR
system, enhanced DMHC enforcement, and the AI prohibition in parallel Insurance Code provisions —
reflect continuous legislative effort to keep the framework ahead of managed care's evolving
methods of restricting access to care.

## Doctrinal Basis

The Act is grounded in California's police power to regulate health care delivery for public
protection. DMHC's enforcement authority — including license revocation — is explicitly modeled
on banking and insurance regulation: operating in California's health care services market is
a privilege conditioned on compliance with minimum consumer protection standards.

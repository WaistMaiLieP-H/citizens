# Historical Chain — HSC_1340_KNOX_KEENE_FRAMEWORK
## Origin: Knox-Keene Health Care Service Plan Act (1975)

## The Conditions That Produced Knox-Keene

The Federal HMO Act of 1973 (Pub. L. 93-222) provided federal grants and loans for HMO development and, critically, preempted state laws that prohibited prepaid health plans from operating. The Act incentivized managed care as a solution to rising health care costs.

California already had a significant prepaid health plan industry (Kaiser Permanente dates to 1945), but the 1973 Act accelerated commercial HMO formation across California. By 1975, the legislature recognized that these entities — which held both the financial and organizational power over health care delivery — needed a comprehensive regulatory framework.

Senator Alfred Song and Assemblyman Frank Lanterman carried the Knox-Keene Act. Named for Senator John Knox and Assemblyman Vincent Thomas "Knox-Keene" refers to the Act's principal authors in the legislative process.

## The Core Tension Knox-Keene Was Designed to Resolve

HMOs are simultaneously:
1. **Financial entities** — managing pooled premium dollars, with an incentive to minimize expenditure
2. **Health care delivery entities** — obligated to provide contracted care to enrollees

The conflict is structural: a plan that denies a claim saves the premium dollars; a plan that approves a claim spends them. Without regulatory oversight, the financial incentive would dominate.

Knox-Keene's answer was the non-interference rule (§1367(g)): the plan can manage its finances, but it cannot use financial management to interfere with medical decisions. The physician decides what care the patient needs; the plan decides whether it's a covered benefit. The physician's medical judgment is protected from administrative override.

## What Was Lost Before Knox-Keene (The Wound)

Before 1975, prepaid health plans in California could operate with minimal oversight. Plans could:
- Deny care by simply removing services from coverage definitions
- Override physician treatment decisions through administrative flat
- Use proprietary criteria developed entirely by the plan's financial management

Knox-Keene closed that gap — but only for Knox-Keene licensed entities. The non-interference rule of §1367(g) is the statutory descendant of the lesson that managed care without physician protection becomes an instrument of systematic denial.

The MRI with 4 duplicate pages and the denial letter with no physician's name represents 2026's version of that same structural failure: the financial management apparatus generating the denial, with the physician's role reduced to a checkbox.
